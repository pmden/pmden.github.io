Sainsbury's turned £1.4bn of Argos into £120m. Precedent says the shrinking starts now.

Sainsbury's agreed on 31 July to sell Argos to Swift Partners for at least £120m. A decade ago it paid £1.4bn for the chain's parent.

4 min read

What happened

Sainsbury's has agreed to sell Argos to a company set up by three retail veterans for at least £120m, ten years after paying £1.4bn for its parent Home Retail Group.

(The Guardian, 31 Jul 2026)

On 31 July Sainsbury's announced the sale of Argos to Swift Partners for cash proceeds of at least £120m: £70m on completion, expected in February 2027, and £50m deferred over the following three years (RNS, 31 July 2026). Sainsbury's paid £1.4bn for Argos's parent Home Retail Group in 2016, and will book a non-cash impairment of around £350m on exit (The Guardian, 31 July 2026).

Swift takes the whole business: 201 standalone Argos stores, 466 outlets inside Sainsbury's supermarkets, more than 450 collection points, the Daventry distribution centre, sourcing offices in Shanghai and Hong Kong, and the Argos Care and Argos Pet Insurance operations. Long-term commercial agreements keep Argos concessions, collection points, Nectar and Habitat running inside Sainsbury's (J Sainsbury plc, 31 July 2026).

The buyers are operators, not liquidators. Richard Pennycook, who ran the Co-operative Group through its 2014-17 rescue and was finance chief at Morrisons, becomes executive chair. He is joined by former Morrisons finance and operations chief Trevor Strain and by Matt Truman of retail investor True Capital, which backs the vehicle (The Grocer, 31 July 2026).

Two features frame what follows. This is a solvent sale of a going concern, subject only to customary regulatory conditions, with completion guided for February 2027 and full separation from Sainsbury's systems by 2029. And the price is roughly 9p in the pound against 2016, which puts Argos into a well-populated category: the big British retail brand sold at a fraction of its former value.

The Pattern

Four large UK retail brands have been sold at heavy discounts to their former value since 2018. The estate shrank every time. How much depended on whether the sale was a going concern or a salvage.

2023
Wilko collapsed into administration in August 2023. The Range's owner CDS Superstores bought the brand and website for a reported £5m; all of the roughly 400 stores closed and about 12,500 jobs went.
2021
Debenhams, once valued near £1.7bn on the stock market, was bought out of liquidation by Boohoo for £55m in January 2021. Boohoo took the brand and website only; all 118 department stores closed with around 12,000 jobs lost.
2019
HMV entered its second administration six years after Hilco's £50m rescue. Sunrise Records' Doug Putman bought the chain, keeping 100 of 127 stores. The counter-case: under an operator owner, the shrunken chain stabilised and later reopened a flagship.
2018
Homebase, Argos's sister chain at Home Retail Group, was sold to Wesfarmers for £340m in 2016 and sold on to Hilco for £1 just 27 months later after roughly £1bn of writedowns. A CVA closed 42 stores within months; by late 2024 the business was in administration and the brand passed to CDS Superstores.
4 of 4
All four heavily discounted brand transfers since 2018 produced a smaller store estate within 18 months. The split inside the sample is the real signal: the administration sales (Debenhams, Wilko) kept only the brand, while the going-concern sales (Homebase, HMV) kept trading under new owners, shrank, and changed hands again within six years. Argos enters the second group, with buyers who have turnaround records rather than salvage ones.

4 of 4 discounted brand sales since 2018 led to a smaller store estate within 18 months; both going-concern rescues were sold on again within six years.

The Read

The completion call leans YES. Swift is a newly formed vehicle with no competing retail estate, so the regulatory conditions are described as customary rather than substantive; Sainsbury's has already booked the impairment and wants the exit; and the closest precedents cut the same way, since both Homebase disposals in 2016 and 2018 completed on or ahead of schedule. The 76% rather than higher reflects the date test: February 2027 is company guidance, and disposal timetables slip for mechanical reasons that have nothing to do with deal failure. The closure market leans NO for a structural reason: until completion the stores remain Sainsbury's, and announcing closures early would cut across a transaction priced on a going concern.

The honest counter-case sits in the deal structure. Only £70m arrives at completion; the £50m balance is deferred over three years and depends on Argos continuing to trade, which is exactly where discounted retail deals have historically wobbled. And the six-year resale clock from Homebase and HMV suggests the defining markets on Argos come later: what the 667-outlet estate looks like when separation from Sainsbury's finishes in 2029.
