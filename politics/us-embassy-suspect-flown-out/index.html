Diplomatic immunity14 Sep 2026

US flew accused embassy man home before police acted. 6 of 8 countries pushed back.

American officials took a US embassy worker from his London flat in August and flew him to the United States. British police had not questioned him.

4 min read

What happened

US officials detained a US embassy staff member accused of possessing indecent images of children at his south London flat in August and flew him to the United States on a commercial flight before the Metropolitan Police had questioned him; the embassy says it informed UK law enforcement and reassigned him while its own investigation continues.

(BBC 13 Sep)

A staff member at the US embassy in Vauxhall, accused of possessing indecent images of children, was detained at his south London flat by American officials in August and put on a commercial flight to the United States. British police had not questioned him. He was not a diplomat, but he came under the embassy's umbrella of diplomatic protection. The embassy says it informed UK law enforcement quickly, took swift action and has reassigned him to the United States while an investigation continues. (BBC 13 Sep 2026; Guardian 14 Sep 2026)

The Metropolitan Police says US authorities told it on Tuesday 25 August about an American investigation into suspected offences in London and that the two sides continue to liaise. American personnel have no power of arrest in Britain and no extradition request was made. A UK government spokesperson declined to comment on the US investigation and said Britain expects foreign diplomats to obey its laws. On 14 September the shadow justice secretary Nick Timothy wrote to Alex Norris, the justice secretary, calling the removal a cover-up and asking for the man to be tried in an English court. (Guardian 14 Sep 2026; BBC 13 Sep 2026)

Britain has a written procedure for this. When police report a serious alleged offence by someone with immunity, the Foreign Office asks the sending state to waive it, and if no waiver comes it asks for the person to be withdrawn. The most recent annual count listed nine such cases in 2023, one of them for indecent images of children. The procedure was last tested in June, when the Guardian revealed that a US fighter pilot who strangled a woman in Cambridge had been tried by court martial at RAF Lakenheath; David Lammy told MPs he was raising that case with Washington to establish the facts. (GOV.UK 16 Jul 2015; Guardian 14 Nov 2024; Guardian 30 Jun 2026)

The Pattern

Cases since 2001 in which a foreign state used immunity, its own courts or a flight home to keep one of its people away from the host country's police after a serious alleged offence. Britain first, then the same mechanic abroad. Held means that within 30 days of the case becoming public the host government said in public that it had asked the sending state to waive protection or return the person, or announced its own charge. Newest first.

Six of the last eight cases held: Sacoolas 2019, New Zealand 2017 and 2014, the United States 2014, Pakistan 2011, Japan 2001. The two misses are British: Manchester 2022 and Wulfson 2026. With Italy 1998 and 1984, six of ten.

Six of the last eight host governments asked for the person back, sought a waiver or laid their own charge within 30 days; the two that did not are both British, in 2022 and 2026.

The Read

Washington's move fits its need to keep the case inside its own system. The embassy detained the man itself, flew him out on a commercial flight, told the Met afterwards on 25 August and describes the transfer as a reassignment during an ongoing investigation, the sequence that avoids a second Sacoolas standoff. Britain's need is to look firm without a fight with the White House. The players with a move before 14 October are the Foreign Office and Home Office, whose own procedure is to ask for a waiver and then for withdrawal; the Met, which can open a London investigation; the US Justice Department, which can charge him at home; and Nick Timothy, who can press for an Urgent Question when the Commons sits on 15 September or returns from conference recess on 12 October. (Guardian 14 Sep 2026; BBC 13 Sep 2026; UK Parliament recess dates)

The Hunch line sits well below the six-of-eight record because the record splits by host. Every foreign government in the window, Wellington twice, Washington, Islamabad and Tokyo, pressed within days. Britain's own answers went one way in 2019, when public anger over Harry Dunn took Dominic Raab to Mike Pompeo inside 48 hours, and the other way in 2022 and 2026, when the Foreign Office waited for the police and the justice secretary spoke of establishing the facts. The first week here reads like 2022: a spokesperson line and contact with the embassy. The market opens at 30. (BBC 7 Oct 2019; Guardian 14 Dec 2022; Guardian 30 Jun 2026)

The honest counter-case is that this offence is the kind that moves ministers fastest, the man is not a diplomat, and the Conservative letter has already framed the choice as a cover-up. A Home Office statement that it has requested his return, or a US charge that takes the sting out, would each end the argument. The prediction fails if a minister answers a Commons question with a formal request before recess starts on 16 September. The result itself, a trial in either country, runs on years, so no long market is set. We will return to this on 31 December 2027. (Guardian 14 Sep 2026; BBC 13 Sep 2026; UK Parliament recess dates)
