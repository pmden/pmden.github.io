Your status was granted in error. The Home Office has sent that letter before.

EU citizens are receiving Home Office letters saying their post-Brexit residency status should never have been granted, with 14 days to respond. The department's record with letters like these points one of two ways.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

At least 100 EU citizens have received Home Office letters saying their pre-settled status was granted in error. Freedom of information disclosures to the3million show 95 people were contacted in March alone, and the department has declined to give a full figure.

2 · The event

What happened

On 2 August the Guardian reported that the Home Office is writing to EU citizens to say their pre-settled status under the EU Settlement Scheme was granted in error, giving recipients 14 calendar days to supply evidence of their original eligibility (The Guardian, 2 August 2026). Disclosures obtained by the3million show 95 people were contacted in March alone; campaigners believe the true number runs into the hundreds or more.

One recipient, an NHS worker with Portuguese citizenship through her father, was told she had not shown she was a relevant EEA citizen before the 31 December 2020 cut-off because she held no Portuguese passport when she first applied. The Home Office response, paraphrased, is that recipients keep their status until it expires, can apply through other immigration routes, and gain appeal rights only if a fresh application is refused (The Guardian, 2 August 2026).

The letters sit inside a wider tightening. Since 9 April the department has run an automated process to remove pre-settled status from people it judges to have ceased UK residence (GOV.UK, April 2026). The Independent Monitoring Authority accepts removal can be lawful under the Withdrawal Agreement but has flagged proportionality and consistency concerns (IMA, 9 April 2026), while the3million argues that revoking status granted in good faith breaches the agreement outright (the3million, 2026). One caution: as of 4 August the story itself carries a single national byline; corroboration here rests on the IMA, GOV.UK policy papers and the3million's published casework.

3 · The pattern

The Echo

Three times since 2014 the Home Office has notified people at scale that their immigration status rested on an error. What happened next depended less on the scale than on whether the department kept defending the original decision.

2017
Around 100 EU nationals legally living in the UK received letters saying they were liable for detention and removal. The press exposed the mistake on 23 August; the Home Office conceded the same day that the letters were sent in error and disapplied them, with Downing Street calling it an unfortunate error. Exposure to correction: one day.
2018
Windrush. Individual wrongful-detention cases were reported from November 2017; the scandal reached critical mass in April 2018. The home secretary apologised in the Commons on 16 April 2018, the prime minister apologised to Caribbean leaders the next day, and the home secretary resigned on 29 April. The compensation scheme opened in April 2019. Exposure to apology: about five months. Apology to compensation: a further year.
2014
TOEIC. The Home Office cancelled roughly 35,000 student visas over English-test cheating allegations. The National Audit Office found in 2019 that the evidence carried a real risk of wrongful accusation, yet the department stood by its decisions. No general apology or correction scheme ever came, and individuals were still winning appeals a decade later.
2 of 3
Correction came within a day when the error was undisputed (2017), within months when political pressure broke the department's line (Windrush), and never when the Home Office stood by its evidence (TOEIC). Its current posture, defending the letters as lawful eligibility checks, sits closest to the third mode.

2 of 3 mass status errors were corrected; the Home Office moved fast only once it stopped defending the decision.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case says posture beats scale. The Home Office is defending the letters, the IMA calls removal lawful in principle, and Parliament is in recess, so the levers that forced the 2017 same-day climbdown are not currently engaged. That is why the withdrawal market sits at 22%. The Commons market is closer to a coin toss: MPs return in early September, the3million is organising affected cases, and any letter echoing Windrush phrasing is an easy urgent question for the opposition benches.

What would move the first number: a formal IMA inquiry, a legal challenge under the Withdrawal Agreement, or a single sympathetic case dominating a news cycle, which is precisely how Windrush escalated. The honest counter-case runs the other way. The story has one national outlet on it so far. If wider coverage does not come, salience fades over recess and both markets resolve NO with the letters still standing.
