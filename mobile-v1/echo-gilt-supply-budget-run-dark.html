Gilts31 Aug 2026

£126bn of gilts left to sell. The long end is barely open.

The Debt Management Office has raised £120.0 billion of a £246.2 billion programme for 2026-27, leaving £126.2 billion to place in seven months. Long conventional sales stand at £368 million against a £22.8 billion plan.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Gilt sales in 2026-27 reached £119.95bn by 25 August against total planned sales of £246.2bn, leaving £126.2bn required and 29 auctions remaining; long conventional sales stand at £368m of a £22.77bn plan.

UK Debt Management Office, 25 Aug 2026
2 · The event

A programme two-fifths done in five months

The DMO's remit table to 25 August shows £119.95 billion of gilt sales completed against total planned sales of £246.2 billion for 2026-27, with £126.25 billion still required and 29 of the 51 scheduled auctions still to come (DMO, 25 Aug 2026). The short and medium conventional sectors are carrying the programme: £55.8 billion and £49.2 billion respectively. Index-linked sales stand at £14.6 billion.

The long end is the outlier. Against £22.77 billion of planned long conventional sales, the DMO has raised £368 million, all of it through a July tender rather than an auction, and none of the three planned syndications in the sector has taken place (DMO, 25 Aug 2026). Four long auctions remain in the calendar. Demand at the long end has been the weak point of the gilt market since 2022, and the DMO has shifted issuance toward shorter maturities in response.

The price backdrop has hardened. The Bank of England's monthly average yield on ten-year gilts was 5.01% in July 2026, after 5.03% in May. Before those two months the series had not printed above 5.00% since June 2008 (Bank of England, series IUMAMNZC). The Chancellor delivers his first Budget on 28 October, inside the busiest quarter of the issuance year.

3 · The pattern

What autumn does to the ten-year yield

This is a threshold event, not a decision. No committee sets the ten-year gilt yield; a continuous series either travels a given distance by a given date or it does not. Counting comparable episodes would be the wrong instrument here, so the base rate is drawn from the series itself. Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: the Bank of England's monthly average ten-year nominal spot yield in every August and the following October, for the ten consecutive years 2016 to 2025, with no gaps and no extension of the window. The test is simply whether October printed higher than August.

9 of 10
Nine of the last ten Octobers printed a higher ten-year gilt yield than the August before them. The fraction is strong but the sizes behind it are not uniform: five of those nine rises were under 20 basis points, and the average is dragged upward by 2022 alone. What travels to this year is direction rather than magnitude. What argues against the base rate is the starting level: the series is already at ground it has not held since 2008, and the one exception in the sample, 2025, came from a similarly elevated base.

In 9 of the last 10 years the ten-year gilt yield averaged higher in October than in August. The single exception was 2025.

What this pattern excludes

Every month pair other than August to October
The markets resolve on the Budget month, so the window was set to end in October before the count was run. A rolling two-month window across all months would answer a different question.
Years before 2016
The window was fixed at ten consecutive years in advance. Extending it reaches back into the period when the Bank of England was an active net buyer of gilts under quantitative easing, a mechanism that has since reversed into active sales and no longer holds.
Autumn 2022, a rise of 1.75 percentage points
Considered for removal as a mini-Budget outlier and kept. It falls on the majority side, so removing it would tighten the fraction to 8 of 9 rather than loosen it, and no rule for its removal survives except its effect on the answer.
The 30-year gilt series
Long-dated yields have moved further and would produce a more dramatic fraction. The markets resolve on the ten-year, so the base rate uses the ten-year.

5 · The read

The read

The weighted case is that yields drift up into the Budget. The DMO has to place £126.2 billion in seven months, the long sector is almost entirely unsold and four long auctions plus three syndications sit in front of it, and supply arriving into thin demand is the mechanism behind most of the nine rises in the sample. The three previous years in which the Budget itself fell in October — 2018, 2021 and 2024 — all saw the yield higher in October than in August. A Chancellor briefing the market on greater use of the fiscal rules adds to that pressure rather than relieving it.

The counter-case rests on the level and on what has already been priced. Ten-year yields near 5% are close to the highest monthly averages of the last eighteen years, and the one year in the sample that fell, 2025, also started from an elevated base with a Budget on the horizon. Softer oil and a cooling labour market have pushed the expected date of the next Bank Rate rise into 2027, which pulls the front of the curve down and takes some of the term premium with it. A Budget that lands as a credible consolidation would compress yields, as the November 2025 event did.
