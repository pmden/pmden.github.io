Rough sleeping fell twice. July asks for a third.

London's official CHAIN count recorded 3,944 people sleeping rough between January and March — down 11% on a year earlier, and down 18% on the previous quarter. The next quarterly count, due within weeks, tests whether Sadiq Khan's 2030 pledge has real momentum.

4 min readEcho #116LONDON
1 · Today

CHAIN data showed 3,944 people slept rough in London between January and March, down 11% year on year — the fall Khan promised would begin in 2026.

LabourList, 6 May 2026
2 · The event

What happened

The CHAIN database — London's official multi-agency record of rough sleeping — counted 3,944 people on the streets between January and March, an 11% fall on the same quarter a year earlier and an 18% fall on October–December 2025. First-time rough sleepers dropped 15% year on year to 1,762, and long-term rough sleepers fell 9% to 641 (CHAIN quarterly report, April 2026).

The numbers arrived on schedule for Sadiq Khan, who launched his Plan of Action in May 2025 with a pledge to end rough sleeping in London by 2030 — backed by £17m in government funding, up to 500 refurbished empty homes, an Ending Homelessness Hub and a dedicated prevention phone line — and who said improvement would not show until 2026 (London City Hall, May 2025).

The wider backdrop remains severe: London Councils estimates 210,000 Londoners are homeless and living in temporary accommodation, with boroughs collectively spending £5m a day on it. The next CHAIN quarterly report, covering April to June, is expected from the Greater London Authority in late July — the first test of whether the fall survives warmer months, when street counts typically behave differently.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Political pledges to end rough sleeping have a long record, and it is mostly a record of failure. One pledge in a generation has landed. The current fall must outrun that history.

2020
"Everyone In" cut London's street population sharply during the pandemic — proof numbers can fall fast when money and rooms appear — but the effect faded, and CHAIN's annual count hit a record 11,993 by 2023/24.
2018
The May government pledged to end rough sleeping in England by 2027. National counts fell to 2021, then rose for three consecutive years. The pledge is badly off track.
2008
Boris Johnson pledged to end rough sleeping in London by 2012. CHAIN's count was substantially higher in 2012 than when he made the promise.
1998
The Blair government's target to cut rough sleeping by two-thirds by 2002 was hit a year early, in 2001 — the one pledge on this list that landed, built on prevention and a dedicated central unit.
1 of 4
One of the four major rough sleeping pledges since 1998 hit its target. But the short-term record points the other way: both CHAIN quarters reported in 2026 have shown falls. The long-term base rate and the recent trend are genuinely in tension — that tension is the market.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case for a third consecutive fall is momentum plus money. Two quarters of decline, a 15% drop in first-timers — the pipeline into street homelessness narrowing, which is the metric prevention programmes move first — and a funded plan still in its delivery phase all point the same way. The Blair-era precedent shows that when a dedicated unit, central funding and political attention align, falls can compound rather than fade. A 68% price treats this quarter as the plan's to lose.

The honest counter-case is that spring quarters flatter. Warmer months change how and where people sleep out and how outreach finds them, so a strong Jan–Mar figure can precede a flat or rising Apr–Jun one, and the comparison base — spring 2025, before the plan's effects — is not especially high. The structural pressure has not eased either: 210,000 Londoners in temporary accommodation is a reservoir that feeds the street count, housing benefit rates are frozen for a second year, and every pledge since 2001 has eventually been overwhelmed by exactly this arithmetic. One good winter does not repeal it.
