The veto is gone. Now Kyiv races the machine.

Ukraine opened its first EU accession negotiating cluster on 15 June, four years after gaining candidate status — unblocked by Viktor Orbán's election defeat in April. The Commission is now drafting enlargement reforms to keep control of a process member states have been running without it.

4 min readEcho #116INTERNATIONAL
1 · Today

The Commission is drawing up enlargement reform plans — including safeguards to keep new members in line after accession — as Ukraine's negotiating track gathers pace.

Politico Europe, 26 Jun 2026
2 · The event

What happened

The EU formally opened the first accession negotiating cluster with Ukraine on 15 June, after member states agreed the step on 12 June. The 'fundamentals' cluster covers rule of law, democratic institutions, public administration reform and economic criteria — the first cluster opened in any accession and, by design, the last one closed. Moldova opened the same cluster the same day (Council of the EU, 15 Jun 2026).

The politics behind the date matter as much as the date. Ukraine's cluster openings had been blocked for two years by Viktor Orbán's Hungary — a veto that dissolved when his government lost the April 2026 elections. Four years from candidate status to first cluster makes Ukraine the fastest mover in recent enlargement history (Politico, 15 June 2026).

Brussels is already fighting over what comes next. The Commission is preparing enlargement reform proposals — including safeguards written into future accession treaties to prevent democratic backsliding after entry — aiming to regain the initiative before EU leaders' strategic enlargement debate in October (Politico, 26 June 2026).

The safeguard design is explicit: enlargement chief Marta Kos says the EU is developing rules that would 'bite hard' if new members go rogue after joining (Politico, 15 June 2026).

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Opening talks is a ceremony; sustaining them is the test. The last five accession processes split cleanly into two kinds: the ones that kept opening chapters, and the ones that opened a door onto a frozen corridor.

2024
Albania opened its first cluster in October and its second in December — two months apart, the fastest sequence in modern enlargement.
2022
North Macedonia finally opened negotiations after a 17-year wait as a candidate — and has opened zero clusters since, held by Bulgaria's conditions.
2021
Serbia opened its last new chapter in December 2021: 22 of 35 opened, then more than four years of nothing.
2020
Montenegro became the first candidate to open all 33 of its chapters, eight years after talks began — and is now the frontrunner to join.
2016
Turkey opened Chapter 33 in June 2016 — its 16th of 35 — and the process has been formally frozen ever since.
2 of 5
2 of 5 recent accession processes sustained momentum after their opening milestone; 3 stalled for years. This is a split pattern, and the split is the point: cluster openings measure member-state politics, not candidate readiness — Ukraine's file was ready throughout the two years Hungary held it shut.

Of 5 recent accession processes, 2 kept momentum after opening; 3 froze for years. The pattern is genuinely split.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case: the machinery is slow by construction, and August is when Brussels stops. Screening for the remaining clusters takes months, intergovernmental conferences need unanimity and scheduling, and the Commission's own reform proposals are being drafted with October's EUCO in mind, not the summer. The 55% on 'one cluster' and 22% on pre-August safeguard proposals reflect a system that just delivered its milestone and has no institutional reason to deliver another before autumn.

The honest counter-case: the single variable that froze this process — the Hungarian veto — is gone, and both Kyiv and the Commission have political reasons to bank a second opening while the window is wide. Albania showed a two-month cluster cadence is possible when member states want it, and Ukraine's screening is further along than its four-year wait suggests. A summer intergovernmental conference would be unusual. So was opening a cluster with a country at war.
