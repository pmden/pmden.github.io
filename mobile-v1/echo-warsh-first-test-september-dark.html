Every new Fed chair gets tested. Warsh's test is live.

Kevin Warsh heads to his first Jackson Hole symposium as Federal Reserve chair with three colleagues pushing for rate rises, inflation at 3.4% and long-term yields at two-decade highs. Since 1979, every new chair who met an early market test answered it the same way: tighten or stand firm. None eased.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Minutes of the Fed's July meeting, released on 19 August, revealed internal disagreement: the committee held rates at 3.5–3.75% on a 9–3 vote, with three members — Hammack, Kashkari and Logan — formally dissenting in favour of an immediate quarter-point rise, while the minutes noted that tightening would likely be necessary if it persists.

Federal Reserve / The Guardian, 19 Aug 2026
2 · The event

What happened

Markets have probed every incoming Federal Reserve chair since Paul Volcker, and this week they started on Kevin Warsh. Minutes of the July meeting, released on 19 August, showed the committee holding rates at 3.5–3.75% while three members argued for increases if inflation, at 3.4% in July after 4.2% in May, fails to head towards the 2% target (Federal Reserve, 19 Aug 2026). The minutes landed in the middle of a bond selloff that has pushed 10- and 20-year Treasury yields to 20-year highs and the 30-year to its highest since 2007 (The Guardian, 19 Aug 2026).

Warsh, chair since May, has made silence a policy. He has scrapped the dot plot and forward guidance, assembled a 15-strong panel including former Bank of England governor Mervyn King to review how the Fed communicates, and declined to indicate a direction for rates; former BoE deputy governor Charlie Bean has observed that he is offering neither a rate path nor a reaction function (The Guardian, 16 Aug 2026). As global yields climbed this week, that reticence itself drew criticism, with the chair faulted for withholding guidance during the rout (The Guardian, 20 Aug 2026).

The stage for his answer is set. The Kansas City Fed's Jackson Hole symposium runs from 27 to 29 August under the theme of financial innovation and its implications for payments and policy, and it will be Warsh's first appearance there as chair (Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City, 2026). Markets currently expect a hold at the 15–16 September meeting, with any move to a 4–4.25% range priced for 2027 (The Guardian, 16 Aug 2026).

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Since 1979, four incoming Fed chairs have met a market test in their first months in office. Across five decades, one constant holds: the new chair proves resolve first and softens later, if at all.

1979
Paul Volcker, two months in: with inflation near 13% and bonds sliding, his 6 October package raised the discount rate to a then-record 12% and switched the Fed's operating regime. He tightened straight into the test.
1987
Alan Greenspan, 72 days in: Wall Street fell 22.6% in a single session. He had already raised the discount rate in September, the first rise in three years, and answered the crash with a one-sentence liquidity pledge rather than a policy retreat.
2006
Ben Bernanke, four months in: accused of sending mixed signals as inflation climbed, he delivered a 17th consecutive rate rise to 5.25% in June 2006, then paused on his own terms once markets stopped doubting him.
2018
Jerome Powell, sworn in on 5 February 2018 as the Dow fell 1,175 points, then its biggest one-day points drop. He raised rates four times that year regardless, relenting only after December's slump.
4 of 4
Four Federal Reserve chairs since 1979 met a market test in their first months: Volcker's bond rout, Greenspan's crash, Bernanke's inflation scare, Powell's volatility spike. All four answered by tightening or standing firm at the next decision; none eased. The constant favours a hawkish hold in September, with the three hawks in the July minutes marking where the pressure sits.

4 of 4 new chairs facing an early market test tightened or stood firm at the next scheduled decision. None eased into the pressure.

5 · The read

How to read it

The case for the pricing above runs through the pattern's fine print. Standing firm, not hiking, was enough for Greenspan in 1987 and Powell in 2018; only Volcker and Bernanke needed actual tightening to settle the question, and both faced inflation far worse relative to target than today's 3.4%. Warsh has spent three months refusing to pre-commit, and a chair who has abolished forward guidance is unlikely to validate market pressure with a surprise hike at his first contested meeting. Hence 30% for September but a coin-flip-plus for a rise at some point in 2026: the minutes' language that tightening would likely be necessary if inflation persists reads as a loaded spring, and three sitting dissenters make a repeat dissent the likeliest single outcome of the September vote.

The honest counter-case: the sample is four, and outside this stretch dissent-free unanimity has been the Fed's default in most meetings of the past decade. The September-rise market fails high if oil above $91 pushes the August inflation print, due days before the meeting, back towards 4% — that is the scenario in which Warsh's test becomes Volcker-shaped rather than Greenspan-shaped. The 2026-rise market fails low if the bond rout itself does the tightening for him, slowing the economy enough that the committee judges the work already done.
