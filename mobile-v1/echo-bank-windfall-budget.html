Banks04 Aug 2026

The banks made £29bn in six months. The Chancellor writes his first Budget in twelve weeks.

HSBC posted £7.5bn of quarterly profit on 4 August as campaigners demanded a windfall tax on banks to fund cost-of-living help. John Healey delivers his first Budget on 28 October.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Campaigners renew calls for a windfall tax on UK banks after HSBC reports $10.1bn (£7.5bn) of second-quarter profit, up 60% year on year, arguing the proceeds could fund the government's cost-of-living plans.

The Guardian, 4 Aug 2026
2 · The event

What happened

On 4 August HSBC reported second-quarter pre-tax profit of $10.1bn (£7.5bn), up 60% year on year, taking first-half profit to $19.5bn, and announced a $1bn share buyback (HSBC Interim Results, 4 August 2026; Yahoo Finance, 4 August 2026). The result was driven by wealth-management fees and elevated interest income.

The big four UK banks — HSBC, NatWest, Barclays and Lloyds — have now made £29.2bn in six months and pledged £13.7bn to shareholders in dividends and buybacks. Positive Money, the TUC and ActionAid UK argue that a windfall levy at 38%, the rate applied to oil and gas, could raise around £19bn this year (The Guardian, 4 August 2026). Campaigners have costed the wishlist: an £850m VAT cut on electricity, a £500m bus-fare cap, £100m of business-rates relief for hospitality.

The decision has a date. Chancellor John Healey confirmed on 31 July that his first Budget falls on 28 October, framed around moving money and power out of Westminster (Bloomberg, 31 July 2026). Banks currently pay a 3% surcharge on profits above £100m, on top of 25% corporation tax, plus the balance-sheet bank levy — so the live question is whether Healey adds a new levy or moves the surcharge back up.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Britain has faced this exact configuration before: a sector booking outsized profits, a squeezed public, a chancellor needing revenue. Five episodes since 1981 give the base rate — and they split.

1981
Geoffrey Howe imposed a one-off 2.5% tax on banks' non-interest-bearing deposits in his 10 March Budget, raising about £400m from lenders that had profited from 17% base rates (Hansard, 12 May 1981). A Conservative chancellor, over the banks' objections.
1997
Gordon Brown's first Budget, on 2 July, levied a one-off £5.2bn windfall tax on the privatised utilities to fund welfare-to-work (Commons Library, SN00338). The closest analogue: a new government's first Budget, taxing an unpopular sector to fund a signature programme.
2022
Rishi Sunak announced the Energy Profits Levy on 26 May at 25%, forecast to raise about £5bn in its first year; successors raised it to 35%, then 38%. Introduced mid-crisis after months of resisting the idea.
2024
Rachel Reeves's first Budget left bank taxes untouched despite union and campaign pressure, after concerted City lobbying on competitiveness (TUC, 2025).
2025
The November Budget again declined to raise the 3% surcharge — itself cut from 8% in April 2023 — even as banks posted record profits and executives braced for a rise (Bloomberg, 31 October 2025).
3 of 5
Three of five windfall-pressure episodes since 1981 produced a levy. The split has a structure: the levies came when a new government's first Budget or an acute crisis supplied political cover; the refusals came from chancellors mid-term, guarding the City. October is a new government's first Budget, with a cost-of-living programme waiting for a funding line.

3 of 5 windfall-pressure episodes since 1981 ended in a new levy — but both refusals were bank-specific, and both came at mid-term Budgets defending City competitiveness.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case leans toward a measure. The 1997 template fits almost exactly: first Budget of a new government, a flagship social programme needing a funding line, and a target sector reporting record profits while cutting cheques to shareholders. Bank profits are the most visible revenue pool available to Healey without breaking manifesto lines on income tax and VAT, and the surcharge sits at 3% against 8% as recently as 2023 — a rise can be framed as restoration rather than raid. That is why the 60% read sits above the raw 3-of-5 base rate, and why a surcharge rise is priced well ahead of a standalone levy.

The honest counter-case: the two most recent data points are both refusals, and they are the bank-specific ones. City lobbying beat this exact campaign in 2024 and 2025, and HSBC weakens the headline maths — most of its £19.5bn is earned in Asia, beyond any UK levy's reach, so the true domestic base is nearer the big four's UK books than the campaign's £19bn suggests. The prediction fails if the Treasury rules a levy out on the record before October, if Burnham funds the cost-of-living package another way, or if growth data improves enough to shrink the gap the levy would fill.
