ECB04 Sep 2026

The ECB has raised rates into an energy shock four times. Three were reversed within a year.

Euro-area inflation jumped to 3.3% in August on a 14.3% rise in energy prices. The ECB meets on 10 September with a quarter-point rise expected.

4 min read

What happened

Euro-area annual inflation is expected to be 3.3% in August 2026, up from 2.9% in July, with energy at 14.3% against 10.3% a month earlier, according to Eurostat's flash estimate.

(Eurostat, 1 Sep 2026)

Eurostat's flash estimate on 1 September put euro-area inflation at 3.3% for August, up from 2.9% in July. Energy is the driver: its annual rate rose to 14.3% from 10.3%, while services eased to 3.0% from 3.3%, non-energy goods stood at 1.2% and food at 1.2% (Eurostat, 1 Sep 2026). Strip out energy and the picture is close to target; include it and the headline is the highest since 2023.

The Governing Council meets on 10 September with the deposit rate at 2.25% and a 25-basis-point increase to 2.50% priced by money markets. The ECB has not moved rates since the spring, and the energy shock traces to the war with Iran that began in February and the renewed strikes on Gulf shipping since.

The question is not whether the Bank hikes on Thursday but how long the hike lasts. The ECB has tightened into energy-led inflation four times in its history, and its own record of key rate decisions shows that in three of the four the next move was a cut inside seven months (ECB key interest rates).

The Pattern

The ECB has raised rates with energy prices leading inflation four times since 1999. The dates come from the Bank's own record of key interest rate decisions, and the throughline is how quickly each tightening was undone.

2022
July 2022: with energy prices surging after the invasion of Ukraine, the ECB raised rates by 50 basis points, its first increase in eleven years (Al Jazeera, 21 Jul 2022). The first cut did not come until 6 June 2024 (ECB, 6 Jun 2024).
2011
July 2011: the Governing Council raised rates by 25 basis points to 1.5%, its second increase that year (ECB, 7 Jul 2011). On 3 November a new president, Mario Draghi, cut them back to 1.25% (Al Jazeera, 3 Nov 2011).
2008
July 2008: with oil near its peak the ECB raised its main rate to 4.25% on 3 July. On 8 October it cut in a coordinated move with other central banks as the financial crisis broke (ECB, 8 Oct 2008).
2000
October 2000: the ECB raised its main refinancing rate to 4.75% on 5 October, the last step of a cycle driven partly by oil (ECB, 5 Oct 2000). The first cut came on 10 May 2001 (ECB, 10 May 2001).
3 of 4
In 3 of the 4 episodes in which the ECB raised rates with energy prices leading inflation, the next move was a cut within seven months: 2000 (seven months), 2008 (three months) and 2011 (four months). The exception is 2022, when the energy shock coincided with broad-based inflation and the cycle ran for two years. Which of the two the current episode resembles depends on whether services inflation, now 3.0% and falling, follows energy upward.

Three of four energy-led tightenings were reversed within seven months.

The Read

The weighted case for Thursday is a single quarter-point step: the flash estimate was above the Bank's projection, energy is running at double digits, and markets have priced the move. That is why the first market sits above 80% and the larger-step market near zero; the ECB has never opened with more than 50 points and has not needed to signal urgency.

The honest counter-case is about the second market. Three of the four precedents say a hike into an energy shock is the top of the cycle, not the start, because the shock does the tightening itself. Services inflation is already falling. The prediction of a further hike in October fails if the Council reads August as the peak, and it is the history of 2000, 2008 and 2011 that argues it will.
