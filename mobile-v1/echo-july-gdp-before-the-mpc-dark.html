Growth03 Sep 2026

June's economy grew 0.3 per cent. July's number lands six days before the Bank votes.

Monthly GDP for July is published on 11 September, ahead of the labour market on the 15th, inflation on the 16th and the Monetary Policy Committee on the 17th.

4 min read

One number, and the week it opens

Monthly real GDP is estimated to have grown by 0.3% in June 2026, following no growth in May, with growth of 0.4% in the three months to June compared with the three months to March.

(ONS, 13 Aug 2026)

The Office for National Statistics reported real GDP growth of 0.3 per cent in June 2026, following no growth in May, and 0.4 per cent across the three months to June against the three months to March, with services the main contributor while production was flat and construction rose modestly. The next release is 11 September 2026 (ONS, 13 Aug 2026).

The underlying index shows how small these moves are. Monthly gross value added, measured against a 2022 base, ran 102.3 in January, 102.8 in February, 103.2 in March, 103.1 in April, 103.1 in May and 103.4 in June (ONS, series ECY2). Six months of data, and the whole range from bottom to top is about one per cent.

The date matters more than usual because of what follows it. The labour market figures land on 15 September, consumer price inflation on 16 September and the Monetary Policy Committee announces on 17 September, having held Bank Rate at its previous meeting with three members voting for an increase. A weak July print and a firm inflation print in the same week put the Committee's two arguments directly against each other.

The Pattern

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: this pattern takes the published ONS monthly gross value added index and computes the June to July change for every year from 2011 to 2025. The test is direction: did the index rise. Two years are excluded and named in advance, 2020 and 2021, because the July 2020 move of 6.8 per cent and the July 2021 move are reopening effects from a national shutdown rather than observations of an ordinary economy. That leaves thirteen years, and none of the thirteen is dropped for any other reason.

2012
July 2012 rose 1.3 per cent, the largest ordinary July move in the set and roughly four times the next largest. Even excluded years aside, the tail on this series is long.
2018
July 2018 fell 0.2 per cent, one of only three falls in the thirteen, and one of two falls smaller than half a point.
2022
July 2022 rose 0.5 per cent, the largest rise after 2012, in a month when the annual inflation rate was above 10 per cent. Growth and inflation moving together is the combination the Committee reads as demand rather than cost.
2025
July 2025 fell 0.1 per cent, and August 2025 fell another 0.2, the only consecutive falls in the recent series. The most recent July in the set is one of the three that went the wrong way.
9 of 13
9 of 13. Hold it loosely for three reasons. Thirteen observations of a noisy monthly series is a small base; the series is revised long after publication, so the history is not the history contemporaries saw; and July is a month whose seasonal adjustment carries school holidays, factory shutdowns and, in some years, sporting events. What the count is good for is bounding expectations, not forecasting the decimal.

9 of the 13 comparable Julys showed growth, 1 was flat and 3 were falls. Of the nine rises, five were 0.2 per cent or more.

What this pattern excludes

July 2020 and July 2021
July 2020 rose 6.8 per cent as the economy reopened, a move twenty times larger than a normal July. Including it would put a number in the base that no policy or forecast could ever reproduce, and excluding it is stated here rather than done quietly.
Quarterly GDP
It is the measure that enters the record books, but it is published later and on a different basis. The market resolves on the monthly estimate because that is what is released on 11 September.
Revisions
The monthly series is revised for years afterwards, so a base rate built from today's revised numbers is not the base rate that was visible at the time. This is a real weakness of the count and it is stated rather than hidden.

The Read

The positive band carries most of the weight, and it carries it from the record rather than from any view about demand. Nine of thirteen comparable Julys grew, and the recent months have alternated between small rises and flat prints rather than falling. What tempers it is that the last July in the series, 2025, was one of the three falls, and that it was followed by another fall in August, the only consecutive decline in the recent run.

The revision market is the one worth thinking about separately. Monthly GDP is an early estimate built on incomplete returns, and the ONS revises it routinely as more data arrives, which is why a market on whether June stays at 0.3 per cent is close to a coin flip rather than a formality. Read the three together and the useful question is not whether the economy grew in July. It is whether the Committee, six days later, is looking at a growth picture that has quietly been revised down while inflation is being revised up. That combination is what has moved votes on this Committee before.
