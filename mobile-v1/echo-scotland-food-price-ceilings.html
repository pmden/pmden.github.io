Scotland03 Sep 2026

Scotland writes legal ceilings on food prices. One measure in five has cleared the wall.

The Scottish Government has opened a consultation on legal price ceilings for essential food items and named a Food Prices (Scotland) Bill in its first-year legislative programme. Its own officials have already assessed the proposal against the United Kingdom Internal Market Act 2020.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The Scottish Government opens a consultation on legal price controls for essential food items, with a Food Prices (Scotland) Bill named in the 2026-27 legislative programme.

Scottish Government, 1 Sep 2026
2 · The event

A price ceiling, a nine-Bill programme, and a 2020 Act

Business Minister Tom Arthur opened a consultation on 1 September asking for views on a price control mechanism for essential food, which retailers would be covered, which items would be covered, and how it would work in practice (Scottish Government, 1 Sep 2026). The consultation closes on 24 November 2026. It delivers a commitment made in the administration's first hundred days.

The mechanism has a legislative vehicle. The Programme for Government published the same day names nine Bills for 2026-27, and the Food Prices (Scotland) Bill is one of them, described as establishing legal price ceilings on some essential food items (Scottish Government, 1 Sep 2026). The other eight are the Budget Bill, a buildings heating and heat networks Bill, a Council Tax Bill introducing new bands for high-value properties by April 2028, a Damages Bill, a Football Banning Orders Bill, a Legal Aid Bill, a Misogyny Bill and a Public Service Renewal Bill.

The constraint is not political. The United Kingdom Internal Market Act 2020 requires goods lawfully sold in one part of the United Kingdom to be sellable in the others, which is why a Scottish rule about what a retailer may charge is a question about reserved trade law as much as about devolved consumer policy. The Scottish Government says its officials have assessed the potential conflict and plan to engage with the UK Government after the consultation closes. That sequencing matters: the assessment comes first, the negotiation comes second, and the Bill comes third.

3 · The pattern

When a Scottish measure meets a UK-level constraint

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: this pattern counts every measure passed or proposed by the Scottish Parliament since 2012 whose operation depended on clearing a constraint that only a United Kingdom institution could lift, where the outcome is on the public record. Three routes qualify and no others: a reference to the UK Supreme Court on legislative competence, an order under section 35 of the Scotland Act 1998, and a decision under the United Kingdom Internal Market Act 2020. The test is whether the measure reached force in the form the Parliament passed. Ordinary policy failures are excluded, because nothing outside Scotland stopped them. Measures still awaiting an outcome are excluded, including the one that prompted this Echo.

2017
Minimum unit pricing of alcohol, legislated in 2012, was challenged to the UK Supreme Court, which dismissed the challenge unanimously on 15 November 2017. Pricing took effect at 50 pence per unit on 1 May 2018. Cleared, five years after Royal Assent.
2018
The EU Legal Continuity Bill, passed on 21 March 2018, was referred to the Court, which held on 13 December 2018 that section 17 was outside competence and that other provisions were not law because the EU (Withdrawal) Act 2018 had passed while the case ran. The Bill was never submitted for Royal Assent and was formally withdrawn on 10 March 2022.
2021
The Bill incorporating the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child was held on 6 October 2021 to contain four sections outside competence. A narrowed version was passed on 7 December 2023 and received Royal Assent on 16 January 2024.
2023
The Gender Recognition Reform (Scotland) Bill was stopped at 12.25pm on 17 January 2023 by the first order ever made under section 35 of the Scotland Act 1998. The Court of Session dismissed the Scottish Ministers' judicial review on 8 December 2023.
2023
The Deposit Return Scheme was granted a temporary exclusion from the Internal Market Act on 27 May 2023, but for cans and PET only: on glass the UK Government said there was insufficient justification. Launch moved from 2023 to October 2025 and then to October 2027, with glass removed.
1 of 5
1 of 5 cleared the constraint intact. State the limitations plainly: five cases is a thin base, three of the five decisions came from the same court, and the section 35 route had never been used before 2023. The record describes a mechanism that is still young rather than a settled frequency.

1 of the 5 reached force in the form the Parliament passed. The one that did took five years and a Supreme Court ruling to get there.

What this pattern excludes

The Scottish Independence Referendum Bill reference, judgment 23 November 2022
It was a constitutional question rather than a devolved policy measure, and the route it needed was an Order in Council rather than a court's blessing. It is counted separately against the record of section 30 orders.
Scottish policies dropped for cost or political reasons
A measure abandoned in Edinburgh tells you nothing about a UK-level constraint. The pattern is about a specific mechanism, not about whether Scottish policy succeeds in general.
Measures currently in progress
Anything without a recorded outcome is left out rather than guessed at, including the food price proposal that prompted this Echo.

5 · The read

The read

The near-term markets are about speed, and the record says Holyrood is not fast in September. A new parliamentary year opened days ago, the Budget Bill has its own timetable, and consultations that close in late November do not produce Bills in the same autumn. That is why both fortnight markets sit low. The longer market is different. The Bill is named, the vehicle exists, and the Scottish Government has already done the internal legal assessment, which is the step that usually precedes introduction rather than the step that kills it.

The counter-case is the pattern itself. Four of the five recorded measures met a wall that Edinburgh could not move, and the one that got through needed a court to say so after five years. Food pricing sits directly on top of reserved trade law, which is a harder place to stand than gender recognition or children's rights, and the closest precedent is the deposit return scheme: an exclusion from the 2020 Act was granted, and the scheme still lost glass and four years. If the UK Government signals early that it will not grant an exclusion, the consultation becomes an argument about the constitution rather than a step towards a statute, and the Bill can be introduced and still never take effect. That is the outcome that would make the long market look badly priced.
