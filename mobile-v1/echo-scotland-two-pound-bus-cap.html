Scotland03 Sep 2026

The £2 bus cap reaches Glasgow on Monday. Scotland has never missed one of these start dates.

The Programme for Government extends the £2 bus fare cap from the Highlands and Islands to the Strathclyde Partnership for Transport area from Monday 7 September, with a national rollout promised by the end of this Parliament. The first pilot has not finished yet.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The Programme for Government commits to expanding the £2 bus fare cap beyond the Highlands and Islands to the Strathclyde Partnership for Transport area from Monday 7 September, followed by a national rollout by the end of this Parliament.

Scottish Government, 1 Sep 2026
2 · The event

One date, one region, one unfinished pilot

The commitment is written with a day attached. The Programme for Government promises expanding the £2 bus fare cap beyond the Highlands and Islands to the Strathclyde Partnership for Transport area from Monday 7 September, followed by a national roll-out by the end of this Parliament (Scottish Government, 1 Sep 2026). Strathclyde is the largest regional transport partnership in Scotland and takes the cap from the thinnest bus markets in the country to the densest.

The existing scheme it extends is a pilot that has not run its course. A twelve-month £2 cap backed by £10 million was delivered across the HiTrans and ZetTrans areas in four phases: Shetland on 30 January 2026, the Western Isles on 23 February, Orkney on 9 March and Highland, Moray and Argyll and Bute on 23 March, with Helensburgh and Lomond excluded (Transport Scotland, 2026). There is no application and no residency test; the cap applies to single adult and child fares on participating routes, and premium, tour and night services are outside it. The pilot is intended to end between January and March 2027, subject to funding.

So the September date is an extension of something still being evaluated, which matters for how it can fail. A cap with no application process and no eligibility check needs operator agreements and ticketing changes rather than a benefits system, and those either exist by the Monday or they do not. That makes the start observable on the day.

3 · The pattern

Scottish fare interventions and the dates attached to them

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: this pattern counts every public transport fare intervention announced by the Scottish Government since 2021 that carried a named public start date, where the start has now passed. The test is stated in advance and is only about the start: did the intervention begin on the date named. Whether it later ended, shrank or grew is recorded separately below, because that is a different question with a different mechanism behind it. Fare changes made by operators rather than by ministers are excluded.

2022
Free bus travel for under-22s went live on 31 January 2022, with applications opening on 10 January. It has since carried over 300 million free journeys, with more than 80 per cent of eligible young people holding a card. Started on time and still running.
2023
Removal of ScotRail peak fares began on 2 October 2023 as a six-month pilot. It started on the day named, and then its end date moved twice, from 29 March 2024 to 28 June 2024 and then to 27 September 2024, when it was withdrawn at a full-year subsidy cost of about £40 million.
2025
Peak rail fares were removed permanently from 1 September 2025, announced in the previous Programme for Government. An Edinburgh to Glasgow peak return fell from £32.60 to £16.80. Started on time, eleven months after the pilot it replaced had been scrapped.
2026
The £2 bus fare cap pilot began in four phases, on 30 January, 23 February, 9 March and 23 March 2026. Every phase landed on its announced date, but the scheme arrived as four starts rather than one.
4 of 4
4 of 4 on the start date. Two limits belong beside it. Four is the minimum that counts as a base, and a perfect record on a small sample carries a wide band of uncertainty rather than near-certainty. And the test is deliberately narrow: it measures whether a scheme starts, not whether it survives, and on that second question the same four cases split two and two.

4 of the 4 interventions began on the date announced. Two of the four had their end date or their coverage moved after they had started.

What this pattern excludes

ScotRail entering public ownership on 1 April 2022
It transferred who runs the trains rather than what a passenger pays. Including it would count an ownership change as a fare intervention and inflate the record with a case that could not have failed in the same way.
The national concessionary scheme for older and disabled people
It has run for two decades and predates the window fixed above. Adding a scheme with no live start date would add an automatic success to the count without adding evidence.
Fare changes announced by bus operators
The mechanism is a commercial decision, not a ministerial one. The pattern is about whether a government delivers on a date it has published.

5 · The read

The read

The Monday market is priced high because the failure mode is narrow. A cap with no application and no eligibility test needs operator participation and ticketing, and the Highlands and Islands rollout showed the government prefers to phase a start rather than miss one. The residual risk is not that nothing happens on 7 September but that the SPT area arrives in pieces, which is why the resolution rule above treats partial operator coverage as a start.

The two later markets pull in opposite directions and that is the interesting part. Naming a national date in the next fortnight would mean committing money before the first pilot has been evaluated, and the government has just spent a year saying the pilot is the evidence base, so the September market sits low. The extension market sits high for the same reason in reverse: a government that has just widened a scheme to its largest region will not let the original pilot lapse in the middle of that widening. The precedent that argues against is the peak fares pilot, which was extended twice and then dropped anyway when the subsidy reached about £40 million a year. Cost, not evidence, is what has ended a Scottish fare scheme before.
