Four early Labour revolts since 1997. Four government wins. This one has no vote.

Andy Burnham's apparent backing for the Rosebank and Jackdaw fields has triggered the first backbench revolt of his premiership. The record of early revolts against first-term Labour governments points one way in the short term — and the other way after.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Burnham faces the first backbench revolt of his premiership over signals that ministers will consent to the Rosebank and Jackdaw developments, with opposition spanning ministers, senior backbenchers and the 2024 intake, and consultations closing in mid-August.

The Guardian, 31 Jul 2026
2 · The event

What happened

In his first fortnight in office, Andy Burnham has signalled a pragmatic approach to the pending Rosebank and Jackdaw decisions, framing the question as where Britain's oil and gas comes from rather than whether it is used (The Guardian, 31 Jul 2026). Rachael Maskell urged ministers to keep the carbon in the ground, Clive Lewis said there was no legitimate case for consenting Jackdaw, and Edinburgh East's Chris Murray invoked the UK's net zero leadership duty. One party figure warned there would be a revolt if new licensing ever came to a Commons vote; some critics would accept the two fields as the price of a halt to anything further (The Guardian, 31 Jul 2026).

The two fields arrive on Burnham's desk through the courts, not the manifesto. Jackdaw was approved in 2022 and Rosebank in 2023, but the Supreme Court's June 2024 Finch ruling required environmental assessments to count the emissions from burning the fuel, and in January 2025 the Court of Session quashed both consents as unlawful while letting work continue pending fresh decisions (Institute for Government, 2026). The previous government had already conceded in court that Rosebank's approval was unlawful (BBC News, 2024). Both operators have since filed revised assessments covering those downstream emissions, with the regulator requesting further information from Rosebank's operator as recently as March 2026 (Institute for Government, 2026).

The timetable is now short. Consultations conclude in mid-August, and energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh takes the final decision (The Guardian, 31 Jul 2026). The Times reports that Burnham's team wants the decisions fast-tracked, with consent possible within weeks of the consultation closing and Jackdaw production able to start by 1 October (The Times, via Energy Pedia, Jul 2026). Crucially, re-consenting fields that already hold licences is an administrative act. No Commons division is required, which shapes everything about how this revolt can actually be fought (The Guardian, 31 Jul 2026).

3 · The pattern

The pattern

First-term Labour governments with large majorities have faced four major backbench revolts on points of principle since 1997. The short-term record is uniform. The long-term record is the interesting part.

1997
Blair, seven months in: 47 Labour MPs voted against the lone-parent benefit cut on 10 December, roughly 100 abstained, and a minister resigned. The cut passed anyway on a 179-seat majority.
2008
Brown's first spring: abolishing the 10p tax rate triggered a revolt organised around Frank Field's amendment. The government never risked the division — it conceded a £2.7bn personal allowance rise that fully compensated 80% of the 5.3 million losing households.
2024
Starmer, three weeks in: seven Labour MPs backed an SNP amendment to scrap the two-child benefit cap and lost the whip. The government won the vote comfortably — then scrapped the cap itself at the November 2025 Budget.
2025
Starmer's welfare bill: the parliament's biggest revolt forced ministers to strip the bill's disability-benefit core mid-debate. 49 Labour MPs still voted against, and four were later suspended.
4 of 4
In all four early revolts against first-term Labour governments since 1997, the government carried its policy through the initial confrontation. But in three of the four, the substance was later softened or reversed: the 10p losers were compensated, the welfare bill was gutted, the two-child cap was scrapped. The catch for Burnham's rebels is that every one of those delayed wins began with a Commons division. A consent signed by the energy secretary offers no division to lose.

4 of 4 early revolts failed to stop the policy on the day — and in 3 of 4, the government later conceded the substance.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case favours a prompt Jackdaw consent. The consultation closes in mid-August, The Times reports the machinery is being prepared to move within weeks, the energy secretary has shifted from opposing Rosebank to talking about pragmatism, and the operator's winter-supply argument gives ministers a natural 1 October deadline. The base rate does the rest: four out of four early revolts failed to stop the policy on the day, and this revolt lacks the one lever the others had — a vote. That is why Jackdaw sits at 72% and the resignation market, the 1997-style pressure valve, is priced low but live.

The honest counter-case is legal, not parliamentary. These consents have been quashed once already, the regulator has twice asked Rosebank's operator for more information, and a rushed decision invites a fresh judicial review from the same campaigners who won in January 2025. Rosebank's paperwork runs behind Jackdaw's, which is why the both-by-Budget market sits near a coin flip. The prediction fails if the consultation is extended, if OPRED requests further information, or if a pre-action legal letter lands before the decision — any of which pushes consent past September without a single MP changing sides.
