Scotland03 Sep 2026

Scotland promises new council tax bands by 2028. It has tried this twice and landed it once.

The Programme for Government names a Council Tax (Scotland) Bill introducing new bands for high-value properties by April 2028.

4 min read

A named Bill, a named year, and a valuation roll from 1991

The Programme for Government names a Council Tax (Scotland) Bill introducing new tax bands for high-value properties by April 2028.

(Scottish Government, 1 Sep 2026)

The Council Tax (Scotland) Bill is one of nine Bills listed for 2026-27, described as introducing new tax bands for high-value properties by April 2028 and addressing the position where properties of very different value pay identical amounts (Scottish Government, 1 Sep 2026). It sits alongside a Budget Bill, a Food Prices Bill and a Public Service Renewal Bill in the same programme.

The problem it names is a valuation problem. Scottish council tax bands still rest on property values as at 1 April 1991, and band H, the top band, has no ceiling, so a flat in one city and a country house can sit in the same band. Adding bands at the top is the smaller of the two possible reforms; revaluing every property in Scotland is the larger one, and the Bill as described does not do it.

The mechanism is a Bill rather than an order, which is a change from the last time this was done. In 2016 the higher bands were moved by statutory instrument: The Council Tax (Substitution of Proportion) (Scotland) Order 2016 was made on 8 November 2016 and came into force on 1 April 2017, raising the multipliers for bands E to H by 7.5, 12.5, 17.5 and 22.5 per cent (legislation.gov.uk, 2016). Primary legislation takes longer and gives the opposition more places to stand.

The Pattern

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: this pattern counts every attempt by a Scottish administration since 1999 to change what households pay in council tax by changing the structure of the tax, where the outcome is on the public record. The test is whether the change took legal effect. Freezes and caps are excluded, because they hold the existing structure rather than change it, and they are recorded separately below. Consultations that were never followed by a proposal are excluded, because there is nothing to score.

2009
On 11 February 2009 John Swinney told MSPs that legislation for a 3p local income tax replacing council tax would not be introduced before the next election. Two reasons were given: no stable majority to steer the Bill through, and UK efficiency savings he said would take £500 million off the Scottish budget. Abandoned.
2016
The multipliers for bands E to H were raised by 7.5, 12.5, 17.5 and 22.5 per cent by an order made on 8 November 2016, in force from 1 April 2017. The only structural change to Scottish council tax that has taken effect since devolution.
2023
The joint Scottish Government and COSLA consultation A Fairer Council Tax ran from 12 July to 20 September 2023, proposing the same increases again for bands E to H and drawing 15,628 responses. The increases were never implemented. On 17 October 2023 the First Minister announced a council tax freeze for 2024-25 instead.
1 of 3
1 of 3, on the floor of what counts as a base rather than an anecdote. The honest complication is that the three cases are not independent of each other: the 2023 proposal was a deliberate repeat of the 2016 change, and it failed in a year when a freeze was politically cheaper. What the record measures is not the difficulty of the policy but the willingness of a government to carry a visible tax rise into an election cycle.

1 of the 3 structural attempts took effect. The one that did was made by order rather than by Bill, and the one that failed most recently proposed exactly the same change.

What this pattern excludes

The council tax freeze from 2008-09 to 2016-17, and again in 2024-25
A freeze holds the structure rather than changes it, so counting it would let a decision to do nothing score as a delivered reform. Worth recording as context: the 2024-25 freeze was agreed by every council, though Inverclyde first set an 8.2 per cent increase and households there were rebated in May 2024.
The 2015 Commission on Local Tax Reform
It reported options rather than proposing a change, so there is no proposal whose fate can be scored. The 2016 order is the government action that followed it and is counted.
Changes to reliefs, discounts and the second homes premium
They alter who pays the existing bands rather than the bands themselves. Including them would answer a different question about the same tax.

The Read

The fortnight market is low because the sequence runs the other way round. A Bill promised for a Parliament that has just opened normally arrives after a consultation, and this government has spent the first days of September publishing consultations on food prices and childcare rather than on property tax. Two weeks is not the window in which a valuation question gets asked.

The Bill market sits above evens for a straightforward reason: it is named in the programme, the deadline attached to it is April 2028 rather than this session, and a Bill that has to be law by 2028 has to be introduced well before then. What argues against is the 2023 case, where a consultation on precisely this change was overtaken within a month by a freeze, and the freeze market is the tell. A government that announces a freeze for 2027-28 in December has spent the political room it would need to add bands at the top, and the two markets should not both settle YES without something having changed in between.
