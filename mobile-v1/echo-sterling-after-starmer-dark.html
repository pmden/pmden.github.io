Starmer went. Sterling barely blinked — so far.

The pound slipped to $1.3181 as Keir Starmer resigned on 22 June, near its 2026 low, then touched $1.3164 three days later. The muted move hides the real question: where does sterling sit once Labour picks his successor?

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The pound fell to $1.3181, near its 2026 low of $1.3159, as Keir Starmer announced his resignation after a by-election defeat.

Bloomberg, 22 Jun 2026
2 · The event

A resignation the market had already priced

Sterling fell close to its 2026 low on Monday 22 June as Prime Minister Keir Starmer announced his resignation, briefly slipping to $1.3181 against the dollar — within sight of the year's low of $1.3159 set in March (Bloomberg, 22 June 2026). The move was deliberately subdued: the pound had already lost roughly 3% since February as Starmer's leadership came under increasing threat, and analysts at Rathbones noted gilt yields and sterling moved broadly with global trends rather than domestic politics (Bloomberg, 22 June 2026).

The pressure is coming from both sides of the Atlantic. The Federal Reserve turned hawkish in June, with markets pricing a possible hike rather than a cut, while June's flash PMI showed the UK services sector shrinking at its fastest rate in nearly three and a half years (The Guardian, 23 June 2026). Sterling touched $1.3164 on 25 June, its weakest of the month, before stabilising around $1.32.

The open question is the successor. Greater Manchester Mayor Andy Burnham has emerged as the frontrunner, and the currency market's verdict on the new prime minister — and crucially on whoever holds Number 11 and the fiscal rules — will be delivered against a dollar that is winning by default. Forecasters frame the rest of 2026 as a 1.30–1.40 range, with the pound starting at the bottom of it.

3 · The pattern

Sterling and resigning prime ministers: a split record

A prime minister resigning mid-term is a recurring British market event, and the pattern is consistent in one respect only: the resignation day itself is usually muted, because the damage is done in the run-up. What happens over the following three months is genuinely split — it depends on whether the successor reads as stability or as more risk.

2022
Truss, October 2022: sterling traded near $1.12 on resignation day after the mini-Budget crash. Three months later, with Sunak installed, it was around $1.24. Higher.
2022
Johnson, July 2022: the pound was near $1.19 when he quit. Amid a surging dollar and the Truss transition it fell to about $1.13 by early October. Lower.
2019
May, May 2019: sterling had already dropped about 4% in the weeks before her announcement at $1.27; by late August, under Johnson and no-deal risk, it was near $1.22. Lower.
2016
Cameron, June 2016: the referendum-morning resignation came amid an 8% crash; three months on, sterling was around $1.30 versus $1.37 immediately after the vote. Lower.
1990
Thatcher, November 1990: the pound rallied roughly two cents on the announcement and held its gains three months later as Major took over. Higher.
2 of 5
In 2 of 5 modern PM resignations, sterling was higher three months later. The split is honest and it hinges on the successor: continuity candidates (Major 1990, Sunak 2022) lifted the pound; contested or riskier transitions (2016, 2019, July 2022) sank it. This one resolves on who wins the Labour contest — and what they say about the fiscal rules.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case is range-bound weakness rather than a break of $1.30. The resignation was priced over months, the initial move was measured in fractions of a cent, and the base-rate lesson from 1990 and late 2022 is that a swift, credible succession lifts the pound off its lows. If the leadership contest resolves cleanly and the June MPC dissent hardens into UK rate-hike expectations, the rate differential starts working for sterling, not against it.

The counter-case is that both engines of the decline are still running. The Fed turned hawkish while the UK economy printed a 14-month PMI low, and 3 of 5 resignation precedents saw sterling lower three months on — every one of them a case where the succession added risk rather than removing it. A contested contest, or a new chancellor loosening fiscal rules with gilt investors already nervous, is exactly the 2019-shaped path that ends with $1.30 giving way.
