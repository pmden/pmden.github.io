Ukraine21 Aug 2026

Four purges since 2022, no election. Now the bureau is inside Zelenskyy's office.

Volodymyr Zelenskyy has dismissed his deputy chief of staff hours after anti-corruption investigators searched her home, while his sacked defence minister demands a wartime election. Every previous corruption wave has cost officials their jobs within days. None has moved the election clock.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Zelenskyy dismissed deputy chief of staff Iryna Mudra by decree on 19 August, hours after investigators searched her home in a money-laundering probe, as the anti-corruption bureau alleged a criminal group spanning the president's office, a serving MP and state-bank executives.

The Guardian, 19 Aug 2026
2 · The event

What happened

Volodymyr Zelenskyy dismissed Iryna Mudra, the deputy head of his office responsible for judicial affairs, by decree on 19 August; the decree gave no reason, and Ukrainian media reported her home was being searched the same day (The Guardian, 19 Aug 2026). The National Anti-Corruption Bureau says it has identified a criminal group spanning high-ranking officials of the president's office, a serving MP, a former MP and state-bank executives, which allegedly laundered 150m hryvnias, about £2.5m, through a state-owned bank to post June bail for the former energy minister Herman Haluschenko (The Guardian, 19 Aug 2026).

The searches extend a chain running back to the Energoatom kickback scandal uncovered in November 2025, which sent the presidential associate Timur Mindich fleeing to Israel; the former chief of staff Andriy Yermak resigned during that investigation and was arrested on money-laundering suspicion in May (The Guardian, 19 Aug 2026). July's removal of Mykhailo Fedorov, the 35-year-old defence minister widely seen as a reformer, produced the largest street protests of the war, with a Sunday rally of roughly 2,000 people outside the presidential office (The Guardian, 19 Aug 2026).

Fedorov answered on 18 August with a video address demanding a wartime presidential election, arguing that democracy cannot be held hostage to Russia and proposing what he called a legal, safe and realistic mechanism for a vote (The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026). Martial law has suspended elections since 2022 and Zelenskyy's five-year term expired in May 2024; August polling puts him on 22.3%, the former commander Valerii Zaluzhnyi on 21.4% and Fedorov third on 13.1%, with run-off projections favouring both challengers (The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026). Parliament has confirmed Yevhen Khmara as the new defence minister.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Wartime Ukraine has been through four major corruption confrontations since the full-scale invasion. Each followed the same arc: investigators move, senior officials fall within days, the state absorbs the shock — and the election question stays exactly where it was.

2023
January 2023: a procurement scandal over food bought at inflated prices swept out the deputy head of the president's office Kyrylo Tymoshenko, a deputy defence minister and a deputy prosecutor general — roughly a dozen senior officials gone within days.
2023
September 2023: defence minister Oleksii Reznikov was replaced after months of supply scandals he was never personally accused of. The defence ministry changed hands 18 months into the invasion without a pause in Western support.
2025
July 2025: a law curbing the anti-corruption bureau's independence triggered the first wartime street protests. Within 9 days parliament restored the agencies' powers at Zelenskyy's own request — a full retreat.
2025
November 2025: the bureau's Energoatom file alleged kickbacks of roughly $100m on nuclear-power contracts. Two ministers were dismissed within a week, and the scandal ultimately consumed chief of staff Andriy Yermak.
4 of 4
In all four corruption confrontations since 2022, senior officials were out within days of the investigators moving — a dozen in January 2023, the defence minister that September, two ministers and eventually the chief of staff in 2025. In none of the four did the crisis shift the election question: martial law outlasted every scandal, and the polls stayed shut.

Four confrontations, four rounds of departures within days of the investigators' move — and zero movement on holding an election.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case says the purge is not finished. Every previous wave took more than one senior official, the bureau's current file already names an MP and bank executives beyond Mudra, and Zelenskyy's standard response — rapid dismissals to get ahead of the story — has been consistent across all four precedents. That is why the next-departure market sits at 66%. The election market prices the other side of the pattern: the constitution bars wartime elections, a fifth of the country is occupied, and a president trailing both challengers in run-off projections has no incentive to volunteer a date.

The honest counter-case is that this wave is structurally different. For the first time the investigation is inside the president's own office while a popular sacked minister stands outside it with 13.1% and a protest movement — the combination none of the four precedents contained. Fedorov may also wait rather than declare: martial law makes a formal candidacy pointless, which is why his market sits below a third. The next-departure prediction fails if the bureau's file stalls at notices of suspicion, if Zelenskyy chooses to tough this one out to avoid feeding Fedorov's crisis narrative, or if a major Russian escalation — Kyiv was under heavy bombardment on 20 August — freezes domestic politics entirely.
