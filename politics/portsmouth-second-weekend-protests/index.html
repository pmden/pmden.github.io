Portsmouth protests return a second weekend. 2 of 3 past cases went on to a third.

Anti-immigration protesters marched in Portsmouth again on 12 September, a week after the trouble in Eastney. Three arrests. Police seek six more men.

4 min read

What happened

Anti-immigration protesters marched from Commercial Road to Guildhall Square in Portsmouth on Saturday 12 September 2026, a week after the Eastney Marina disorder, met by a Stand Up to Racism counter-march and a police operation that made three arrests, including the first under the new face-covering power.

(The Guardian 12 Sep)

Anti-immigration protesters gathered outside All Saints Church on Commercial Road shortly before midday on Saturday and marched to Guildhall Square chanting against small boats; the BBC put the march at up to 500 people and the Stand Up to Racism counter-march that followed on the same route at about 300. Police kept the two groups apart under protest conditions. Officers arrested a 76-year-old man on suspicion of concealing his identity, the first use in the city of the Crime and Policing Act power that took effect at 10am, a 33-year-old on suspicion of obstructing the highway and criminal damage, and a 16-year-old from Waterlooville on suspicion of violent disorder at Eastney the previous Sunday. The police and crime commissioner said the day passed without a repeat of the assaults on officers seen a week earlier. (Guardian 12 Sep; BBC 12 Sep)

The second weekend followed the disorder of 6 September, when hundreds of people blocked roads at Eastney Marina to stop coaches carrying 120 people who had crossed from near Cherbourg in a dinghy; seven officers were treated for injuries, Coastguard and Border Force vehicles were damaged and nobody was arrested on the night. On Sunday Hampshire and Isle of Wight Constabulary released CCTV images of six men after reviewing hundreds of hours of footage, and the assistant chief constable said arrests at the time are not always possible and that the investigation should be read as a warning for future events. Three people have been charged so far: a 42-year-old with violent disorder and assaulting an officer, remanded to the crown court on 16 September; the 76-year-old with concealing his identity, in court on Monday; and the 33-year-old with obstructing the highway and attempted criminal damage, listed for 29 October. (Guardian 13 Sep; BBC 13 Sep; BBC 13 Sep)

Ministers have so far acted through the police rather than Parliament. The policing minister told MPs on 7 September that Dover and Portsmouth were protest activity, the home secretary told chief constables on 10 September to make full use of the face-covering power, and on 12 September Hampshire, Staffordshire and Northumbria all used it. Kent Police made no arrests at Dover. The Patriot Platform, launched at Dover on 5 September, distributes its footage through a YouTube channel with more than a million views and a £4.99 monthly subscription, and its leader told a recruitment event in August of a large push in the following three or four weeks. Hope Not Hate says the 2024 riots were shut down by making arrests quickly. (Guardian 13 Sep; Guardian 12 Sep; Guardian 10 Sep; BBC 12 Sep)

The Read

The organisers' need is visibility and income: the Patriot Platform's leader distributes footage through a subscription channel and told recruits in August to expect a large push within three or four weeks, while a locally branded Portsmouth group supplied Saturday's march. The police need is deterrence by charge, the lesson Hope Not Hate draws from 2024, and Hampshire's assistant chief constable framed Sunday's six-man appeal as a warning for future events. The government's need is to be seen directing enforcement without new law: the policing minister took the Commons statement on 7 September and the home secretary told chiefs on 10 September to use the powers they have. The actors with a move inside the window are Hampshire and Isle of Wight Constabulary and the CPS, which control charges and conditions, the Patriot Platform and the Portsmouth group, which decide whether to call a third march, and Stand Up to Racism, which has said it will oppose every one. (Guardian 12 Sep; Guardian 13 Sep; Guardian 13 Sep; BBC 12 Sep)

The weighted case is a third weekend with arrests. Two of the three series that reached a second consecutive weekend went on to a third, and the closest analogue, Epping in 2025, ran every Sunday from 20 July to 31 August with arrests on most of them. Portsmouth has what Epping had, a local group and a national one, and the face-covering designation means that any march now carries an arrest risk even when nothing else happens, as the 76-year-old's charge shows. The market opens at 45 rather than at the fraction because Saturday passed without disorder by the commissioner's own account, the trigger was a single boat landing rather than a hotel that stays in place, and three cases is a thin base. (Guardian 20 Jul 2025; Guardian 31 Aug 2025; BBC 13 Sep)

The honest counter-case is the 2024 wave and Knowsley. In 2024 no town saw a second weekend once charges and sentences flowed within days, and in 2023 the Knowsley series faded after the fifteen night-one arrests without a third-weekend protest making the record. The Patriot Platform's method is mobile, following boat landings rather than a fixed address, so its next appearance may be at another port. The prediction fails if no march is called for 19 or 20 September or if one passes with no arrest; the charges market fails if the six-man appeal produces interviews and bail rather than charges by 27 September; the Commons market fails if the Home Office lets the 7 September statement stand and the House rises on 16 September without the home secretary at the dispatch box. (Guardian 13 Sep; Guardian 11 Feb 2023; Guardian 13 Sep)
