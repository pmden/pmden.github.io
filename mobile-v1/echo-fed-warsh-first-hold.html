Warsh's first vote is a hold. The dots say hike.

Kevin Warsh kept the federal funds rate at 3.50–3.75% at his first FOMC meeting on 17 June. Nine of 18 officials pencilled in at least one rise before year-end.

4 min read

A hold with a hawkish accent

The Fed held rates at 3.50–3.75% at Kevin Warsh's first meeting as chair, while a raised dot plot showed nine of 18 officials projecting a hike before year-end.

(Bloomberg, 17 Jun 2026)

The Federal Open Market Committee voted unanimously on 17 June to keep the target range for the federal funds rate at 3.50–3.75%, the first decision of the Kevin Warsh era. The statement itself was the news as much as the rate: dramatically shortened, stripped of the language that had signalled a bias toward future cuts, and blunt that inflation "remains elevated" relative to the 2% goal (Federal Reserve, 17 Jun 2026; Bloomberg, 17 Jun 2026).

The summary of economic projections turned the hold into a warning. Nine of 18 officials now project at least one rate rise before the end of 2026, six of them two quarter-point moves, and traders came away pricing a possible hike as early as October (Bloomberg, 17 Jun 2026). The backdrop is a genuine inflation problem: headline CPI ran at 4.2% in May, the fastest in three years, driven by a 23.5% annual jump in energy prices after the Iran war closed the Strait of Hormuz — though core inflation sat at a far tamer 2.9% (Bloomberg, 10 Jun 2026).

Two weeks later the ground shifted. June payrolls rose just 57,000 against a 115,000 consensus, and markets promptly pared expectations of an autumn hike, knocking the dollar to its biggest weekly drop in nearly three months (Bloomberg, 2 Jul 2026). The committee reconvenes on 28–29 July with its two mandates now pulling in opposite directions.

The Pattern

Warsh is the sixth Fed chair since 1979 to take the seat with price pressure live and the committee's stance in question. The record of his predecessors is strikingly one-sided: chairs who inherited a tightening question tended to answer it fast.

2018
Jerome Powell took office in February and raised rates at his very first meeting in March, to 1.50–1.75%. He delivered four hikes in his first year.
2014
Janet Yellen inherited the taper, not a hike cycle — and waited. The first rate rise of her tenure came in December 2015, 22 months after she took office. The exception in the set.
2006
Ben Bernanke took over in February and hiked at his first meeting in March, extending the cycle to 5.25% by June — his first five months were all tightening.
1987
Alan Greenspan was sworn in on 11 August and raised the discount rate within four weeks, on 4 September — the first rise in three years. Black Monday followed in October; he tightened anyway.
1979
Paul Volcker took office in August and by 6 October had launched the "Saturday Night Special": discount rate to 12% and a whole new operating regime to crush inflation.
4 of 5
Four of five new Fed chairs who inherited an inflation question tightened within their first two meetings; only Yellen waited. Warsh has already used his first meeting on a hold — July is the second.

Four of five incoming chairs facing price pressure tightened within their first two meetings.

The Read

The weight of history and the weight of the dots point the same way: this committee wants to tighten, and four of five new chairs in a comparable spot delivered fast. Headline inflation at 4.2% gives Warsh cover, and his rewritten statement — shorter, cut-bias deleted — reads like a chair preparing the ground rather than marking time. But the timing case for July specifically is thin. The committee was unanimous in June, market pricing has settled on October, and a chair who prizes credibility rarely spends it on surprising his second meeting.

The honest counter-case is that the hike never comes at all. The inflation spike is overwhelmingly an energy shock — core is running at 2.9%, within sight of target — and supply-driven price surges can fade as fast as they arrive if oil retreats. Meanwhile the labour market printed 57,000 and participation is falling: if July's payrolls confirm the stall, the dots of June will look like the peak of the hawkish moment, not the start of it. Yellen's 22-month wait is the reminder that inherited intentions are not commitments.
