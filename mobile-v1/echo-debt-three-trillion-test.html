Public debt31 Aug 2026

Net debt stalls £15bn below £3 trillion. August rarely holds the line.

Public sector net debt stood at £2,984.9 billion at the end of July, after the first monthly fall of the financial year. The August figure, published on 22 September, decides whether Britain's debt stock carries a thirteenth digit into the Budget.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Public sector net debt excluding banks was £2,984.9bn at the end of July 2026, or 94.1% of GDP, after July borrowing of £1.8bn came in £2.3bn above the OBR's forecast.

ONS, 21 Aug 2026
2 · The event

£15.1bn from a thirteenth digit

The debt stock fell in July for the first time this financial year, from £2,994.8 billion in June to £2,984.9 billion, equivalent to 94.1% of GDP and 0.8 percentage points lower than a year earlier (ONS, 21 Aug 2026). July is the Treasury's strongest receipts month because self-assessment income tax lands in it, so a fall is normal. The June reading was nonetheless the highest in a series the ONS has published monthly since March 1975 (ONS series HF6W).

The flow underneath the stock is still running hot. Borrowing in July was £1.8 billion, £0.7 billion more than in July 2025 and £2.3 billion above the profile the Office for Budget Responsibility set out in March. Central government debt interest was £7.7 billion in the month, £0.7 billion higher than a year earlier, driven by the retail prices index uplift on index-linked gilts. Cumulative borrowing for April to July was £56.7 billion, 9.6% below the same period last year but the same £2.3 billion above the OBR's line (ONS, 21 Aug 2026).

The next print is fixed. The ONS publishes August public sector finances at 7am on 22 September (ONS release calendar). It arrives five weeks before John Healey's first Budget on 28 October, and it is the last debt reading before the OBR closes its pre-Budget forecast round. A £3 trillion headline would be a round number with no policy content and considerable political weight.

3 · The pattern

What Augusts do to the debt stock

This is a threshold event, not a decision. Nobody chooses whether debt crosses £3 trillion; a continuous series either travels £15.1 billion in a month or it does not. Counting episodes would be the wrong tool, so the base rate here is taken from the series itself. Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: every August in the ONS net debt series from 2016 to 2025 inclusive, ten consecutive observations, no gaps, no extension of the window. The test is whether net debt excluding public sector banks rose by £15.1 billion or more from the preceding July.

4 of 10
Four of the last ten Augusts produced a month-on-month rise in net debt of £15.1bn or more. The raw fraction understates the current setting in one respect and overstates it in another. It understates because the three most recent Augusts all cleared the bar, on a stock that is now larger and carrying more index-linked interest. It overstates because July 2026 already delivered an unusually strong receipts month, borrowing has run 9.6% below last year across the financial year to date, and the OBR profile the ONS measures against assumes the gap narrows rather than widens.

In 4 of the last 10 Augusts the debt stock rose by £15.1bn or more. Three of those four are the three most recent.

What this pattern excludes

Every month that is not an August, 2016 to 2025
Net debt has a pronounced seasonal shape — January falls on self-assessment receipts, spring rises on gilt issuance — so a mixed-month sample would misstate the odds for a print covering August alone.
Augusts before 2016
The window was fixed at ten consecutive observations before the count was run. Extending it into years when the stock was under £1.6 trillion, and monthly moves were correspondingly smaller in cash terms, would push the fraction down for reasons of scale rather than behaviour.
August 2020, a rise of £31.2bn
Considered for removal as a pandemic outlier and kept. Dropping the single largest observation would cut the fraction to 3 of 9, and no justification for the removal survives except its effect on the answer.
The net debt measure including public sector banks
The ONS headline and the OBR forecast profile both use the excluding-banks measure. Switching series would move the £3 trillion line itself and make the comparison meaningless.

5 · The read

The read

The weighted case is that the line holds for one more month. July's £1.8bn of borrowing was flattered by record self-assessment receipts, and August has no equivalent support: borrowing in August has run between £11.5bn and £14.9bn in each of the last four years while the debt stock moved by less than the headline deficit, because gilt premiums, cash timing and the Bank of England's balance sheet all sit between the flow and the stock. That wedge is why the four-of-ten fraction is lower than the raw borrowing numbers would suggest, and it is why the market sits below evens on the £3 trillion question while still favouring a bigger borrowing month than last August.

The counter-case is index-linked interest and the calendar. Debt interest was £7.7bn in July on RPI-linked movements, and the uplift falls unevenly across months, which is what pushed August 2023, 2024 and 2025 above the £15bn bar in succession. Add a debt stock that has grown roughly £96bn in a year and the same percentage move now buys more cash. A crossing would be arithmetic rather than news, but it would hand the Chancellor a headline number five weeks before a Budget already framed around borrowing capacity.
