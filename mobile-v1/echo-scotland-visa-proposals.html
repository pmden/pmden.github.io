Scotland03 Sep 2026

Scotland asks for its own visa for the fifth time. The first four got nothing.

The Scottish Government published proposals on 29 August for a Scottish Visa, a Rural Visa and a Scottish Graduate Visa, citing population falls in 20 of 32 council areas. Immigration is reserved, and the record of asking is short and one-directional.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The Scottish Government publishes proposals for a Scottish Visa, a Rural Visa and a Scottish Graduate Visa, and invites the UK Migration Minister to discuss them.

Scottish Government, 29 Aug 2026
2 · The event

Three routes, twenty shrinking councils, one reserved power

Business Minister Tom Arthur published the paper on 29 August, setting out a Scottish Visa, a Rural Visa and a Scottish Graduate Visa, and saying he had invited the UK Migration Minister to constructive discussions to ensure the immigration system is tailored to Scotland's needs (Scottish Government, 29 Aug 2026). The paper singles out the closure of the adult social care visa route as a risk to workforce supply in rural and island communities.

The demographic case is made with published estimates rather than assertion. The paper cites National Records of Scotland figures showing populations fell in 20 of Scotland's 32 council areas between mid-2024 and mid-2025, that 21.0 per cent of the population was aged 65 or over at mid-2025 against 16.3 per cent twenty years earlier, and that the share aged 0 to 15 fell from 18.2 to 15.8 per cent over the same period.

None of that changes where the power sits. Immigration is reserved under the Scotland Act 1998, so a Scottish visa can only exist if a UK minister creates one, and the paper is therefore a request rather than a plan. That is what makes it countable: the ask is published, dated and specific, and so is the answer whenever it comes.

3 · The pattern

What has come back the last four times Scotland asked

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: this pattern counts every formal Scottish Government publication since 2015 proposing a Scotland-specific migration route or a devolved migration power, where the UK Government's position is now on the record. The test is single: did a Scotland-specific immigration route come into existence. Proposals still awaiting an answer are excluded, which is why the August 2026 paper is not in the count. The list is drawn from Scottish Government publications and is stated as at least four rather than exactly four, because the government's own publication search is unreliable for older items.

2018
Scotland's population needs and migration policy, a discussion paper published on 7 February 2018 under Fiona Hyslop, arguing for a tailored solution and modelling a 4.5 per cent reduction in Scottish GDP by 2040 from lower migration. No published UK Government response has been found.
2020
Migration: helping Scotland prosper, published on 27 January 2020, proposed a Scottish Visa with Scottish ministers assessing and nominating applicants and the Home Office issuing the visa. Within hours the Home Office dismissed it, saying immigration will remain a reserved matter.
2022
The Rural visa pilot proposal, published on 27 September 2022, followed two recommendations from the UK Government's own Migration Advisory Committee, in May 2019 and January 2020, that a remote areas pilot should be run. It was never implemented, and a UK Government source later said they were not at all considering a Scottish visa system.
2025
Migration: meeting Scotland's needs, published on 22 January 2025, proposed a Scottish Graduate Visa giving graduates two more years to qualify. The UK immigration white paper of 12 May 2025 contained no such route (Scottish Government, Jan 2025).
0 of 4
0 of 4. Two limits to hold alongside it. Four is still a thin base, and three of the four refusals came from the same party in government at Westminster, so most of the record describes one administration's revealed position rather than a constant of the constitution. The exception is the one that matters: the January 2025 paper was put to a Labour government, and its own white paper of 12 May 2025 carried no Scotland-specific route either. The change of government in 2024 was the only reason to expect a different answer, and it has already been tested once.

0 of the 4 decided proposals produced a Scotland-specific immigration route. Two were refused in terms, one was answered only by omission from a white paper, and one received no published answer at all.

What this pattern excludes

The August 2026 paper itself
No UK Government response to it has been published as of 3 September 2026. Scoring an undecided case would be scoring a guess, and quotations from the 2024 Home Office position are answers to an earlier proposal, not to this one.
Scotland's separate shortage occupation list
It predates 2015 and was not produced by any of these proposals. Counting a pre-existing feature as an outcome would flatter the record.
Proposals for full devolution of immigration
That is a constitutional transfer rather than a route inside the existing system, and the mechanism that would deliver it is an Act of Parliament rather than a change to immigration rules.

5 · The read

The read

The first market is about whether Whitehall engages at all, and the honest answer from the record is that it usually does not engage in writing. Three of the four previous proposals produced either a same-day dismissal quoted to journalists or no published response, and a quotation given to a reporter is not a document on gov.uk. The fourth was answered only by omission from a published white paper. The Holyrood debate market sits higher because that is the arena the Scottish Government controls: a paper published as a hundred-day commitment, in a Parliament that has just returned, is a debate the government can schedule itself.

The long market is where the base rate does most of its work and where it should be held loosest. Three refusals from one administration is a thin record, and the composition of the UK Government has changed since the last of them. What has not changed is the structural reason for refusal, which is that a route defined by where someone lives requires enforcement of where they stay, and no UK department has wanted to build that. Until a UK minister publishes something that engages with that problem rather than with the principle, the pattern is the better guide.
