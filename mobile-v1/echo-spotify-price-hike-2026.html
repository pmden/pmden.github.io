Spotify raises prices again. 300 million is the next test.

Spotify lifted US Premium to $12.99 a month in January, its third increase in three years, with every tier rising. The question it sets up resolves this month: do subscribers keep coming anyway?

4 min readEcho #116INTERNATIONAL
1 · Today

Spotify raises US Premium to $12.99 a month from February, its third US increase in three years, with Student, Duo and Family tiers all rising.

Spotify Newsroom, 15 Jan 2026
2 · The event

What happened

Spotify announced on 15 January that US Premium Individual rises from $11.99 to $12.99 a month from February, with every tier moving: Student to $6.99, Duo to $18.99 and Family to $21.99 (Spotify Newsroom, 15 Jan 2026). Prices also rose in Estonia and Latvia. It is the third US increase in three years, after rises in July 2023 and June 2024 — a cumulative $3 a month added to the individual plan (Variety, Jan 2026).

The financial logic is straightforward. Spotify entered the year as the largest music service by a wide margin, with 751m monthly active users and 290m Premium subscribers as of Q4 2025.

The risk is cumulative fatigue. Each previous rise passed without visible churn, but a third in three years lands as streaming costs rise across the board — Netflix, Disney and the rest have moved in the same window. Spotify's Q2 2026 results, expected in late July, are the first full-quarter read on subscriber behaviour after the new prices took effect.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Streaming price rises have produced two very different outcomes depending on the service. Music subscriptions have so far absorbed every increase without a dent in growth. Video subscriptions have twice shown the opposite: a rise followed by a visible hit to subscriber numbers.

2024
Spotify's June rise to $11.99 passed cleanly: Premium subscribers grew from 246m to 252m the following quarter.
2023
Spotify's first US increase, to $10.99 in July, was followed by subscriber growth from 220m to 226m by Q3 — and the stock rose.
2022
The counter-example: Netflix's January rise coincided with its first subscriber loss in a decade — down 200,000 in Q1 2022 — and the shares fell 35% in a day.
2019
Netflix's then-biggest US increase, to $12.99, preceded a rare quarter of US subscriber decline that summer.
2 of 4
The record is genuinely split — two of four major streaming price rises were followed by uninterrupted growth, and both of those were Spotify's own. Music has no proven churn response yet; video has two. Which precedent governs a third hike in three years is exactly what is unresolved.

Music hikes: 2 for 2 with growth intact. Video hikes: 2 for 2 with subscriber pain. The pattern is split by medium.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case says Spotify clears 300m. The company has raised prices twice before and grown straight through both, its own history is the only music-streaming precedent that exists, and the arithmetic is friendly: from 290m at year-end, two quarters of net adds at anything near the recent 5m-per-quarter pace lands at or above the line. Music also differs structurally from video — there is one catalogue, switching means rebuilding a decade of playlists, and the $1 rise is smaller in percentage terms than either Netflix move that caused trouble.

The honest counter is that every precedent has a first breach. A third rise in three years arrives alongside increases from nearly every other subscription service, and household audits of recurring charges are exactly how music subscriptions die — not in protest, but in a spreadsheet. Q1 seasonality is historically Spotify's softest quarter for net adds, and if the February effective date pulled any churn into Q2, the 300m line could be missed by a rounding error while the business remains perfectly healthy. A 57% call is confidence, not certainty.
