The Treasury01 Jul 2026

The OBR opens the books. Reeves's autumn starts now.

The Office for Budget Responsibility publishes its 2026 Fiscal Risks and Sustainability report on 7 July — the last major audit of the public finances before Rachel Reeves frames her autumn Budget.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The OBR confirms its 2026 Fiscal Risks and Sustainability report will be published on 7 July, updating 50-year debt projections weeks before the Chancellor sets her autumn Budget strategy.

Office for Budget Responsibility, 1 Jul 2026
2 · The event

What happened

The Office for Budget Responsibility publishes its Fiscal Risks and Sustainability report on 7 July from the Darlington Economic Campus, updating its long-run projections for debt, demography and climate-related spending pressure OBR, July 2026. The report is the watchdog's annual stress test of the public finances, and it arrives at a delicate moment: at the March Spring Statement the OBR cut its 2026 growth forecast to 1.1% from 1.4%, even as it projected borrowing falling from 4.3% of GDP this year to 3.6% next OBR, 3 Mar 2026.

Reeves used the Spring Statement to hold the line on her single-fiscal-event rule, announcing no new tax or spending measures and seizing on the OBR's improved borrowing path — £8bn lower by 2030-31 than the autumn projection — as evidence the strategy is working OBR, March 2026. In January she said her £26bn tax-raising Budget had 'likely drawn a line' under further increases Bloomberg, 20 Jan 2026.

The gilt market has given her little room: ten-year yields were back near 4.8% in late June as leadership speculation built (The Guardian, 19 June 2026), keeping a debt-interest bill that costs taxpayers billions a year more than peer economies The next tests come quickly: June borrowing figures from the ONS in late July, then the naming of a Budget date.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

The OBR has published a summer fiscal-risks assessment in most years since 2017, and each edition has warned that UK debt is on an unsustainable long-run path. The question is what the Chancellor of the day did at the next fiscal event. The recent record leans one way — but not unanimously.

2025
Reeves's second Budget in November raised taxes by £26bn, after a July fiscal risks report and months of speculation about a shortfall against her rules.
2024
Reeves's first Budget in October raised £40bn in tax, three months after the Treasury's July audit claimed a £22bn in-year hole.
2023
The exception: despite OBR long-run warnings, Jeremy Hunt cut national insurance by 2p at the November Autumn Statement as inflation fell.
2022
Hunt's November Autumn Statement delivered a consolidation of around £55bn, weeks after the September mini-budget broke the gilt market.
2021
Two months after the OBR's July fiscal risks report, the Health and Social Care Levy raised roughly £12bn a year.
4 of 5
In four of the last five years, the autumn fiscal event that followed a summer warning on the public finances raised taxes. 2023 is the exception — falling inflation bought Hunt room to cut instead.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case: the machinery is pointing the same way it pointed in 2024 and 2025. The OBR's long-run projections will not have improved enough to change the headline — UK debt remains among the highest in the advanced world, with the third-highest borrowing costs of 36 rich economies. Every summer that message has landed alongside a borrowing overshoot, and in four of the last five years the autumn answer was more tax. June borrowing above £20bn would extend that rhythm and harden expectations before Reeves even stands up.

The honest counter-case: 2026 is not 2024. Ten-year gilt yields were still near 4.8% in late June, the OBR's March update cut projected borrowing by £8bn by 2030-31 against the autumn view, and Reeves has publicly committed to no further big tax rises. If the FRS reads as a long-horizon document rather than a near-term alarm, and June borrowing lands under £20bn, the summer could pass without the pressure that forced her predecessors' hands. The 2023 precedent shows a Chancellor with improving numbers can bank them instead.
