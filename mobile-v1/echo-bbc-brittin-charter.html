Brittin takes the chair. The charter clock is running.

Matt Brittin became BBC director-general on 18 May after Tim Davie's resignation, with charter renewal and the £180 licence fee's future unresolved.

5 min read

An outsider inherits a corporation in review

The BBC confirmed former Google EMEA president Matt Brittin as director-general; he took charge on 18 May on £565,000, with charter renewal now his defining test.

(Deadline, 25 Mar 2026)

The BBC confirmed Matt Brittin as its next director-general on 25 March 2026 (Deadline), ending the search triggered by Tim Davie's resignation in November 2025 over the Panorama editing scandal. Davie left on 2 April; Rhodri Talfan Davies held the post on an interim basis (Deadline, Jan 2026) until Brittin took charge on 18 May on a salary of £565,000 (Variety).

Brittin arrives with the corporation's constitutional future open. The government launched the charter review on 16 December 2025 (GOV.UK), publishing a green paper that puts licence fee reform, concessions and commercial revenue all on the table; the public consultation closed on 10 March 2026. The current charter, and the £180 fee set in April 2026 under the existing settlement, run only to the end of 2027.

The first hard test of the new regime is close. The BBC's annual report, historically published in July, will disclose licence fee income after another year of cancellations, the corporation's on-air pay list in the first full year without Gary Lineker's £1.35 million, and the financial baseline Brittin must defend into charter negotiations.

The Pattern

The BBC has changed director-general eight times since 1987, and the record splits cleanly in two: half the exits were orderly handovers, half were resignations under fire. Editorial crisis, not funding, is what removes a DG early.

2025
Tim Davie resigned on 9 November 2025 over the Panorama Trump edit row after 5 years in post, taking News CEO Deborah Turness with him.
2012
George Entwistle resigned after 54 days, the shortest tenure in BBC history, over Newsnight's failures in the Savile affair.
2013
Tony Hall, brought in to steady the corporation after Entwistle, served 7 years and left on his own terms in 2020, the model for a post-crisis recovery.
2004
Greg Dyke, like Brittin an external commercial hire, resigned in January 2004 after the Hutton Report, roughly 4 years in.
1987
Alasdair Milne was forced to resign by the governors under government pressure, the first DG removed in the corporation's history.
4 of 8
4 of the 8 director-generals since 1987 left through forced or crisis resignation; 4 completed orderly exits. The pattern is genuinely split — but the post-crisis appointee (Hall in 2013) survived, which is the slot Brittin now occupies.

Of 8 DGs since 1987, 4 left in crisis resignations and 4 left on their own terms — a true coin-flip record.

The Read

The weighted case: the annual report lands inside the window. The BBC has published its annual report in July in every recent year — 23 July in 2024, mid-July in 2025 — because the disclosure cycle is tied to the pre-summer parliamentary calendar. A new director-general has every incentive to get bad news out early and date-stamp it to his predecessor's era. On the pay list, Lineker's departure in May 2025 removes the perennial number one, and Radio 2 and Radio 1 breakfast salaries (Zoe Ball's reduced role, Greg James's rising one) make James the structural favourite.

The honest counter-case: transitions delay paperwork. Davie's exit, an interim DG, and a new leadership team bedding in through May give the corporation a plausible reason to slip publication into September, as the report is signed off by the board the DG has only just joined. And the pay list has surprised before — presenter pay disclosures cover only licence-fee-funded roles, so a Fiona Bruce (Question Time, Antiques Roadshow) or an undisclosed news salary can top a list the public expects a DJ to lead. A September publication resolves the first market NO and voids the shortcut everyone is pricing.
