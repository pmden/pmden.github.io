The car-loan bill is on hold. The reckoning isn't.

Parts of the FCA's £9.1bn motor finance redress scheme were suspended on 2 July while four legal challenges head to the Upper Tribunal — weeks before Britain's big banks report half-year results.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The Upper Tribunal suspends parts of the FCA's motor finance redress scheme while four legal challenges proceed — lenders need not calculate or pay compensation until the cases are resolved.

Financial Conduct Authority, 2 Jul 2026
2 · The event

What happened

The Upper Tribunal on 2 July suspended parts of the Financial Conduct Authority's motor finance redress scheme, on terms agreed between the regulator and four challengers: consumer group Consumer Voice and three lenders — Volkswagen Financial Services, Mercedes-Benz Financial Services and Crédit Agricole Auto Finance FCA, 2 Jul 2026. Until the challenges are heard — listed for 14–18 December 2026 or 16–26 February 2027 — lenders do not have to calculate compensation, pay it, or contact eligible customers FCA, updated 2 Jul 2026.

The scheme itself, confirmed by the FCA on 30 March, covers around 12.1 million motor finance agreements written between April 2007 and November 2024 where commission paid to dealers was not properly disclosed. The regulator puts redress at £7.5bn — an average of £829 per agreement — and the total cost including administration at £9.1bn FCA PS26/3, 30 Mar 2026. If the scheme is upheld without appeal, payments could begin in 2027; if it is overturned, redress slips to 2028 or beyond.

Notably, the big banks are not among the challengers. Lloyds — the most exposed lender through its Black Horse arm — along with Barclays, Santander and Close Brothers declined to fight the scheme, even while questioning whether its scope compensates motorists who suffered no loss. Their provisions stay on the books through the pause, and the market gets its first look at how boards are treating the delay when Barclays reports half-year results on 28 July, Lloyds on 30 July and NatWest on 31 July.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Britain has run this play before: a mis-selling scandal, a regulator-designed redress programme, an opening industry estimate. The record on whether first estimates hold is not unanimous — but the two biggest programmes blew through them by multiples.

2026
Motor finance: FCA confirms the scheme at £7.5bn redress, £9.1bn all-in, across 12.1 million agreements — then a tribunal pauses it within four months.
2019
PPI closes: the final industry bill reaches £38.3bn — against first bank provisions of roughly £5bn in 2011, an overshoot of more than sevenfold.
2013
The counter-case: interest rate hedging product redress for small firms settles near £2.2bn, close to early expectations.
2002
The personal pensions mis-selling review concludes at about £11.8bn — several times the estimates made when the review began in 1994.
2 of 3
Of the three completed major UK redress programmes, two — PPI and pensions mis-selling — ended several times above their opening estimates; one, the swaps redress, landed close to them. The record leans towards bills growing, but it is not a clean sweep.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case: the suspension changes the timetable, not the liability. Banks provisioned against the scheme the FCA confirmed in March, the big lenders chose not to challenge it, and accounting rules do not let a board release or freeze a provision merely because payment dates slipped — auditors will want the best current estimate either way. With the challenge brought largely by carmakers' finance arms rather than the banks, the base case for July results is provisions held steady and the pause framed as operational relief, which is why the market prices an addition at only one in three.

The honest counter-case: PPI began with provisions a seventh of the final bill, and the mechanism was the same one now in play — scope questions resolved in consumers' favour, one tranche at a time. Consumer Voice's challenge argues the scheme is too narrow; if the tribunal agrees even in part, the £9.1bn becomes a floor, not a ceiling. And the December-or-February hearing dates mean the uncertainty discount on UK bank shares survives at least two more results seasons, whatever July's statements say.
