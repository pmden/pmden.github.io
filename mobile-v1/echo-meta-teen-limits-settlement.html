Meta buys peace for $18bn. Five of it depends on the rivals.

Meta has settled with 47 US states and four territories, agreeing two-hour daily limits for under-18s, an overnight curfew and an end to cosmetic surgery filters. The last $5bn is payable only if Snapchat, TikTok and YouTube adopt similar measures.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Meta agreed a settlement worth up to $16.68bn over ten years plus $10bn in legal fees, suspending a trial in Oakland that began on 18 August, with the judge indicating she is inclined to approve it.

Al Jazeera, 26 Aug 2026
2 · The event

What happened

Meta settled on 26 August, eight days into a trial in the federal court at Oakland that had been expected to run six weeks. The headline package is $18bn: $16.7bn to 47 states, Washington DC, Puerto Rico, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands, and more than $1bn to Texas separately, paid over ten years. California takes $2.2bn and New York $1.1bn (Al Jazeera, 27 Aug 2026).

The structure is conditional. Meta pays 70%, about $12.7bn, regardless. The remaining $5bn falls due only if Snapchat, TikTok and YouTube adopt similar measures, and Meta will cut its own daily limit from two hours to one if competitors follow. The product changes include a two-hour daily cap for under-18s, a curfew from midnight to 6am, notification blackouts during school hours, hidden like counts on teen accounts, a non-personalised feed option and removal of accounts held by children under 13 (Al Jazeera, 26 Aug 2026).

Nothing starts until the court approves. Implementation then runs on a clock: non-personalised feeds within four months of approval, broader compliance within six, age assurance within a year. Parents who lost children have questioned the deal (Al Jazeera, 27 Aug 2026). The UK has announced a ban on social media for under-16s to come into force next year and is weighing overnight curfews and limits on infinite scrolling for under-18s.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Australia has been running the closest thing to a controlled experiment since December 2025: a legislated under-16 ban across the same platforms, with fines attached. The sequence of official assessments since then is the best evidence anyone has on whether account rules move teenagers.

2024
In November Australia passed legislation banning under-16s from social media, the first country to do so (Al Jazeera, 28 Nov 2024).
2025
The ban took effect on 10 December across Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, TikTok, Snapchat and Reddit, with fines of about $33m for platforms that failed to remove under-16 users (Al Jazeera, 9 Dec 2025).
2026
In January the government declared victory, reporting 4.7 million accounts closed (Al Jazeera, 16 Jan 2026).
2026
In June the maximum penalty for systemic breaches was doubled from A$49.5m to A$99m because children were bypassing the checks (Al Jazeera, 27 Jun 2026).
2026
In August the regulator's study found more than eight in ten young Australian teens and preteens still using the platforms, through VPNs, fake age checks and unlisted apps (Al Jazeera, 3 Aug 2026).
1 of 3
Three official checkpoints have followed Australia's ban. January's headline of 4.7 million accounts closed was the one that read as success. June's doubling of fines and August's regulator study, which found more than eight in ten young teens still using the platforms, read as failure of enforcement rather than of intent. Meta's settlement borrows the same instruments, age assurance and account rules, and adds a time limit the Australian regime does not have.

1 of 3 official assessments of Australia's ban found it working; the other two found children bypassing the checks and prompted heavier fines.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case is that the court approves and the rivals do not follow. Judges approve negotiated state settlements of this size as a matter of routine, the judge has already signalled she is inclined to, and 47 states plus four territories signing removes the main objection route. The $5bn contingency is the interesting number precisely because it prices Meta's own expectation that rivals will refuse: a two-hour default cap is a direct hit to engagement, and TikTok, Snap and YouTube face no equivalent trial forcing them to accept one. Meta would rather pay nothing extra and see its own limit stay at two hours.

The honest counter runs through Westminster rather than Oakland. Britain has announced an under-16 ban for next year and is actively considering curfews and scroll limits, and the European Commission has told Meta the ball is in its court on extending the protections to EU users. If regulators impose what Meta has just conceded, the rivals adopt time limits by law and the contingency triggers anyway. The Australian record is the reason to keep expectations low on outcomes: 4.7 million accounts closed, fines doubled, and eight in ten young teens still using the apps.
