The dollar clawed back above 100. Payrolls just shook the floor.

June's 57,000-payroll report pushed back Fed rate-rise expectations. The dollar index fell to 100.77 on 3 July, its biggest weekly drop in nearly three months.

4 min read

A round trip meets a soft jobs print

The dollar index slid to 100.77, down 0.58% for the week — its biggest weekly drop since early April — after June's weak jobs report cooled expectations of a Fed hike.

(Reuters, 3 Jul 2026)

The dollar index closed the first week of July at 100.77, down 0.58% on the week, its steepest weekly fall since early April. June's payroll gain of just 57,000 — the smallest in four months — prompted markets to trim the probability of a September rate rise, lifting the euro to a two-week peak of $1.1442, sterling to $1.3361 and the yen off multi-decade lows to 161.01 per dollar (Reuters, 3 Jul 2026).

The slide interrupts a remarkable recovery. The dollar entered 2026 off a 9.4% annual decline, its worst in eight years (Bloomberg, 31 Dec 2025), and in January broke below 97 to touch 95.5, a four-year low (Bloomberg, 27 Jan 2026). Then came March: the US–Israel strikes on Iran and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz sent investors into the dollar as a haven, while the inflationary oil shock pushed Fed-cut expectations out of sight (Bloomberg, 2 Mar 2026).

That rebound rested on two supports — safe-haven demand and a hawkish Fed under Kevin Warsh, whose June dot plot showed half the committee projecting a 2026 hike. The jobs report has now weakened the second support. The next tests come quickly: the June FOMC minutes, July CPI in mid-month, and the Fed's 28–29 July meeting.

The Pattern

The dollar index has suffered five annual declines of roughly 9% or more since 1985. What happened next is the honest problem: the record splits almost down the middle between slides that extended and slides that snapped back.

2017
The index fell 9.9%, its worst year since 2003 — then reversed, gaining 4.4% in 2018 as the Fed kept hiking and US growth outran the world.
2003
Down 14.7%, the index's worst modern year — and the slide extended, with a further 7.0% decline in 2004 as the twin deficits dominated.
2002
The index dropped 12.8% as the post-dotcom easing cycle bit — and kept falling through 2003. Extension, not reversal.
1987
A fall of roughly 18% into the Louvre Accord era — followed by a 1988 rebound of about 8% as coordinated intervention and Fed tightening took hold.
1985
The Plaza Accord broke the super-dollar: down around 13% in 1985, and the decline ran straight through 1986. Extension.
3 of 5
Three of five 9%-plus down years extended into the following year; two reversed. The pattern is genuinely split — and 2026 has already tried both directions, a 95.5 low then a rebound above 100.

In three of five big down-years since 1985, the slide extended into year two; in two it reversed.

The Read

The weighted case is that the rebound was borrowed strength. Both of its props are wobbling: hike expectations retreated the moment payrolls printed 57,000, and safe-haven flows fade whenever the Gulf is quiet for a fortnight. Under the surface the 2025 story — fiscal worries, a 9.4% annual decline, foreign reserve diversification — never resolved, and three of five comparable down-years bled into a second. If July's data confirm a cooling economy, the January low becomes the magnet again.

The honest counter-case: this oil shock is dollar-positive in a way the old episodes were not, because the US is now a net energy exporter and the inflation it causes lands harder on Europe and Japan. The euro at $1.14 and the yen at 161 reflect exactly that. And the hawkish dot plot still stands — nine of 18 officials project a hike this year. One 4%-plus CPI print in mid-July would put the tightening trade, and the dollar, straight back on.
