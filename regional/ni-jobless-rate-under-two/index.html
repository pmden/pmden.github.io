Northern Ireland19 Aug 2026

Northern Ireland's jobless rate is under 2%. Four in ten adults are outside work.

The latest Labour Force Survey puts Northern Ireland's unemployment rate at 1.96 per cent and economic inactivity at 40.7 per cent.

4 min read

What happened

NISRA's Labour Market Report puts Northern Ireland unemployment at 1.96% for April to June 2026, with economic inactivity at 40.7% and 628,000 people economically inactive.

(NISRA, 18 Aug 2026)

The headline tables published by NISRA on 18 August 2026 record 18,000 people unemployed in Northern Ireland in the April to June quarter, an unemployment rate of 1.96 per cent, against 898,000 in employment and an employment rate of 58.2 per cent (NISRA headline tables, 18 Aug 2026). Unemployment is down 4,000 on the year. Economic inactivity is up 10,000 on the year, at 628,000 people and 40.7 per cent of those aged 16 and over (NISRA, 18 Aug 2026).

Those two movements are not independent of each other. Someone who stops looking for work leaves the unemployment count and joins the inactivity count, which lowers one headline and raises the other in the same step. The record high for inactivity in the seasonally adjusted series is 42.4 per cent in the March to May quarter of 2009, 1.7 percentage points above where it stands now (NISRA historical tables, table 2.1a).

The next reading is dated. NISRA's published schedule puts the Labour Market Report, with the monthly Labour Force Survey, the claimant count, redundancies and the HMRC payrolled employees figures, on 15 September 2026, with the following two on 20 October and 17 November (NISRA publication schedule, 2026). The September release will cover the May to July rolling quarter, which overlaps the current one by two months.

The Pattern

Action type: NISRA publishes a seasonally adjusted unemployment rate for those aged 16 and over below 2.0 per cent. The population is the whole published series from the January to March 1995 rolling quarter to April to June 2026, 376 observations, of which 17 are below the line and 16 have a successor. One test, fixed before the count and the same one the first market asks: was the next observation also below 2.0 per cent. No episodes are selected and no window is chosen; the last eight observations with a successor are shown.

Seven of the last eight sub-2 per cent readings were followed by another below the line; the full record is 14 of 16. The limitation is severe and has to be stated: rolling quarters overlap by two months, so consecutive observations share two thirds of their sample and are not independent trials. The number describes persistence in a smoothed series, which is real information, but it is not sixteen separate coin flips and should not be read as one.

7 of the last 8 sub-2 per cent observations were followed by another sub-2 per cent observation; 14 of all 16 were. The exceptions are August to October 2023, which was followed by 2.39 per cent, and February to April 2025, which was followed by 2.06 per cent. All 17 sub-2 readings in the series have come since July to September 2023; the record low is 1.55 per cent in August to October 2024.

What this pattern excludes

The claimant count series
It counts people receiving a benefit rather than people meeting the survey definition of unemployment, and it is classed as experimental. Two different definitions in one base rate would make the number unreadable.
The 16 to 64 table
It gives a different rate for the same population, 2.07 per cent for April to June 2026 against 1.96 per cent on the 16 and over basis. Mixing the two would let the answer depend on which table was open.
UK-wide comparisons
The question is what a Northern Ireland print does next, not how Northern Ireland compares. A UK series would answer a different question with a different mechanism behind it.

The Read

The unemployment market is priced off persistence rather than off any forecast. In a series where each observation shares two of its three months with the last one, a reading of 1.96 per cent does not jump to 2.3 per cent in a month without something breaking, and the published record says a sub-2 print is usually followed by another. What argues the other way is direction: the last three prints have moved up, from 1.78 to 1.85 to 1.96, and one more step of that size crosses the line, which is exactly how the February to April 2025 exception happened.

The inactivity market is the more interesting one and it sits low for a reason. The rate has not printed above 41 per cent at any point in the last year, though it came within a twentieth of a point in the December to February quarter, and the moves in this series are usually a tenth or two rather than a third. Read the two together and the picture is a labour market that is not producing unemployed people, it is producing people who have stopped counting as being in it. That is the number the Executive's economy department has to move, and it is the one no monthly release has moved yet.
