The market priced the rocket at $1.77 trillion. Now the rocket has to fly.

SpaceX became a public company on 12 June, raising $75 billion in the largest listing in history. Its first earnings-free test comes within weeks: Starship Flight 13.

4 min readEcho #116INTERNATIONAL
1 · Today

Two weeks after the record Nasdaq debut, short sellers are raising bets against SpaceX as the shares sell off from their first-day close.

Reuters, 24 Jun 2026
2 · The event

What happened

On 12 June SpaceX began trading on the Nasdaq, selling 555.6 million Class A shares at $135 apiece to raise $75 billion, the largest amount ever raised in a public listing, at a valuation of $1.77 trillion (BBC, 12 June 2026). The company marked the day in character, launching the Starlink 10-54 mission from Cape Canaveral the same morning, the 650th flight of a Falcon 9.

The listing lands mid-programme for Starship. Flight 12 in May was the first flight of the Version 3 vehicle (BBC, 12 June 2026), and Flight 13, flying new-build Booster 20 and Ship 40, is scheduled from Starbase in late July subject to final static fires and FAA sign-off (Reuters, 24 June 2026).

That makes this a listing unlike any before it. Public investors have never owned a company whose core development programme detonates or delivers on live video every few weeks. Every Starship milestone, and every anomaly, now reprices a trillion-dollar equity in real time.

The IPO run-up had its own subplot: in the weeks before listing, Musk posted about UK politics far more than about the company he was floating (The Guardian, 4 July 2026) — and within a fortnight of the debut his trillionaire status was gone as the stock came back to earth (The Guardian, 24 June 2026).

3 · The pattern

The pattern

The five largest listings before SpaceX offer a clean test of what happens in month one after a mega-IPO. The record leans one way, with a famous exception.

2021
Rivian raised $12 billion having barely delivered a vehicle. The stock nearly doubled within a week and was still comfortably above its $78 listing price a month later; the collapse below it came only in 2022.
2019
Saudi Aramco raised $29.4 billion, the previous record. It rose 10% on day one, touched a $2 trillion valuation on day two, and held above its listing price through its first month.
2014
Alibaba raised $25 billion and jumped 38% on day one. A month after the debut it still traded well above its $68 listing price.
2012
Facebook raised $16 billion at $38, and broke the pattern: within a month the shares had lost roughly a quarter of their value amid a botched opening day and mobile-revenue doubts.
2008
Visa raised $17.9 billion in the middle of the credit crisis, rose 28% on day one, and held the gain through month one even as banks fell around it.
4 of 5
Of the five largest IPOs before SpaceX, four traded above their listing price one month after debut. Facebook is the exception, and the warning: the misses come when a core doubt about the business surfaces immediately. For SpaceX, that doubt has a launch date.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case favours both a July-window launch and a firm stock. Ship 40's static fire campaign is under way, Starbase has flown from Pad 2 before, and SpaceX's cadence pressure is now shareholder pressure too. On price, the base rate is 4 of 5, the float is small relative to demand that produced $75 billion of orders, and index inclusion buying is still ahead. The 68% call on Flight 13 flying by 10 August roughly matches the programme's recent gap between static fire and launch.

The honest counter-case: Starship has never respected a schedule, and V3 is two flights old. A pad anomaly or an FAA hold pushes Flight 13 past 10 August without anything being wrong. And the Facebook precedent cuts here, because SpaceX is the first mega-listing whose central doubt can materialise on camera. A Flight 13 failure inside month one would test whether public markets price rapid iteration the way Elon Musk's private investors did.
