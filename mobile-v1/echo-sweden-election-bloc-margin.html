Sweden04 Sep 2026

Sweden's gap is down to three points. In five elections the government has survived twice.

Ulf Kristersson's right-wing bloc has closed to within about three points of Magdalena Andersson's opposition ten days before Sweden votes on 13 September. Since 2006 the sitting government has kept power at two of five elections, and both times by a handful of seats.

4 min readEcho #116INTERNATIONAL
1 · Today

Sweden votes for the Riksdag on 13 September, with early voting open since 26 August. The last polls before the vote put the four centre-left parties a little over three points ahead of Kristersson's government and the Sweden Democrats, the narrowest margin of the year.

Swedish Election Authority, 3 Sep 2026
2 · The event

What happened

Sweden's general election is on Sunday 13 September, when 349 seats in the Riksdag are decided alongside regional and municipal councils; advance voting has been open since 26 August (Swedish Election Authority, 2026). The contest is between the four-party Social Democrat-led opposition under former prime minister Magdalena Andersson and the three-party government of Ulf Kristersson, which has governed since 2022 with the support of the anti-immigration Sweden Democrats.

The polls have tightened sharply in the final fortnight. A Demoskop survey published on 3 September put the centre-left on 50.6% against 47.3% for the government and its support party, a gap of 3.3 points and the second-smallest of the year, down from 5.8 points in the previous survey; Bloomberg reported the day before that the opposition's lead had narrowed in the latest poll (Bloomberg, 2 Sep 2026). The same survey kept Kristersson's Liberal coalition partner at 2.8%, well below the 4% threshold a party needs to enter parliament.

The threshold is the mechanism that decides the outcome. If the Liberals fall short, their votes count for nothing in the seat allocation, which is why a three-point deficit in votes can translate into a gap of more than twenty seats. In 2022 the right bloc took 176 seats to 173 on a vote share margin of less than one point (Swedish Election Authority, 2022).

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Five Swedish governments have gone to the country since 2006. Two were returned, three were removed, and in every close case the seat count was decided by which small parties cleared the 4% line.

2022
September 2022: Magdalena Andersson conceded on 14 September once the count showed the right-wing bloc with 176 seats to 173, and Ulf Kristersson formed a government backed by the Sweden Democrats (Al Jazeera, 14 Sep 2022).
2018
September 2018: Stefan Lofven's centre-left bloc finished on 40.6% against 40.3% for the centre-right Alliance, a lead of 144 seats to 142. The government survived, but only after four months of negotiation (Al Jazeera, 10 Sep 2018).
2014
September 2014: the Social Democrats won 31.2% and Fredrik Reinfeldt's Moderates fell to 23.2%, from 30.1% four years earlier. Reinfeldt resigned as prime minister and party leader the same night (Al Jazeera, 15 Sep 2014).
2010
September 2010: Reinfeldt's four-party Alliance won 49.3% and 173 of 349 seats, two short of a majority, and stayed in office as the first non-socialist government re-elected in Swedish history (Al Jazeera, 20 Sep 2010).
2006
September 2006: Goran Persson conceded within three hours of polls closing as Reinfeldt's Alliance took about 48% of the vote, ending twelve years of Social Democrat government (Al Jazeera, 17 Sep 2006).
2 of 5
In 2 of the 5 Swedish elections since 2006 the sitting government kept power: Reinfeldt in 2010 and Lofven in 2018. Both survived by fewer than five seats. The three removals, in 2006, 2014 and 2022, each followed a clear vote-share swing against the government of at least three points.

Two of five governments returned since 2006, both by fewer than five seats.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case favours the opposition, but not by the margin the summer polls implied. Two of five governments have survived and both did so from a near-tie in votes; the current three-point deficit sits between those survivals and the clear defeats of 2006, 2014 and 2022. What moves the seat market is the Liberals: at 2.8% their votes evaporate, and the same Demoskop poll that showed a 3.3-point gap in votes translated it into 163 seats for the right against 186 for the left. That is why the bloc market prices the government's survival at 40% rather than closer to even.

The counter-case is momentum. Every survey of the final fortnight has moved the same way, and the Liberals crossed the threshold in 2022 having polled below it for most of that campaign. The prediction fails if tactical voting lifts the Liberals over 4%, at which point three points in votes becomes a genuine coin-flip in seats, or if the Sweden Democrats' vote is understated as it was in 2018 and 2022.
