July is the Treasury's payday. Healey just ran £1.8bn short.

Self-assessment receipts hit a July record and the government still borrowed £1.8bn in a month the OBR expected to end in surplus.

4 min read

What happened

The government ran an unexpected £1.8bn deficit in July, a month City economists had pencilled in as balanced, as spending growth outran record self-assessment receipts and John Healey prepared his first Budget for 28 October.

(The Guardian, 21 Aug 2026)

£1.8bn is the July shortfall, in a month that usually flatters the public finances: self-assessment income tax receipts came in at £17.1bn, £1.7bn up on July 2025 and the highest July figure since monthly records began in 1999, yet spending grew faster still (ONS, 21 Aug 2026). City economists had expected the month to balance, and the OBR's March forecast pencilled in a £0.5bn surplus, leaving the outturn £2.3bn adrift for the month alone (The Guardian, 21 Aug 2026).

£56.7bn is the cumulative April–July total — £6bn lower than the same point last year on stronger receipts, but £2.3bn above the OBR's profile for the year so far (ONS, 21 Aug 2026). Public sector net debt stands provisionally at £2,984.9bn, or 94.1% of GDP, and the ten-year gilt yield has held above 5% as energy-driven inflation keeps debt-refinancing costs elevated (The Guardian, 21 Aug 2026).

£23.6bn was the fiscal headroom left at the March statement; higher yields, slower growth and inflation are expected to have eaten a large share of it by Budget day, with WPI Strategy's Martin Beck noting that rising gilt yields feed through to the debt-interest bill only gradually — and in one direction (The Guardian, 21 Aug 2026). The chancellor's response so far is language: fiscal discipline, rules met in full, support for household costs. The numbers that define his first Budget arrive in two instalments — the August borrowing data on 22 September, and the OBR's verdict on 28 October.

The Pattern

Four times since 2008 a chancellor has walked into a set-piece fiscal event with borrowing running above forecast and the gilt market restive. Three of the four events raised taxes. The exception needed a slump.

2008
Darling's November pre-Budget report: with the 2009-10 borrowing forecast raised to £118bn, the answer was stimulus — VAT cut to 15% at a cost of roughly £12.5bn. The one giveaway in the series came in a full-blown recession.
2010
Osborne's June emergency Budget, inheriting a deficit near £156bn: VAT up to 20% and a consolidation plan building to about £40bn a year by 2014-15.
2022
Hunt's November Autumn Statement, weeks after the gilt crisis: a £55bn consolidation, roughly £25bn of it tax and £30bn spending restraint.
2024
Reeves's October Budget: with mid-year borrowing running £4.7bn above profile at the August release, the Budget raised taxes by £41.5bn a year by 2029-30, led by £25.7bn of employer national insurance.
3 of 4
Three of the four fiscal events since 2008 staged against above-forecast borrowing ended in net tax rises or consolidation: Osborne in 2010, Hunt in 2022, Reeves in 2024. The exception, Darling's 2008 stimulus, came in a full-blown slump. With July receipts at record levels and the pressure coming from spending and debt interest, 2026 resembles the majority cases.

The Read

£2.3bn over profile after four months is a small miss on £56.7bn, but every component points the same way: spending and debt interest are doing the damage while receipts perform, which is the hardest kind of overshoot to fix without a Budget measure. That is why the tax-rise market sits at 71% — the base rate is 3 of 4, the March headroom of £23.6bn is being eroded from three directions at once, and a chancellor promising fiscal discipline with ten-year gilts above 5% has few painless levers. The September release rarely reverses a four-month trend, hence 78% on the overshoot persisting.

£6bn lower than last year is the honest counter-case: this year's borrowing is falling, not rising, and July's record £17.1bn self-assessment haul shows the tax base holding up. Healey could plausibly reach 28 October leaning on spending restraint and let receipts close the gap, keeping net tax rises under the £10bn line. The gilt market is the swing factor in both directions — an easing of energy-driven inflation that pulls yields back below 5% would rebuild headroom without a single policy decision, which is why that market is priced at a genuine 40%, not a token hedge.
