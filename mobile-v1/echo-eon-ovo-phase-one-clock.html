E.ON moves on OVO. The last merger of this size took eight months.

The Competition and Markets Authority opened its formal Phase 1 inquiry into E.ON's purchase of OVO Energy on 2 September, with a statutory decision deadline of 28 October. That is Budget day, and it lands in the middle of a winter in which the price cap has just risen again.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The CMA launches its Phase 1 merger inquiry into the anticipated acquisition of OVO Energy Ltd by E.ON SE, with a statutory decision deadline of 28 October 2026.

GOV.UK, 2 Sep 2026
2 · The event

Fifty-six days, one statutory deadline

The CMA announced the launch of its merger inquiry by notice to the parties on 2 September 2026. The acquiring party is E.ON SE, acting through E.ON UK Limited, and the target is OVO Energy Ltd. The statutory deadline for the Phase 1 decision is 28 October 2026, and the authority notes that the deadline can be extended in limited circumstances (GOV.UK, 2 Sep 2026). The case was opened on 8 July and the invitation to comment ran from 8 to 23 July.

The commercial background is a retail market where the regulated price is rising rather than falling. Ofgem set the cap for October at £1,723 a year for a typical dual fuel household paying by direct debit, up 4 per cent from £1,663 for July to September and the highest in three years, covering around 22 million households; wholesale costs account for £812 of that bill and network costs £417 (Guardian, 26 Aug 2026).

Two things make the timing awkward rather than merely tight. The deadline falls on the same day as the autumn Budget, which is the noisiest day in the British policy calendar and a poor one on which to announce a contested decision about household bills. And a Phase 1 clearance in retail energy is a judgement about whether fewer large suppliers still leaves enough rivalry to matter when most customers sit on a capped tariff and do not switch.

3 · The pattern

What the competition authority has done with household energy mergers

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: this pattern counts every merger inquiry recorded on the CMA case finder since 2013 in which one party supplied gas or electricity to households in Great Britain and the transaction transferred that supply business or its customers. The test is single and stated in advance: was the merger cleared at Phase 1, or referred to a Phase 2 investigation. Network, generation and infrastructure deals are excluded, because they raise a different competition question. Cases still open are excluded, including the one that prompted this Echo.

2013
Telecom Plus plc and npower. Closed on 16 December 2013 with a Phase 1 clearance, the smaller of the transfers in the set and the least contested.
2018
SSE Retail and npower, a proposed combination of two of the then big six suppliers. Opened 28 February 2018 and closed on 10 October 2018 with a Phase 2 clearance. Referred to the longer investigation, and eight months from opening to close.
2019
OVO and SSE Retail. Opened 24 October 2019 and closed on 10 December 2019 with a Phase 1 clearance, forty-seven days from opening to decision.
2 of 3
2 of 3, and three is the floor of what counts as a base rate rather than an anecdote. Two further limits are worth stating: the three cases span thirteen years of a market that changed shape twice in that period, and the single Phase 2 case is the closest in size to the one now open, which is the wrong kind of company for a number this small.

2 of the 3 decided cases cleared at Phase 1. The one that did not was the one between two of the largest suppliers, and it took eight months.

What this pattern excludes

Iberdrola and North West Electricity Networks, opened 22 October 2024 and cleared at Phase 1 on 1 May 2025
A distribution network, not household supply. Network mergers are assessed against a regulated monopoly rather than against rivalry for customers, so the mechanism is different.
Generation and upstream deals such as IGas and Dart Energy
No household supply business changed hands, so the question the authority answers is not the question this Echo is about.
Supplier failures transferred under the supplier of last resort process
Those transfers were made by Ofgem under an emergency mechanism, not reviewed as mergers, so there is no Phase 1 or Phase 2 outcome to score.

5 · The read

The read

The fortnight market is close to a formality in the other direction. The authority has a 56-day statutory clock and no reason to use less than half of it on a deal between two suppliers with millions of household accounts, which is why the September market sits in single figures. Phase 1 decisions in this sector have come in at 47 days at the fast end, and that was for a smaller target in a market with more suppliers in it than there are now.

The Phase 2 market is the real one and the two arguments pull hard in opposite directions. Against a reference: the CMA cleared the last supply deal quickly, most households now sit under a regulated cap that limits how much a supplier can extract, and the authority has been under political instruction to weigh growth. For a reference: this is the biggest consolidation of household supply since SSE and npower, that one went to Phase 2, and a merger that reduces the number of large suppliers in a winter of rising bills is the sort of decision an authority prefers to take slowly and with a published case behind it. Watch the deadline extension market as the tell. Extensions are usually a sign that the parties are negotiating remedies, and remedies at Phase 1 mean no reference.
