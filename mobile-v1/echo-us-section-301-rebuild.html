Tariffs02 Jun 2026

The court killed the tariffs. Washington is rebuilding them.

After the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February, the US Trade Representative has proposed new duties of 10% to 12.5% on 60 economies under Section 301, citing forced labour. Hearings open on 7 July.

5 min readEcho #116INTERNATIONAL
1 · Today

USTR proposed additional duties of 10% to 12.5% on 60 economies after Section 301 forced-labour investigations, with written comments due 6 July and a public hearing on 7 July.

USTR, 2 Jun 2026
2 · The event

What happened

On 20 February the Supreme Court ruled 6-3, in an opinion by Chief Justice Roberts, that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorise the president to impose tariffs, affirming the invalidation of both the fentanyl-related duties on Canada, Mexico and China and the worldwide "reciprocal" tariffs (Supreme Court, 20 February 2026). The ruling left open the question of refunds on more than $200bn already collected, and stripped the administration's trade strategy of its central legal instrument overnight.

The rebuild came through older statutes. A temporary layer of duties under Section 122, a balance-of-payments authority capped at 150 days, is due to expire on 24 July. The durable replacement arrived on 2 June, when USTR announced findings in 60 parallel Section 301 investigations into forced-labour policies and proposed additional tariffs: 10% for economies that ban forced-labour imports, 12.5% for the rest, China among the 46 economies facing the higher rate (USTR, 2 June 2026). Written comments closed on 6 July and a public hearing follows on 7 July.

Beijing's posture complicates the timetable. China has signalled it wants to extend the one-year trade truce agreed last autumn, and has indicated it would accept restoration of tariffs through the Section 301 route provided rates go no higher than before (Bloomberg, 20 May 2026). The truce runs to November, but the shape of the 301 action will decide whether it survives that long.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Section 301 proposals are not trial balloons. Since 2018, every proposed action has produced a final one. But the hearing stage matters: final measures have repeatedly been trimmed, delayed or restructured between the Federal Register notice and the duty actually landing. Four completed 301 actions set the base rate.

2025
The shipbuilding and maritime 301 proposed port fees of up to $1.5m per vessel call in February; the April final action was substantially softened after industry hearings, with fees restructured by tonnage and carve-outs for shorter routes.
2024
The four-year review proposed 100% tariffs on Chinese EVs and 50% on semiconductors in May; the September final determination kept headline rates but shifted timelines and granted exclusions after the comment period.
2019
List 4 was announced at 10%, raised to 15%, then split: List 4A took effect on 1 September, List 4B was cancelled before its December deadline, and 4A was halved to 7.5% under the Phase One deal.
2018
The original China 301 went from proposal in April to List 1 duties of 25% on $34bn of goods in force on 6 July, roughly three months, essentially as proposed.
4 of 4
Every Section 301 proposal since 2018 became a final action, so the direction is not in doubt. The split is on shape and speed: three of the four were softened, restructured or rescheduled between proposal and final duty, and the median gap from proposal to action ran about three months.

Four of four Section 301 proposals reached final action, but in three of the four the final measure differed materially from what was proposed.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case says the tariffs return, just not by 10 August. All four prior 301 proposals became final action, and the administration has an unusually hard deadline pushing it forward: the Section 122 stopgap dies on 24 July, and a White House that spent 2025 building leverage will not readily let its baseline tariff lapse to zero. But the median proposal-to-action gap is around three months, the hearing only opens on 7 July, and 60 parallel investigations is an unprecedented administrative load. A final determination inside five weeks would be the fastest major 301 action on record, which is why the YES price sits below a third.

The counter-case is that this administration treats process speed as a virtue and has the strongest possible incentive to compress it. The 24 July cliff could produce either the fastest 301 finalisation ever or a creative stopgap, and the pattern warns against assuming the proposal survives contact with the hearing intact: China's stated red line on rates, and the EU's presence on the 60-economy list three weeks after its trade deal took effect, are exactly the pressures that trimmed the 2019 and 2025 actions. Direction near-certain, timing and shape genuinely open.
