Twenty-two governments called E1 unacceptable. Burnham's measures come next.

Israel put more than 1,200 settlement homes out to tender in the E1 corridor. Britain signed a joint statement calling the plan unacceptable, and the Prime Minister is now expected to approve a ban on settlement goods.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Al Jazeera reports that Prime Minister Andy Burnham is expected to approve a ban on goods linked to Israeli settlements, after Israel issued tenders for more than 1,200 homes in the E1 area of the occupied West Bank.

Al Jazeera, 27 Aug 2026
2 · The event

What happened

On 20 August the United Kingdom signed a joint leaders' statement calling Israel's decision to publish construction tenders for E1 unacceptable, warning that the scheme would drive a wedge through the West Bank. Signatories were added between 20 and 27 August until 22 governments and the European Commission had put their names to it (GOV.UK, 20 Aug 2026). The statement warned businesses that bidding for the contracts carried legal and reputational consequences. It set no deadline and imposed nothing.

Al Jazeera reported on 27 August that Burnham is expected to approve a ban on goods produced in Israeli settlements, after the Foreign Secretary said on 19 August that the government would set out a comprehensive set of measures in the coming weeks. YouGov polling cited in the same report put support for a ban at 50% against 16% opposed, and more than 140 Labour MPs back a trade ban. Officials said they do not comment on speculation (Al Jazeera, 27 Aug 2026).

Britain already has a stack of measures short of trade. Around 30 of 350 arms export licences were suspended in September 2024 (GOV.UK, 2 Sep 2024), two serving Israeli ministers were sanctioned in June 2025, and the UK formally recognised the State of Palestine in September 2025. A trade ban would be the first British measure to touch commerce with the settlements themselves.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

British foreign secretaries have promised measures on Israel three times since taking office in 2024. Each time something landed, and landed quickly. The one demand that keeps being raised and keeps not arriving is the trade ban now under discussion.

2024
On 2 September the government suspended around 30 of 350 arms export licences to Israel after a review of compliance with international humanitarian law, exempting the F-35 programme (GOV.UK, 2 Sep 2024).
2025
On 10 June the UK sanctioned Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich with asset freezes and travel bans, acting with Australia, Canada, New Zealand and Norway weeks after suspending trade talks with Israel (GOV.UK, 10 Jun 2025).
2025
The Prime Minister said on 29 July that Britain would recognise a Palestinian state unless conditions were met (GOV.UK, 29 Jul 2025). Recognition followed on 21 September, about eight weeks later (GOV.UK, Sep 2025).
2026
A ban on trade with the settlements has been urged by Labour backbenchers and campaign groups throughout, and has never been enacted. The E1 statement of 20 August again warned companies off the contracts without prohibiting anything (GOV.UK, 20 Aug 2026).
3 of 4
Three of the four British commitments on Israel since September 2024 produced a named measure within roughly eight weeks: arms licences, ministerial sanctions and recognition. The fourth, a ban on trade with the settlements, has been demanded since 2024 and has never been imposed. That is the split: this government moves fast on sanctions and status, and has so far refused to move on commerce.

3 of 4 British pledges on Israel since 2024 produced a concrete measure within roughly eight weeks; the settlement trade ban is the one that has never come.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case is that something lands, and that it is smaller than a full import ban. The base rate is strong on delivery and silent on scope: every pledge since 2024 has produced a measure, but each one has been an instrument the Foreign Office already owns, applied to a named list. Sanctions designations and licence suspensions can be signed off inside the department. An import prohibition needs trade law, customs rules and a settled definition of what counts as settlement produce, and it would be the first British restriction on commerce with any Israeli entity. Fifty per cent public support and 140 Labour backbenchers give the political cover; the machinery is the constraint.

The honest counter is that the E1 statement is a warning to business rather than a policy, and warnings have been the ceiling before. The government has declined to comment on the reports, Labour Friends of Israel has argued a ban would be impossible to implement, and the Foreign Secretary's own formulation was a set of measures in the coming weeks, not a ban. If the package arrives as further designations plus procurement guidance, the headline market resolves NO while the pattern still holds. The prediction fails outright if the Israeli government withdraws the E1 tenders and the pressure evaporates.
