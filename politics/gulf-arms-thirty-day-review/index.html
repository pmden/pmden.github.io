US arms sales18 Sep 2026

US senators move to block a Saudi bomb sale. Will the Senate vote by 15 October?

The State Department approved 48 F-35 jets for Saudi Arabia on Thursday. A separate Saudi bomb sale is already inside its 30 days in Congress.

5 min read

What happened

The Trump administration says it has approved the sale of nearly 50 F-35 fighter jets to Saudi Arabia, and Congress has 30 days to scrutinise or attempt to block the 24 billion dollar deal. (BBC News)

The State Department said on Thursday that it had cleared the sale of 48 F-35 jets to Saudi Arabia for about 24.3 billion dollars. It called Saudi Arabia a major non-NATO ally and said the sale would not change the military balance in the region. (BBC News)

Congress now has 30 days to look at the deal or try to block it. Within a day a Democrat on the House Intelligence Committee came out against the sale, saying it could put American stealth technology within reach of China. (BBC News)

Israel is the only other country in the region that flies the aircraft.

The jet sale has no public start date for its 30 days. The formal notice of the sale to Congress is not on the public record. It is not in the Congressional Record for 14 to 17 September or in the Federal Register, so there is no date to count 30 days from. (Al Jazeera)

A second Saudi sale does have a date. A formal notice, called a transmittal, for 10,004 bomb guidance kits and 10,004 general purpose bombs was printed in the Congressional Record on 15 September 2026. It is transmittal 26-35, sent under section 36(b)(1) of the Arms Export Control Act. (Text of S.J.Res.216, govinfo)

On 16 September the senators Ed Markey, Bernie Sanders and Jeff Merkley filed a resolution, S.J.Res.216, to stop that sale. Counting 30 days from 15 September puts the end of the review on 15 October. (Text of S.J.Res.216, govinfo)

Any vote in this window will be in the Senate. The House has left Washington, and its leader’s calendar shows no working days before 9 November. The midterm elections are six weeks away.

The Pattern

We looked at Gulf arms sales where Congress was formally told of the sale and a senator filed a resolution to block it. We counted the days from that notice to a recorded vote in the Senate. We found eleven since 2016, and five of them got a vote within the 30 days.

5 of 11
Five of the last eleven Gulf arms notifications that drew a disapproval resolution reached a recorded Senate vote inside the 30-day review. The distribution sits on the deadline rather than scattering around it: seven of the eleven land between day 25 and day 35, then a gap of six days, then a second group at 41, 44 and 48 where the sale had already gone through before the chamber voted, and one outlier at 77 days where a Qatari package was bolted onto somebody else's vote. The reason for the clustering is procedural. A joint resolution of disapproval is privileged once a notification is transmitted, so a single senator can force the chamber onto the record, and the incentive is to do it before the sale clears rather than after.

The Read

Start with the clock that can be counted. No formal notice for the F-35 jets has been published, so there is no 30-day period for them yet. The bomb kit sale does have one. Its notice was printed in the Congressional Record on 15 September, and the resolution against it names that date in its own text.

History says a vote by 15 October is possible but a little less likely than not. The deadline, more than the argument, decides when the Senate votes. Seven of the last eleven Gulf sales got a Senate vote between day 25 and day 35, and five of those came inside the 30 days. The reason is a rule: once Congress is told of a sale, a resolution against it gets a fast track, so one senator can force a vote even if party leaders do not want one. The votes bunch on the deadline or come after the sale has already gone through.

Two things could break the pattern this time, and neither is about Saudi Arabia. The House is away until 9 November, so any vote would be a Senate vote taken for the record, six weeks before the midterms. The exact deadline is also up to three days unclear, because the notice reaches Congress before the Congressional Record prints it, so the real end of the review may come slightly before 15 October. We could not check the Senate’s sitting calendar for October, and that is the biggest risk to this question.
