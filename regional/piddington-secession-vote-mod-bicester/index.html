Asylum accommodation15 Sep 2026

Village votes to leave UK over asylum site. 2 of 8 such councils sued in a month.

Piddington, 400 people near Bicester, voted on 15 September on leaving the UK. It protests a Home Office plan for 1,256 male asylum seekers next door.

4 min read

What happened

Residents of Piddington, a village of about 400 people near Bicester in Oxfordshire, voted on Tuesday 15 September in a symbolic referendum organised by the parish council on whether the village should remain part of the UK, in protest at a Home Office plan to house up to 1,256 male asylum seekers at the nearby MoD Bicester site.

(BBC 15 Sep)

Piddington's parish council ran a symbolic vote on 15 September asking its roughly 400 residents whether the village should stay in the United Kingdom. The vote has no legal effect. It was agreed on 4 July, when 96 per cent of villagers backed holding it, and it answers a Home Office plan announced on 25 June to turn MoD Bicester, half a mile away, into basic accommodation for up to 1,256 men. The Home Office lodged an Urgent Crown Development application on 2 September and public comments close at midnight on 17 September. (BBC 15 Sep; Independent 15 Sep; BBC 2 Sep; GOV.UK 2 Sep)

The village has pulled Westminster in. On 14 September shadow home secretary Chris Philp, shadow minister Joy Morrissey and Reform UK MPs Sarah Pochin and Suella Braverman met about 20 women on the green, after 133 women of Piddington wrote to all 266 female MPs saying they already lived in fear. Cherwell District Council, whose leader Lesley McLean called the plan arbitrary in June, meets in planning committee at 4pm on 16 September to agree a response that lists transport, services, drainage and security problems but does not mention legal action. A residents' group opened a £20,000 fund on 3 September for advice on a possible court challenge. (BBC 15 Sep; Cherwell DC 11 Sep; BBC 27 Jun; Oxford Mail 3 Sep)

The decision now sits with the Secretary of State for Housing, Angela Rayner, not the council, and the applicant is the Home Office under Shabana Mahmood. Asylum minister Anna Turley said on 12 August that the government would not drop the military-site plan. A letter to service personnel at the base put a provisional 270 asylum seekers on site by the end of the year. The same field has been here before: Labour named it for an asylum centre in 2002, Cherwell fought the planning permission to the Court of Appeal, and the scheme was scrapped in June 2005 at a cost of £28m. (GOV.UK ministers; BBC 12 Aug; BBC 3 Jul; BBC 8 Nov 2007)

The Pattern

Every Home Office plan since 2020 to house asylum seekers on a large ex-military or similar site where the district council or residents formally mobilised against it, dated from the day the Home Office publicly confirmed the site. Held means a council or residents' court claim, a judicial review or an injunction application, was lodged within 31 days of that date. Newest first. The two June 2026 sister sites at Barnham and Linton-on-Ouse are left out because their 90-day windows have not closed.

The Read

Piddington needs a hearing from a department that chose a route with no local vote, so the parish council built a vote with a date, set for the day before the consultation closes, to bring cameras and MPs to a village of 400. The Home Office needs beds outside hotels: 16,021 people were in hotels in June 2026, the pledge is to close every one by 2029, and the urgent Crown route puts the decision with Angela Rayner's department on its own clock. Three players hold a move before 16 October: Cherwell District Council, whose committee on 16 September talks about drainage rather than lawyers; the Secretary of State, who can grant, refuse or wait; and Chris Philp and Suella Braverman, for whom Piddington is the autumn's asylum symbol. Calum Miller, the Liberal Democrat MP, has a petition and a consultation win and nothing to lose by escalating.

The weighted case is that the vote changes the headlines and not the court list. The two councils that sued did so within days of the announcement in 2023 and both lost within weeks; Bicester is 82 days past its announcement with a planning objection and a £20,000 advice fund, which is the Northeye and Cameron Barracks profile, not the Scampton one. A claim would most naturally attack the Secretary of State's decision, and the only comparable urgent Home Office application, Manston, took 128 days to decide, which pushes both the decision and any challenge to it past 16 October; the court-claim market opens at 20 and the approval market at 18. The arrivals market sits at 22 because only two of eight sites opened within 90 days, this one has no permission, no water and no power yet, and the 270-by-Christmas figure is the Home Office's own provisional line.

The honest counter-case is that this government moves faster than the record shows. Crowborough went from confirmation to first arrivals in 86 days under emergency powers with a claim already lodged, and Manston was approved without a public representation stage. The court-claim forecast fails if Cherwell's committee on 16 September turns its planning objections into an instruction to lodge a claim, or if the residents' group files against the process itself before a decision, as Crowborough Shield did. The approval forecast fails if the Secretary of State decides inside four weeks of the 17 September deadline; the arrivals forecast fails if permission comes in October and the Home Office uses modular units and tankered utilities to meet its own 270 figure.
