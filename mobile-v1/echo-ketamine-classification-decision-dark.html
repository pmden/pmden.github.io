Seizures at a 15-year high. The classification call still waits.

A record 1,298kg of ketamine was seized in the first quarter of 2026. Seven months after advisers said it should stay Class B, the Home Office has not replied.

4 min read

A record quarter, and an unanswered letter

Border Force seizures of ketamine reach 1,298kg in the first quarter of 2026, a 42% rise on the previous quarter and the highest quarterly total in 15 years.

(GOV.UK, 28 Aug 2026)

The Home Office published Border Force seizure figures on 28 August 2026 showing 1,298kg of ketamine intercepted between January and March, up 42 per cent on the 915kg taken in the final quarter of 2025 and ahead of the previous high of 1,238kg in the second quarter of 2025 (GOV.UK, 28 Aug 2026). Harwich International Port alone accounted for 300kg over the year. The release is an enforcement announcement and says nothing about classification.

The classification question is nonetheless open. The Advisory Council on the Misuse of Drugs, commissioned by the Home Office in January 2025 to review whether ketamine should move from Class B to Class A, reported on 28 January 2026 that it should remain Class B (ACMD, 28 Jan 2026). The council recorded that this was a majority rather than a unanimous view, and that its working group and stakeholders agreed reclassification on its own would do little to change prevalence or harm. It made 14 other recommendations covering testing, treatment and research.

The mechanics leave the decision entirely with ministers. Under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 the Home Secretary must consult the ACMD before changing a drug's class, but is not bound by what it says, and no statute sets a period within which the government must reply. There is no default outcome either: if nothing is done, ketamine stays Class B by inaction rather than by decision. That is why the elapsed time, now seven months, is the variable rather than any scheduled date.

The Pattern

Inclusion rule, fixed before the decision: this pattern counts every occasion since 2002 on which the ACMD formally advised the Home Secretary against placing a drug in a higher class, whether by recommending no change or by recommending a lower class, and where a ministerial decision followed. Advice recommending tighter control is excluded, because there the minister and the adviser already want the same thing and the case tests nothing. Scheduling decisions under the Misuse of Drugs Regulations are excluded, because they use a different instrument from classification.

2004
The ACMD recommended in 2002 that cannabis move from Class B to Class C. The Home Secretary agreed and the change took effect on 29 January 2004. Advice followed.
2009
The ACMD advised in 2005 and again in 2008 that cannabis should stay in Class C. The government moved it back to Class B in 2009. Advice overridden.
2009
The ACMD recommended moving MDMA from Class A to Class B. The government declined and MDMA remains Class A. Advice overridden.
2014
The ACMD concluded the evidence of harm from khat was insufficient to justify control at all. Khat was brought under control as a Class C drug. Advice overridden.
2023
The ACMD advised against controlling nitrous oxide under the Misuse of Drugs Act. It was controlled as a Class C drug. Advice overridden.
4 of 5
4 of 5 since 2002. All four overrides moved in the direction of more control, not less, which is the direction the pressure now points on ketamine. Five cases is a small base and the interval around it is wide, which is why the fraction is published rather than a percentage.

In 4 of the 5 cases since 2002 where the ACMD advised against a higher classification, the Home Secretary of the day went the other way.

What this pattern excludes

Ketamine moved from Class C to Class B in 2014, and GHB and GBL moved to Class B in 2022
In both the ACMD recommended tighter control and ministers agreed, so the case cannot test whether ministers accept advice they dislike.
Generic scheduling of novel psychoactive substances
High-volume technical orders where the Home Office and the ACMD are not in tension, so including them would flood the set with cases that carry no information.
Rescheduling of cannabis-based medicinal products in 2018
A change to the Misuse of Drugs Regulations governing lawful medical access rather than a change of class under the 1971 Act, so it uses a different instrument.

The Read

The weighted case is delay rather than reversal. Nothing forces a reply, silence keeps ketamine in Class B without ministers having to defend either course, and the government has already chosen a public health campaign as its visible response. The seizure figures cut both ways: a record quarter can be presented as enforcement working, which is how the 28 August release frames it, or as evidence that supply is growing, which is the argument for Class A. On the record, when a Home Secretary does move on this kind of advice, the move is usually against it.

The counter-case is that the advice itself was split. The ACMD recorded a majority rather than a unanimous recommendation, which lowers the political cost of overruling it, and the council's own reasoning conceded that classification is not the main lever on prevalence. That concession is the argument a minister would use. What would invalidate the reading here is a formal response accepting Class B on the record: that would be only the second time since 2002 that the advisers were followed when they counselled against tightening, and it would say the base rate has stopped holding.
