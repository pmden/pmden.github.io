The Met goes static. The watchdogs go to work.

Sir Mark Rowley has announced fixed live facial recognition cameras on London's street furniture and drone coverage of every borough by June 2027. Civil liberties groups say the rollout is spiralling out of control.

4 min readEcho #116LONDON
1 · Today

Rowley announced static LFR cameras on street infrastructure and drone coverage of all London boroughs by June 2027, warning that crime is "evolving at speed".

Metropolitan Police, 24 Jun 2026
2 · The event

What happened

Metropolitan Police Commissioner Sir Mark Rowley used a speech on 24 June to set out the next phase of the Met's technology strategy: "static" live facial recognition cameras fixed to street infrastructure and monitored remotely, repositionable to high-crime areas, with the rollout continuing into next year (Metropolitan Police, 24 June 2026).

Rowley said LFR has led to more than 2,000 arrests since 2024, and announced an ambition for police drone coverage across all 32 boroughs by June 2027, built with London's other blue-light services into a city-wide emergency drone network. He argued that criminals are going digital and called for lighter regulation before deployment, warning that forces risk being outwitted by AI-enabled crime (Metropolitan Police, 24 June 2026).

The reaction was immediate. Big Brother Watch called facial recognition "intrusive", "discriminatory" and "undemocratic", and said its use is "spiralling out of control"; director Silkie Carlo described police drones overseeing public behaviour as excessive and counterproductive. With no dedicated statute governing LFR, the contest over the rollout now moves to City Hall scrutiny, Whitehall and — potentially — the courts.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

A decade of UK police facial recognition has one consistent theme: expansion announced, objection raised, expansion delivered anyway. Only once has a challenge actually altered course — and even then, only briefly.

2025
The Met announced London's first fixed LFR cameras for Croydon town centre; despite objections from privacy campaigners, the cameras went up during the summer.
2024
The Met sharply scaled up van-based LFR deployments across London — the expansion that produced the bulk of the 2,000+ arrests Rowley now cites.
2020
The Court of Appeal ruled South Wales Police's LFR use unlawful in Bridges — the only successful challenge to date. The force paused, rewrote its policies, and resumed.
2016
The Met's first LFR trial at Notting Hill Carnival went ahead despite warnings from civil liberties groups; trials continued in the years that followed and hardened into routine deployment.
3 of 4
Three of four major UK police facial recognition expansions since 2016 went ahead without alteration despite public challenge. The exception, Bridges in 2020, forced South Wales Police to pause and revise — and then continue. Challenges slow this technology; they have never yet stopped it.

3 of 4 major LFR expansions proceeded unaltered; one court defeat forced a pause and a rewrite, not a stop.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case favours the rollout proceeding untouched through the summer. The base rate is emphatic — objections have never stopped a UK LFR expansion — and the politics currently run the Met's way: 2,000 arrests is a potent number, ministers are demanding visible action on crime, and Rowley is asking for less regulation at a moment when nobody in government is offering more. The 62% on "neither" reflects a decade of precedent. New camera sites by early August are less likely: Croydon's fixed cameras took months from announcement to installation, and 31% prices that machinery honestly.

The counter-case is that static cameras are a different legal object from vans. A van deployment is temporary and targeted; a permanent camera network watching passers-by is closer to the indiscriminate processing that sank South Wales Police in Bridges. Big Brother Watch has litigated before and its language — "spiralling out of control" — is the register of an organisation building a case, not just a press line. One filed claim before 7 August flips the multi, and a 24% price on a well-resourced, motivated litigant with a fresh target is far from nothing.
