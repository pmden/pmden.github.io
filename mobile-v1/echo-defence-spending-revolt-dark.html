Defence11 Jun 2026

Two ministers walked over 2.68%. The number outlived the government.

Defence Secretary John Healey and Armed Forces Minister Al Carns resigned on 11 June, accusing Downing Street of refusing to fund defence at a time of rising threats. The prime minister they defied is now gone — the 2.68% settlement is still on the table.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Healey resigned with a letter accusing Starmer and the Treasury of being 'unable, and unwilling, to commit the resources that the nation needs to defend the country at this time of rising threats.'

Al Jazeera, 11 Jun 2026
2 · The event

The 2.68% resignation

John Healey resigned as Defence Secretary on 11 June, writing to Keir Starmer that the financial settlement he had received on the Monday fell well short of what defence required, rising only to 2.68% of GDP. He cited the Middle East conflict, Britain's lead of the multinational Strait of Hormuz mission and increased Russian activity as evidence the plan 'could make the country less safe' (Al Jazeera, 11 Jun 2026; The Guardian, 11 Jun 2026).

Armed Forces Minister Al Carns, a decorated former Royal Marine, resigned six hours later on the same grounds — the seventh minister to leave Starmer's government in a month. The Ministry of Defence and the Treasury had been deadlocked for months over the Defence Investment Plan, already delayed from the previous year (BBC News, 11 Jun 2026). Starmer appointed former soldier Dan Jarvis as Healey's replacement two days later.

The revolt did not stay contained. Within eleven days, Starmer had announced his own resignation amid the wider leadership crisis, leaving the 2.68% settlement as unfinished business for whoever takes over. The Defence Investment Plan remains unpublished, and the incoming leadership inherits both the number and the officers' expectations that it will rise.

3 · The pattern

Do spending resignations move the money?

Ministers resigning over defence and spending settlements is a British tradition with a consistent ending: the resignation makes the front pages, and the number survives. In four comparable cases across seventy years, not one settlement was reversed in the aftermath.

1986
Michael Heseltine walked out of Cabinet over the Westland affair, the sharpest defence-industrial resignation of the Thatcher years. The Westland decision stood; Thatcher's line held completely.
1981
Navy minister Keith Speed was dismissed after publicly opposing John Nott's naval cuts. The cuts proceeded — only the Falklands War in 1982 forced a partial reversal, a year later and by events, not by the resignation.
1966
Navy minister Christopher Mayhew resigned alongside the First Sea Lord over the cancellation of the CVA-01 aircraft carrier. The carrier stayed cancelled; the East of Suez retreat continued.
1958
Chancellor Peter Thorneycroft and his entire Treasury ministerial team — including Enoch Powell — resigned over £50 million in spending estimates. Macmillan dismissed it as 'little local difficulties' and the estimates stood.
0 of 4
In four comparable resignations over defence and spending settlements, zero produced a near-term reversal of the number. The one partial reversal (1982) took a war. Against that: none of those resignations brought down a prime minister within eleven days.

5 · The read

How to read it

The case for the number moving: this is not 1958. The resignations helped bring down the prime minister within eleven days, and the incoming leadership — with Andy Burnham the overwhelming favourite — needs an early, visible break from the Starmer settlement. Defence is the cheapest reset available rhetorically: a new prime minister can announce an 'above 2.68%' ambition in a first-week speech without a single pound moving before the autumn budget. Healey and Carns, both still on the benches, are ready-made validators for exactly that announcement.

The case against is the whole historical record plus the calendar. Zero of four comparable revolts moved the settlement, the Treasury's arithmetic has not changed, and a leadership handover consumes precisely the weeks this market covers — nominations close 16 July, and a contested race would leave a caretaker government until 29 August with no authority to rewrite fiscal plans. 'Still unpublished' is not a bold call; it is the default state of a plan already delayed a year and now owned by nobody.
