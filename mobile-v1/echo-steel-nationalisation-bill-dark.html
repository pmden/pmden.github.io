Steel heads back into public hands. The Lords want a price tag.

The Steel Industry (Nationalisation) Bill cleared Lords committee unamended on 1 July, brushing aside a Conservative £2.5 billion spending cap and a Liberal Democrat demand for parliamentary sign-off. Report stage opens on 8 July, and the government wants Royal Assent before the summer recess.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The Steel Industry (Nationalisation) Bill clears committee stage in the House of Lords without amendment, as peers warn against running the industry on a blank cheque.

The Irish News (PA), 1 Jul 2026
2 · The event

What happened

The Steel Industry (Nationalisation) Bill would let the government transfer the shares or property of a steel company into public ownership where doing so is in the public interest. It is the end-game of a rescue that began in April 2025, when ministers used the Steel Industry (Special Measures) Act to direct British Steel's operations and keep the Scunthorpe blast furnaces lit; roughly £484 million in support has followed since, and in May 2026 the government announced it was minded to nationalise the company outright House of Lords Library, Jun 2026. The bill completed its Commons stages on 9 June and had its Lords second reading on 16 June.

Committee stage, on 29 June and 1 July, was where the price question surfaced. Lord Sharpe of Epsom proposed capping support at £2.5 billion until August 2029, arguing that the government's stated objective 'is not permanent public subsidy'. Lord Fox, for the Liberal Democrats, wanted any financial assistance approved by both Houses, with a 90-day pause after select committees receive the details. Business minister Lord Leong rejected both, saying fixed caps would 'risk constraining the Government's ability to respond effectively', and the bill left committee unchanged The Irish News, 1 Jul 2026.

Report stage opens on Wednesday 8 July, and this time amendments will be pushed to votes. Peers have tabled demands for a statement of contingent liabilities — environmental, pension and operational — a report on nationalisation's effect on inward investment, and a level playing field between publicly and privately owned steel businesses UK Parliament, Jul 2026. Any amendment carried sends the bill back to the Commons, with the summer recess closing in.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Westminster has legislated to take steel into public hands three times in eighty years, and the legislation has never failed. What the record also shows is that ownership of British steel is the least settled question in the industrial politics of the state: the first two nationalisations were both reversed by the next government of the opposite colour.

2025
Parliament was recalled on a Saturday — the first weekend sitting since the Falklands — and passed the Steel Industry (Special Measures) Act through all stages in a single day, handing ministers control of British Steel's Scunthorpe operations.
2021
The Ministry of Defence bought Sheffield Forgemasters for £2.56 million, committing up to £400 million over ten years — a quiet nationalisation of defence-critical steel that passed with barely a division.
1988
The Thatcher government privatised British Steel, raising about £2.5 billion and closing the second nationalised era after 21 years.
1967
Wilson's Iron and Steel Act renationalised the 14 largest producers — around 90 per cent of UK steelmaking — creating the British Steel Corporation.
1951
The Attlee government's Iron and Steel Act 1949 vested the industry in public ownership in February 1951; the incoming Conservative government began selling it back within two years.
3 of 3
Every time a British government has put steel nationalisation to Parliament — 1949, 1967, 2025 — the legislation has passed. That is 3 of 3 on passage. But the same record splits on what follows: two of those three settlements were unwound by a successor government. The near-term forecast is about the bill; the honest long-term base rate is about reversal.

Three steel nationalisations legislated; three passed. Two of the first two were reversed. The bills get through — permanence is the part history refuses to guarantee.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case: assent before recess. Steel legislation has passed three times out of three, and this bill carries less friction than any of its predecessors — the rescue has been running since April 2025, the principle of intervention was conceded when Parliament sat on a Saturday to keep Scunthorpe's furnaces lit, and the opposition's committee amendments were about the invoice, not the ownership. The Lords amends government bills; it does not, by convention, block them. Even a report-stage defeat on contingent liabilities is the kind of amendment a government accepts or reverses in a single Commons sitting, and there are two full sitting weeks to do it in.

The counter-case is the calendar, not the principle. A defeat on 8 July starts ping-pong with perhaps eight sitting days left, against a frontbench operating through a Labour leadership transition with limited bandwidth for a Lords standoff. The Sharpe cap and the Fox approval mechanism both have cross-bench appeal precisely because 'no blank cheque' is not a partisan position, and peers who withdrew in committee did so to fight at report, where conventions on restraint are weaker. If two or more amendments carry, the government must choose between swallowing conditions on its own chequebook and letting assent slip to September — and a bill delayed past recess is a bill renegotiated. The 78 on assent is a forecast about whips' arithmetic; the 56 on a report-stage defeat is the honest reading of how the Lords treats spending powers it cannot see the bottom of.
