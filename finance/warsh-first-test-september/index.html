Every new Fed chair gets tested. Warsh's test is live.

Kevin Warsh heads to his first Jackson Hole symposium as Federal Reserve chair with three colleagues pushing for rate rises and inflation at 3.4%.

4 min read

What happened

Minutes of the Fed's July meeting, released on 19 August, revealed internal disagreement: the committee held rates at 3.5–3.75% on a 9–3 vote, with three members — Hammack, Kashkari and Logan — formally dissenting in favour of an immediate quarter-point rise, while the minutes noted that tightening would likely be necessary if it persists.

(Federal Reserve / The Guardian, 19 Aug 2026)

Markets have probed every incoming Federal Reserve chair since Paul Volcker, and this week they started on Kevin Warsh. Minutes of the July meeting, released on 19 August, showed the committee holding rates at 3.5–3.75% while three members argued for increases if inflation, at 3.4% in July after 4.2% in May, fails to head towards the 2% target (Federal Reserve, 19 Aug 2026). The minutes landed in the middle of a bond selloff that has pushed 10- and 20-year Treasury yields to 20-year highs and the 30-year to its highest since 2007 (The Guardian, 19 Aug 2026).

Warsh, chair since May, has made silence a policy. He has scrapped the dot plot and forward guidance, assembled a 15-strong panel including former Bank of England governor Mervyn King to review how the Fed communicates, and declined to indicate a direction for rates; former BoE deputy governor Charlie Bean has observed that he is offering neither a rate path nor a reaction function (The Guardian, 16 Aug 2026). As global yields climbed this week, that reticence itself drew criticism, with the chair faulted for withholding guidance during the rout (The Guardian, 20 Aug 2026).

The stage for his answer is set. The Kansas City Fed's Jackson Hole symposium runs from 27 to 29 August under the theme of financial innovation and its implications for payments and policy, and it will be Warsh's first appearance there as chair (Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City, 2026). Markets currently expect a hold at the 15–16 September meeting, with any move to a 4–4.25% range priced for 2027 (The Guardian, 16 Aug 2026).

The Pattern

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: every Federal Reserve chair since 1979 who met a market test in his first year in office, the position Warsh is in with three dissenters and a bond rout running. The test is single: at the next scheduled policy decision, did the new chair tighten or stand firm rather than ease. Four cases meet the rule and all four are shown; Warsh is the case under test and is not counted.

2018
Sworn in on 5 February 2018 as the Dow fell 1,175 points, then its biggest one-day points drop. He raised rates four times that year regardless, relenting only after December's slump.
2006
Four months in, accused of sending mixed signals as inflation climbed, he delivered a 17th consecutive rate rise to 5.25% in June 2006, then paused on his own terms once markets stopped doubting him.
1987
72 days in, Wall Street fell 22.6% in a single session. He had already raised the discount rate in September, the first rise in three years, and answered the crash with a one-sentence liquidity pledge rather than a policy retreat.
1979
Two months in, with inflation near 13% and bonds sliding, his 6 October package raised the discount rate to a then-record 12% and switched the Fed's operating regime. He tightened straight into the test.
4 of 4
All four of the last four Federal Reserve chairs to meet a market test in their first year answered by tightening or standing firm at the next scheduled decision: Volcker's bond rout in 1979, Greenspan's crash in 1987, Bernanke's inflation scare in 2006, Powell's volatility spike in 2018. None eased. The window and the full record are the same four cases, because chairs who arrive into calm markets never face the test. Standing firm was enough for two of the four, which is the distinction that matters for September: the record points to a hold, not necessarily to a rise.

All four of the last four new chairs facing an early market test tightened or stood firm at the next scheduled decision; the full record since 1979 is the same four of four. None eased into the pressure.

What this pattern excludes

The Jackson Hole symposium as the object
It is the calendar event standing beside the news, and a speech is not a policy decision. The action is the pressure applied to a new chair, and the cases score what he did at the next scheduled decision.
Chairs who took office in calm markets
Janet Yellen and Arthur Burns among them. Without an early test there is nothing for the held flag to measure.
Emergency inter-meeting moves
The test is the next scheduled decision, which is the event market 1 resolves on. Inter-meeting action is recorded in the case text where it happened but does not change the flag.

The Read

The case for the pricing above runs through the pattern's fine print. Standing firm, not hiking, was enough for Greenspan in 1987 and Powell in 2018; only Volcker and Bernanke needed actual tightening to settle the question, and both faced inflation far worse relative to target than today's 3.4%. Warsh has spent three months refusing to pre-commit, and a chair who has abolished forward guidance is unlikely to validate market pressure with a surprise hike at his first contested meeting. Hence 84% that the committee holds or raises, but only 30% that it actually raises, and a repeat dissent as the likeliest single feature of the September vote given three sitting dissenters.

The honest counter-case: the sample is four, and outside this stretch dissent-free unanimity has been the Fed's default in most meetings of the past decade. The rise market fails high if oil above $91 pushes the August inflation print, due days before the meeting, back towards 4% — that is the scenario in which Warsh's test becomes Volcker-shaped rather than Greenspan-shaped. The direct market fails only in the one way no new chair has yet chosen: if the bond rout does enough damage to growth that the committee eases into the pressure at its first contested meeting.
