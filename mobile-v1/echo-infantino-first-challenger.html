FIFA02 Aug 2026

The rebellion has the numbers game. By 18 November it needs a name.

FIFA Council members are gathering signatures to force an emergency meeting on Infantino's leadership, and UEFA associations are withdrawing support.

4 min read

What happened

FIFA Council members from Europe and North America are seeking the 19 signatures needed to force an extraordinary general meeting that could become a confidence vote on Infantino, as UEFA associations prepare to oppose his re-election.

(The Guardian, 2 Aug 2026)

A group of FIFA Council members is trying to convene an extraordinary general meeting, which requires support from 19 of the council's 37 members, and the push had reached double figures before the latest crisis broke (The Guardian, 2 August 2026). In parallel, European associations are preparing to oppose Infantino's re-election, and Wales became the first to formally withdraw its letter of support, citing failures of governance, leadership and judgement (Sky Sports, 3 August 2026).

Two disputes drive the revolt. Council members want an independent review of FIFA's decision to lift Folarin Balogun's suspension mid-World Cup after multiple phone calls from Donald Trump, a move the Norwegian FA says breached FIFA's political neutrality rules (The Guardian, 2 August 2026). Anger deepened when Infantino abandoned a plan to sell 20% of a $20 billion World Cup commercial vehicle to Thrive Capital, the firm founded by Joshua Kushner.

The electoral clock is already running. Infantino told the Vancouver Congress on 1 May that he would stand for a fourth term (FIFA, 1 May 2026); the vote is set for the 77th FIFA Congress in Rabat on 18 March 2027, and rival candidates need nominations from five member associations by 18 November 2026. Infantino holds more than 200 letters of support, but letters can be withdrawn, and Wales has just shown how.

The Pattern

Sitting FIFA presidents have sought re-election twelve times since 1974. Most runs were coronations. But every time a confederation bloc openly organised against the incumbent, a formal challenger reached the nomination stage.

1974
Stanley Rous, the English incumbent, was challenged by Brazil's João Havelange, who had spent years canvassing African and Asian federations. Havelange won 68-52 on the second ballot in Frankfurt. It remains the only time a sitting FIFA president lost an election.
2002
Amid the ISL collapse and a finance scandal, UEFA backed African confederation head Issa Hayatou against Sepp Blatter. The challenge was formally nominated and heavily beaten: Blatter won 139-56 in Seoul.
2011
Mohamed bin Hammam of Qatar declared against Blatter in March, then withdrew days before the vote as a bribery case engulfed him. Blatter was re-elected with 186 of 203 votes. A challenger filed; the ballot ended up uncontested.
2015
With US indictments landing days before the congress, UEFA-backed Prince Ali bin al-Hussein forced a vote. Blatter led 133-73 and won when Ali withdrew, then resigned four days later. Winning the ballot did not mean surviving the crisis.
2019
The counter-case. European irritation with Infantino was real but never organised into a bloc, and no challenger filed. He was re-elected by acclamation, and again in 2023. Grumbling without a bloc has never produced a name on the ballot.
4 of 4
Four times since 1974 a confederation bloc openly organised against a sitting FIFA president, and four times a challenger was formally nominated. The split inside the pattern is the honest part: of the three challenges that reached a ballot, the incumbent won two, and the 2015 winner resigned within a week anyway. Bloc opposition reliably produces a challenger. It rarely produces a new president on the day.

In all four incumbent races since 1974 with organised bloc opposition, a challenger was formally nominated. The incumbent lost the ballot only once.

The Read

The weighted case sits at 70% for a challenger filing. This is now organised bloc opposition, not grumbling: council members are counting signatures, UEFA's associations control 55 of FIFA's 211 votes, and Wales has made withdrawal respectable. Each previous time Europe organised, in 2002 and 2015, it produced a nominated candidate, and the bar is low: five association nominations, with fifteen weeks left to the deadline. Sheikh Salman, Victor Montagliani and Aleksander Ceferin all circulate as possibilities.

The honest counter-case is that no one has declared, and Ceferin has never publicly expressed interest in the job. Infantino retains Africa, South America and Qatar, and 200 letters of support concentrate minds against a losing run. If the EGM push stalls short of 19 signatures and the commercial row cools, this becomes 2019 again: anger without a candidate. And even a YES here says little about March, because the same history shows incumbents winning two of the three contested ballots.
