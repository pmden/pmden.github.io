Immigration30 Jun 2026

The asylum bill lands. Labour's left starts counting.

The government introduced its Immigration and Asylum Bill on 30 June, with Second Reading set for 13 July. The bill will pass — every major immigration bill since 1999 has. The live question is how many Labour MPs vote against a Labour bill while the party picks a new leader.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The Immigration and Asylum Bill has its First Reading in the Commons; Second Reading is scheduled for 13 July, days before the House rises.

House of Commons Library, 30 Jun 2026
2 · The event

What happened

The Immigration and Asylum Bill was introduced in the House of Commons on 30 June, implementing measures from the government's November 2025 asylum and returns policy statement House of Commons Library, 30 Jun 2026. Part 1 establishes an Independent Immigration Appeals Authority to replace the First-tier Tribunal's immigration chamber; Part 2 reforms how courts interpret Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights in deportation cases; Part 3 introduces a 'core protection' model for people granted asylum Home Office, 13 Jul 2026.

The clause drawing the most fire requires asylum seekers, once they earn above a set threshold, to repay £10,000 of the accommodation and support costs they accrued while their claims were processed. Second Reading is scheduled for 13 July, in the final sitting days before summer recess Home Office, 30 Jun 2026.

The timing is the story. The vote falls in the middle of the Labour leadership succession: nominations close on 16 July, three days after the division. The bill has proved controversial on Labour's left, Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood's position under a new leader is openly questioned, and Andy Burnham — the presumed next Prime Minister — faces pressure to signal whether he would soften the bill he is about to inherit Politico, 19 Jun 2026.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Governments do not lose immigration bills at Second Reading. Twelve major immigration acts have reached the statute book since 1999, under Labour, coalition and Conservative governments, and every one of them cleared this stage. What varies — and what actually constrains ministers — is the size of the rebellion on their own benches, and there the recent record is genuinely split.

2025
Starmer's welfare bill passed Second Reading 335 to 260 only after the government gutted its central measure mid-debate; 49 Labour MPs still voted against — the largest rebellion of the parliament.
2025
The Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Bill — this government's first immigration law — cleared Second Reading in February with no meaningful Labour rebellion.
2023
The Illegal Migration Bill passed Second Reading 312 to 250 and reached royal assent inside five months.
2021
The Nationality and Borders Bill passed Second Reading 366 to 265 and became law the following April.
1999
The Immigration and Asylum Act 1999 — the first of the twelve — set the modern template: fought hard in committee, never blocked at Second Reading.
12 of 12
Twelve of twelve major immigration bills since 1999 passed Second Reading. Passage is close to a lock. But on rebellion size the record splits: the same Labour benches produced near-zero dissent on the 2025 border bill and 49 votes against the welfare bill five months later. Which precedent applies depends on the week — and this vote lands in the strangest week in Labour's recent history.

Twelve major immigration acts since 1999; twelve cleared Second Reading. Rebellion size is the real variable.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case: the bill passes, and the rebellion stays small. Governments whip Second Reading hard precisely because it is the symbolic stage, would-be ministers in a leadership transition do not build reputations as rebels the week nominations close, and the concessions machinery — the thing that shrank the welfare revolt — has three days of debate to operate in. The 2025 border bill, not the welfare bill, is the closer comparator: an immigration measure a Labour whips' office has had months to prepare for.

The counter-case is discipline without a disciplinarian. Starmer is a departing leader with no patronage left to spend, the £10,000 repayment clause gives wavering MPs a specific, quotable objection rather than a vague unease, and a vote against this bill is close to free for anyone positioning for the soft-left of a members' ballot electorate. The welfare bill showed this parliamentary party will produce 49 rebels when the political cost drops. If dissent crosses 30, the market signal is not about this bill at all — it is the first hard measurement of how much of Starmer's programme survives him.
