Hong Kong21 Aug 2026

Convicted over a slogan. Now the only number that matters is the years.

A Hong Kong court has found the two surviving leaders of the Tiananmen vigil movement guilty of inciting subversion, ruling that their slogan calling for an end to one-party rule breached the national security law. Precedent says conviction was never the open question. The sentencing band is.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Lee Cheuk-yan, 69, and Chow Hang-tung, 41, were found guilty of incitement to subversion over the Hong Kong Alliance's call to end one-party rule; both face up to ten years, sentencing was deferred, and the UK Foreign Office condemned the verdict the same day.

The Guardian, 21 Aug 2026
2 · The event

What happened

The conviction turns on a slogan. Three national security judges found Lee Cheuk-yan, 69, and Chow Hang-tung, 41, guilty of incitement to subversion on 21 August, ruling that the Hong Kong Alliance's decades-old call to end one-party rule — rather than the candlelit vigils themselves — was what breached the 2020 national security law, because it was intended to make others lose confidence in the Chinese Communist party (The Guardian, 21 Aug 2026). A third defendant, the former lawmaker Albert Ho, 74, pleaded guilty in January; the trial ran 24 days, and Lee and Chow have been in custody since 2021 (The Guardian, 21 Aug 2026).

The sentencing mechanics now decide everything. Incitement to subversion carries a maximum of ten years, with a statutory split between cases the court deems serious, which draw five to ten years, and lesser ones, which draw under five — and nearly five years already served on remand count towards whatever is imposed. Sentencing was deferred to a later date, which the court has not yet fixed (The Guardian, 21 Aug 2026). The Alliance's June vigils were for three decades the only mass commemoration of 1989 permitted on Chinese soil.

London's response arrived the same day and followed the established template. The Foreign Office minister Baroness Winterton condemned the verdict, saying it showed peaceful acts of remembrance in Hong Kong now being treated as threats to national security (FCDO, gov.uk, 21 Aug 2026). Since the law's imposition in 2020, Britain has answered each of its milestones with statements, visa routes and treaty suspensions rather than sanctions on the officials involved.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Six years of national security law prosecutions have produced a remarkably stable machine. Conviction follows charge almost without exception; the genuine variation is in the sentencing band and in how long defendants wait to hear it.

2021
Tong Ying-kit, the first person tried under the law, was convicted on 27 July and sentenced just 3 days later to 9 years.
2022
Chow Hang-tung had already been convicted twice over banned vigils, drawing 12 and 15 months, with sentence following verdict within days in each case.
2024
The 47 democrats case: 45 of 47 were convicted in May, but sentencing took until November — a 6-month gap — with the organiser Benny Tai receiving 10 years, the heaviest national security sentence to date.
2024
By the security authorities' own count, more than 300 arrests under the law had produced a conviction rate near 100% in completed prosecutions.
2 of 3
Every completed headline national security trial has ended in conviction — the first defendant in 2021, Chow's vigil cases, 45 of the 47 democrats in 2024. The open variable is the clock: in two of the three precedent sentencings the term was handed down within days of verdict, but the 47 waited six months. Two of three point to a sentence before October; the biggest case points past it.

Convictions are near-automatic; the live variables are the sentencing band — serious or minor — and the length of the wait.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case for the serious band is in the judgment itself. The court identified the slogan as the core of the offence and found an intent to undermine confidence in the party — the language used to justify ten years for Benny Tai as an organiser in 2024 — and Lee and Chow led the Alliance for years, contested the trial, and carry no guilty-plea discount. That puts five-plus years at 58%. The dry arithmetic of remand is worth noting: with nearly five years already served, even a sentence in the serious band could see Chow free before some of the 47 democrats convicted before her.

The honest counter-case is that mitigation has room to work. The conduct was avowedly peaceful, the defendants are 69 and 74 in two of three cases, and a court wanting to signal proportionality after international criticism has a ready path below the five-year line. The sentencing-date market sits at a coin flip because three defendants, one guilty plea and separate mitigation hearings can stretch listing well past September, as the 47 case showed. The UK market is priced on six years of unbroken precedent: statements, never sanctions — the prediction fails only if London decides this verdict, unlike every one before it, requires more than words.
