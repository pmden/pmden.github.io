Bills climb into winter. The Budget has eight weeks.

Ofgem has set the October price cap at £1,723, a 4% rise driven by wholesale gas. The Chancellor's Budget falls on 28 October, three days before the higher bills start landing.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Ofgem sets the energy price cap at £1,723 for 1 October to 31 December 2026, a rise of 4% or £60 a year for a typical dual-fuel household paying by direct debit.

Ofgem, 26 Aug 2026
2 · The event

£60 on the typical bill, and a Budget three days later

Ofgem announced on 26 August 2026 that the cap for October to December would rise 4 per cent to £1,723 a year for a typical dual-fuel household on direct debit, an increase of £60 a year or £5 a month, with gas alone up 8 per cent (Ofgem, 26 Aug 2026). The regulator attributed the movement to higher wholesale gas prices. Around 35 per cent of households, roughly 11 million, are on fixed tariffs and are insulated from the change.

The government has one measure already in the post and no settled position on a second. A VAT reduction on household energy takes effect in October and is worth about £45 a year, and the Energy Secretary has said the department is examining what more can be done; reported options include widening the Warm Home Discount to further groups such as recipients of Personal Independence Payment, and a targeted payment identified through HMRC income data at a cost of roughly £2 billion (PoliticsHome, 28 Aug 2026). Campaigners note the existing £150 discount now covers about 9 per cent of a typical bill against 14 per cent when it was introduced.

The decision point is fixed. The Budget is on 28 October 2026, three days before the new cap takes effect, and the January cap is announced in late November. That sequencing gives the Chancellor a single scheduled opportunity to act before the coldest quarter, and it is the reason the question is not whether help arrives eventually but whether it arrives in the Budget.

3 · The pattern

What Chancellors have done at the Budget after an autumn cap rise

Inclusion rule, fixed before the Budget: this pattern counts every UK Budget or Autumn Statement since 2021 delivered within three months of an Ofgem announcement that the October price cap would rise. For each, the test is whether the statement itself contained a new measure aimed specifically at household energy bills, rather than general cost-of-living or welfare measures. Fiscal events following a cap decrease are out, because the trigger condition is absent. Energy packages announced outside a fiscal event are out, because the decision point is different.

2021
Ofgem raised the cap to £1,277 from 1 October 2021. The Budget and Spending Review of 27 October 2021 contained no measure aimed at household energy bills; the response came the following February, outside a fiscal event.
2022
The cap for October 2022 was set at £3,549. The Autumn Statement of 17 November 2022 extended the Energy Price Guarantee for a further year at a higher level and added cost-of-living payments. Yes.
2024
Ofgem raised the cap to £1,717 from October 2024. The Budget of 30 October 2024 contained no energy-specific household measure and confirmed the restriction of the Winter Fuel Payment.
2025
The cap rose 2% to £1,755 from October 2025. The Budget of 26 November 2025 removed three quarters of the Renewables Obligation from bills and ended the ECO scheme, cutting a typical bill by about £150 from April 2026. Yes.
2 of 4
2 of 4 since 2021. The record is genuinely split rather than leaning, and four cases is a thin base. The two that acted did so when the cap movement was either extreme, as in 2022, or when the government had a supply-side lever it wanted to use anyway, as in 2025.

2 of the 4 qualifying fiscal events since 2021 carried a measure aimed specifically at household energy bills.

What this pattern excludes

Autumn Statement, 22 November 2023
The preceding Ofgem announcement in August 2023 lowered the cap rather than raising it, so the trigger condition in the rule is absent.
The fiscal statement of 23 September 2022
The Energy Price Guarantee had been announced on 8 September, before the statement, so the statement confirmed a decision already taken rather than making one.
The February 2022 energy rebate package
Announced on the day of the Ofgem cap decision rather than at a Budget or Autumn Statement, so it sits outside a rule built on scheduled fiscal events.

5 · The read

The read

The weighted case for action is stronger than the bare 2 of 4 suggests, because the two Budgets that did nothing came at moments when the government either had no plan ready, as in 2021, or was actively withdrawing support, as in 2024. This government has done the opposite: it has already legislated a VAT cut taking effect in the same month as the cap rise, and the Energy Secretary has publicly framed further help as under consideration. Where a department is already briefing options, the Budget is the natural place to land them, which is why the market sits just below three in five.

The counter-case is fiscal and arithmetical. A 4 per cent rise is small by the standards of the cases that produced action, the cap remains well below the 2022 and 2023 levels, and 11 million households on fixed tariffs will not feel it at all. Against that, the targeted options being discussed cost around £2 billion, which is real money in a Budget already carrying pressure from borrowing. If the Chancellor concludes the October VAT cut is the answer and points to it as such, the base rate reverts to the 2021 and 2024 pattern and the Budget passes without a new energy line.
