Carnival turns sixty on one-off money. Tuesday brings the count.

Notting Hill Carnival runs its sixtieth edition this bank holiday weekend after a £4.66m rescue package from City Hall. The Met will publish its arrest total.

4 min read

Rescued in March, policed in August

The Met publishes its policing plan for the 60th Notting Hill Carnival, with additional live facial recognition cameras at the event and on the approaches to it.

(Metropolitan Police, 28 Aug 2026)

Europe's largest street festival reached its sixtieth year only after City Hall found the money. On 27 March 2026 the Mayor of London announced £4.66 million in additional funding, on top of £946,300 already committed for the year, to pay for barriers, stewarding and revised crowd management plans recommended by an independent review (Mayor of London, 27 Mar 2026). The package was framed as one-off support for this year's event.

It follows the same shape as the year before. The 2025 Carnival went ahead on £958,000 of emergency funding from City Hall, Kensington and Chelsea and Westminster, after the organisers' own safety review raised concerns about crowd density and the government confirmed in June 2025 that it would not contribute (Notting Hill Carnival Ltd, 2025). Three consecutive years of rescue money have now been signed, none of them recurrent.

The Met set out its operation on 28 August, confirming that the additional funding is supporting stewarding, crowd management and communications, and that live facial recognition will be used more extensively than before, with extra cameras on the approach routes as well as inside the footprint (Metropolitan Police, 28 Aug 2026). The force publishes an end-of-Carnival arrest total in the days after the bank holiday, and that number has become the shorthand by which the event is judged.

The Pattern

Inclusion rule, fixed before the outcome: every Notting Hill Carnival held on the streets since the event returned in 2022, using the arrest total the Metropolitan Police published in its end-of-Carnival statement and comparing it with the total for the previous edition actually held. The run is complete and nothing is dropped; the 2022 comparison is against 2019, the last Carnival before the two cancelled years. The outcome scored is simply whether the total rose.

2022
Carnival returned after two cancelled years. The Met recorded 209 arrests, well below the 353 made in 2019. The total fell.
2023
275 arrests, an increase of 66 on the previous year, alongside a sharp rise in stop and search. The total rose.
2024
334 arrests over the two main days. The total rose again.
2025
528 arrests across the weekend, the highest in the current run, though the Met reported a fall in serious violence against 2024. The total rose.
3 of 4
3 of the 4 Carnivals since 2022 saw arrests rise on the previous edition. This is a short run of a count that tracks policing intensity as much as behaviour, so it should be read as a trend in how the event is policed rather than as a measure of how it is being enjoyed.

3 of the 4 Carnivals held since 2022 recorded a higher arrest total than the edition before. The exception is 2022 itself, the return year, measured against a pre-pandemic Carnival.

What this pattern excludes

The 2020 and 2021 Carnivals
Not held on the streets, so there was no policing operation and no end-of-Carnival arrest total to compare against.
Stop and search totals for the same events
A different measure on a different publication schedule that moves with tactics rather than with the number of people taken into custody.
Arrests made in the Met's pre-Carnival warrant operations
Carried out in the days before the event and reported separately by the force, so including them would mix two different counts.
Other large London events policed by the Met, such as New Year's Eve on the Embankment
Different footprint, crowd profile and policing model; they fail the event limb of the inclusion rule even though the force and the counting method are the same.

The Read

The weighted case is another high number. Arrest totals have risen for three consecutive editions, and the two mechanical drivers both point the same way this year: a larger policing operation funded by the March package, and a wider live facial recognition deployment that reaches the approach routes as well as the event itself. Facial recognition converts existing wanted-person cases into arrests that would otherwise not happen at Carnival at all, which is why the count can climb even in a year when the crowd behaves better. That is the case for 64% above 400 and for the weight sitting in the 450 to 599 band.

The counter-case is that 528 was an outlier driven by a particular weekend, and that the money spent on barriers and stewarding is designed to prevent exactly the crushing and disorder that generates arrests. The Met itself reported less serious violence in 2025 than in 2024 despite the higher total. On the funding market, the record is unambiguous in the other direction: the 2025 package was confirmed in June of the event year and the 2026 package in March of the event year, with the government declining outright in 2025. A decision landing before the end of December would break that habit, which is why it sits at 23%.
