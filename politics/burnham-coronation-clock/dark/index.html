Starmer is out. Burnham's coronation has a deadline.

Starmer resigned as prime minister on 22 June, four days after Andy Burnham stormed the Makerfield by-election. Labour leadership nominations close on 16 July.

4 min read

The clock started on 22 June

Labour opens leadership nominations on 9 July and closes them on 16 July. A sole qualifier becomes prime minister on 17 July; a contested race pushes the result to 29 August.

(LabourList, 25 Jun 2026)

Keir Starmer announced his resignation as Labour leader and prime minister on 22 June, ending weeks of open revolt that began with Labour's poor local election results in May and accelerated through a wave of ministerial resignations in June. He stays on as caretaker until a successor is chosen (Al Jazeera, 22 Jun 2026).

The trigger was Makerfield. Andy Burnham, until then Mayor of Greater Manchester, won the 18 June by-election with 24,937 votes — 54.8% and a majority of 9,241 over Reform UK's Robert Kenyon on a 58.75% turnout (UK Parliament, 18 Jun 2026). Sitting MP Josh Simons had resigned the seat to let Burnham stand. Within days of the result, Labour MPs were publicly calling for an immediate leadership change.

The party's timetable is now fixed: MPs nominate candidates between 9 and 15 July, affiliates on 15 and 16 July. If Burnham is the only candidate to clear the threshold, the contest ends at the close of nominations and he is appointed prime minister the following day. If a rival qualifies, members ballot between 6 and 27 August with a result on 29 August — leaving Britain with a caretaker prime minister for the whole summer (LabourList, 25 Jun 2026). Wes Streeting, long seen as the likeliest rival, has endorsed Burnham (LabourList, 22 Jun 2026).

The Pattern

Five times in the last fifty years a British prime minister has resigned mid-term with the succession decided inside the governing party. The record splits: three coronations, two genuine contests.

2022
Liz Truss resigned on 20 October; Rishi Sunak collected more than 190 nominations, Penny Mordaunt withdrew short of the 100-MP threshold, and Sunak was prime minister five days later without a member ballot.
2016
David Cameron resigned after the referendum; Andrea Leadsom withdrew before the member ballot and Theresa May entered Downing Street 19 days after the vote — a contest that collapsed into a coronation.
2007
Tony Blair stood down; Gordon Brown secured 313 nominations while John McDonnell managed 29, short of the 45 required. Brown was crowned unopposed.
1990
Margaret Thatcher withdrew mid-contest; John Major had to win a real ballot, taking 185 votes against Michael Heseltine's 131 and Douglas Hurd's 56.
1976
Harold Wilson resigned unexpectedly; James Callaghan needed three ballots to beat Michael Foot 176–137. The full contest took three weeks.
3 of 5
Three of the last five mid-term handovers ended in coronation; two went to a genuine ballot. The pattern is split, and 16 July decides which side of it this handover falls.

3 of 5 mid-term handovers were coronations; 2 went to a full ballot.

The Read

The weight sits with coronation. Burnham enters with a by-election mandate of 9,241 votes, the endorsement of Wes Streeting — the one rival with a ready-made campaign — and a parliamentary party that has just watched a leader destroyed by drift and wants the question settled before September. The three modern coronations (2007, 2016, 2022) all happened when the party machine converged on one name early; that convergence is already visible.

The counter-case is the threshold itself. A candidate needs the backing of a fifth of the parliamentary Labour Party, and Labour's benches are large, bruised and ideologically wide. The soft left has no obvious champion but strong reasons to force a summer of scrutiny on a mayor who has been outside Westminster for nearly a decade. In 1976 and 1990 the governing party chose a real fight even in office. If 80-odd MPs decide Burnham should be tested rather than crowned, the market flips on a single afternoon.
