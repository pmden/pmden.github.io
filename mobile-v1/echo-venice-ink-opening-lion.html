Boyle opens Venice with Murdoch. Openers do not win the Lion.

Danny Boyle's Ink, starring Guy Pearce as Rupert Murdoch, has the opening slot in competition at the 83rd Venice Film Festival. It premieres on 2 September.

4 min read

What happened

La Biennale confirms Danny Boyle's Ink as the opening film in competition of the 83rd Venice International Film Festival, premiering on 2 September in the Sala Grande at the Palazzo del Cinema.

(La Biennale di Venezia, 28 Aug 2026)

Ink is directed by Danny Boyle and written by James Graham from his own stage play. Jack O'Connell plays Larry Lamb, Guy Pearce plays Rupert Murdoch and Claire Foy plays Jules Davies. It covers the launch of The Sun in 1969, which Boyle has described as a bigger change to the world than the moon landing of the same year. It opens the festival in competition on 2 September in the Sala Grande (La Biennale di Venezia, 2026).

The 83rd festival runs from 2 to 12 September, with 20 features in the Venezia 83 competition, all world premieres. The jury is chaired by Maggie Gyllenhaal, alongside Kaouther Ben Hania, Xavier Giannoli, Shahrbanoo Sadat, Johnnie To, the composer Daniel Blumberg and the film theorist Francesco Casetti. Golden Lions for Lifetime Achievement go to Ellen Burstyn and George Clooney (La Biennale di Venezia, Venezia 83).

The competition is heavy. Werner Herzog, Hirokazu Kore-eda, Lee Chang-dong, Martin McDonagh, Nanni Moretti, Casey Affleck, Florian Zeller and Paolo Strippoli are all in the running, and five Italian films are in the field. Ink is Boyle's first appearance in the Venice selection as a director, and the Golden Lion is announced on the closing night of 12 September.

The Pattern

Venice gives the opening slot to a film it wants photographed, not a film it intends to reward. In the five most recent festivals where the opening night film was also in competition, none of them left with the Golden Lion.

2019
Kore-eda Hirokazu's La vérité opened the 76th festival in competition (La Biennale, 2019). The Golden Lion went to Todd Phillips's Joker (La Biennale, Sep 2019).
2021
Pedro Almodóvar's Madres paralelas opened the 78th festival in competition (Deadline, Jul 2021). The Golden Lion went to Audrey Diwan's L'Événement (Al Jazeera, 11 Sep 2021).
2022
Noah Baumbach's White Noise opened the 79th festival in competition, the first Netflix film to do so (La Biennale, 2022). The Golden Lion went to Laura Poitras's All the Beauty and the Bloodshed (Deadline, Sep 2022).
2023
Edoardo De Angelis's Comandante opened the 80th festival in competition (Deadline, Aug 2023). The Golden Lion went to Yorgos Lanthimos's Poor Things (Variety, Sep 2023).
2025
Paolo Sorrentino's La Grazia opened the 82nd festival in competition (La Biennale, 2025). The Golden Lion went to Jim Jarmusch's Father Mother Sister Brother (La Biennale, Sep 2025).
0 of 5
None of the five most recent opening films placed in competition won the Golden Lion: La vérité, Madres paralelas, White Noise, Comandante and La Grazia. The mechanism is structural rather than superstitious. The opener is chosen months ahead for red-carpet value and screens before the jury has seen a single rival, and a film watched on night one competes against ten days of accumulating enthusiasm for something newer.

0 of the last 5 opening films that competed at Venice went on to win the Golden Lion, across five different juries and five different fields.

The Read

The weighted case is exposure without the Lion. Opening night hands a film the largest press corps of the festival and the strongest launch into the autumn awards season, which is why studios and sales agents want the slot and why juries almost never end up rewarding it. Ink also carries a specific handicap in this field: a British newspaper origin story, in English, about a proprietor the European critical establishment has spent fifty years writing about, against Herzog, Kore-eda, Lee Chang-dong and five Italian entries in an Italian festival.

The honest counter is that the sample is short and the subject is timely. Five festivals is five juries, and the run of opener defeats could break the moment a jury simply prefers the first film it sees. James Graham's screenplay is the strongest single argument for a prize below the Lion, which is where the any-award market gets its value, and the acting categories are open with Pearce playing Murdoch and Foy in support. The prediction fails if the jury reads Ink as a film about the manufacture of political consent rather than a period newspaper drama, which is the reading that turns a British curio into a Golden Lion.
