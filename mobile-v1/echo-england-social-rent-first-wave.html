Housing03 Sep 2026

Mayors get £2bn for social housing. No year since 2012 has reached 13,000 such homes.

The first wave of the £39 billion Social and Affordable Homes Programme allocates £9.58 billion and around 73,600 homes across England outside London.

4 min read

£9.58 billion out, £16 billion still on the table

The Prime Minister and the Secretary of State announce the first wave of the £39 billion Social and Affordable Homes Programme, allocating £9.58 billion for around 73,600 homes across England outside London.

(GOV.UK, 25 Aug 2026)

The announcement on 25 August allocated the first wave of a ten-year programme: £9.58 billion for around 73,600 homes in England outside London, with over £2 billion going to mayoral areas and £6 billion to London through the Greater London Authority, plus £46 million over three years to build council capacity. Nearly two thirds of the homes are expected to be for social rent, and more than £16 billion of the £39 billion remains unallocated (GOV.UK, 25 Aug 2026).

The regional split names the mayoral areas directly. Greater Manchester receives £529 million for around 4,400 homes, the North East £445 million for around 3,400, West Yorkshire £441 million for around 4,000, the West Midlands £409 million for around 3,200, Liverpool City Region £380 million for around 3,100 and South Yorkshire £249 million for around 2,200. Those are the same authorities that were given planning call-in powers on 23 August over developments above 150 homes, 15,000 square metres of commercial space or 30 metres in height (GOV.UK, 23 Aug 2026).

What the announcement does not carry is a delivery rate. Money allocated is not homes completed, and England publishes the completion figure every year. The most recent live tables, revised on 25 June 2026, put total affordable completions at 64,921 in 2024-25 and social rent completions at 12,267 (MHCLG, 25 Jun 2026).

The Pattern

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: this pattern uses the whole published MHCLG live table of additional affordable homes provided in England, completions basis, from 1991-92 to 2024-25, which is 34 annual observations. The rule takes every year from 2012-13 onwards, the first full year after the 2011 shift from social rent to affordable rent as the main funded tenure, and asks whether social rent completions exceeded 20,000. The period starts at a change in the funding mechanism, not at a convenient number. No year inside the period is dropped.

2012
2011-12 recorded 37,677 social rent completions, the last year of the old programme. 2012-13 recorded 17,580, less than half, as the pipeline switched to affordable rent.
2016
2016-17 was the floor at 5,827 social rent completions, against total affordable completions of 42,206. Roughly one affordable home in seven was for social rent.
2020
2020-21 recorded 6,051, still near the floor a decade after the switch, with total affordable completions of 52,078.
2024
2024-25 recorded 12,267, the highest since 2013-14, out of total affordable completions of 64,921. Four consecutive years of increase, and still a third of the 2011-12 figure.
0 of 13
0 of 13. Two things sharpen it and one softens it. It sharpens the point that the figures are completions, collected the same way for 34 years, and that the trend inside the period is a collapse and a partial recovery rather than a flat line. It softens it that the last four years have all risen and 2024-25 was the highest since 2013-14, so the record describes a mechanism that is already changing. A ten-year programme is also the wrong length to judge against annual figures from a period when no such programme existed.

0 of the 13 years from 2012-13 to 2024-25 delivered more than 20,000 social rent homes in England. The best of them was 12,267, and total affordable completions have passed 60,000 in only 5 of the last 15 years.

What this pattern excludes

Starts on site
MHCLG publishes them, and they are a leading indicator, but the department's own notes say starts outside Homes England and the GLA have only been collected since 2016-17 and on a voluntary basis, so the series may still be under-reporting. Completions are the measure that has been collected consistently since 1991-92.
Affordable rent, shared ownership and intermediate rent
They are in the total and the total is quoted, but they are let at up to 80 per cent of market rent or sold in shares, so counting them as social rent would answer a different question from the one the programme's own two-thirds promise raises.
Years before 2012-13
Social rent was the main funded tenure until the 2011 programme changed it. Including those years would put a different funding mechanism into the count and would make the base rate look far better than the one the new money has to beat.

The Read

The near-term market is low because the first wave was designed as the announcement. More than £16 billion is unallocated, but a department that has just distributed £9.58 billion in one release has no reason to follow it with another inside five weeks, and the capacity money for councils runs over three years rather than three weeks.

The two statistical markets are where the programme meets its own record. Social rent has risen four years running and added 1,942 homes between 2023-24 and 2024-25, so 14,000 in 2025-26 needs a rise of about 1,733, which is inside the recent range but at the top of it. The total is harder: completions have moved 4,758, then 557, then 575 in the last three years, and 68,000 would need a jump larger than any of them. The honest way to hold both is that the new money cannot appear in 2025-26 completions at all, because homes finished in that year were funded years ago. These markets measure the pipeline the programme inherits, not the programme. The number to watch for the programme itself is starts, and the department's own notes say that series is the one it cannot yet count properly.
