Jenrick crossed the floor. Badenoch counts who follows.

Kemi Badenoch sacked Robert Jenrick as shadow justice secretary on 15 January after uncovering his plan to defect — and by that evening he was standing beside Nigel Farage as Reform UK's newest recruit. Defections in Britain rarely come alone.

3 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Jenrick joined Reform UK at a joint press conference with Farage, hours after Badenoch sacked him citing 'clear, irrefutable evidence that he was plotting in secret to defect'.

Politico, 15 Jan 2026
2 · The event

A sacking, then a stage

On 15 January, Kemi Badenoch sacked Robert Jenrick from the shadow cabinet and removed the Conservative whip after being handed copies of his planned defection speech and an accompanying media plan. She said the evidence showed he was 'plotting in secret to defect in a way designed to be as damaging as possible to his shadow cabinet colleagues and the wider Conservative Party' (Politico, 15 Jan 2026).

The pre-emption changed the choreography, not the outcome. Jenrick — who took Badenoch to the final round of the 2024 leadership contest — appeared alongside Nigel Farage at a hastily arranged press conference that same day and joined Reform UK, later taking the party's Treasury brief (New Statesman, 15 Jan 2026).

The aftermath ran against expectations. Rather than opening the floodgates, Jenrick's exit removed the most credible internal threat to Badenoch's leadership; by June, Tory MPs were crediting her combative despatch-box performances for a marked recovery in the party's internal mood, even after difficult May local elections. No second Conservative MP has followed Jenrick across — yet Reform still leads the national polls, and the pull that moved him has not weakened.

3 · The pattern

Do defections come alone?

The modern record on floor-crossing is clear on one thing: the first mover is usually not the last. In four of five defection episodes since 1981, another MP followed within six months. The exception had a general election in the way.

2024
Lee Anderson became Reform UK's first MP in March. No sitting Tory followed before the election was called — but Reform won 5 seats that July. The one 'lone' defection, cut short by the calendar.
2019
Change UK: 8 Labour MPs quit in a single February week, joined by 3 Conservatives within days. All 11 were out of the Commons by December's election.
2014
Douglas Carswell defected to UKIP in August; Mark Reckless followed 5 weeks later. Both won their by-elections, Carswell holding the seat in 2015.
1995
Alan Howarth crossed from the Conservatives to Labour in October; Emma Nicholson followed to the Liberal Democrats within 3 months, deepening Major's majority crisis.
1981
The SDP's Gang of Four triggered the biggest rupture of the century: 28 Labour MPs and one Conservative crossed over two years. Most lost their seats in 1983.
4 of 5
In four of five modern defection episodes, the first mover was followed by another MP within six months. Jenrick crossed on 15 January; the six-month window closes in mid-July with the follower column still empty.

5 · The read

How to read it

The case for a follower: the structural pull is unchanged. Reform leads the polls, Jenrick has been rewarded with a frontbench brief rather than exile, and for Tory MPs in Reform-leaning seats the electoral logic that moved him is a standing offer. Farage has every incentive to land a second defection during Labour's leadership vacuum, when a crossing would own the news cycle for days. Four of five historical episodes say the second name comes.

The counter-case is that the six-month window has nearly closed with nobody moving. Badenoch is stronger now than the day she wielded the axe — sacking Jenrick first denied Reform its ambush and warned the parliamentary party what pre-emption looks like. The MPs most tempted have watched Farage spend the summer under a standards inquiry and Jenrick surrender a plausible path to the Tory leadership for a spokesman's title. Twenty-nine per cent may still be generous for five quiet summer weeks in which Westminster's attention is entirely on the Labour succession.
