Bonds21 Aug 2026

The bond rout went global. Washington's fix lasted a day.

With 10-, 20- and 30-year US yields at or near two-decade highs, the Treasury doubled its debt buybacks on Wednesday. The relief lasted a day. The record of official interventions in bond routs since 1994 says the detail of the fix, not the announcement, decides whether this was the peak.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The US Treasury announced on Wednesday morning that it is doubling its buyback programme for government debt, framing the move as liquidity support for the long-term bond market after 10- and 20-year yields hit 20-year highs and the 30-year its highest since 2007.

The Guardian, 19 Aug 2026
2 · The event

What happened

The US Treasury said on Wednesday 19 August that it would double its debt buyback programme, presenting the step as liquidity support for the long end of the market after a week in which 10- and 20-year Treasury yields reached 20-year highs and the 30-year rose to its highest since 2007 (The Guardian, 19 Aug 2026). Yields dipped on the announcement and equities edged higher. By Thursday the effect had reversed and yields were rising again, with Treasury secretary Scott Bessent's intervention judged to have bought a single session (The Guardian, 20 Aug 2026).

The selloff is global. The 30-year Treasury closed at 5.33% on Monday, its highest since June 2007; UK 10-year gilts reached 5.18%, near their highest since 2008, with 30-year gilts around levels last seen in 1998; Japan's 10-year yield hit 2.95%, a 30-year high; German yields stand at 2011 levels and French yields at a 16-year peak (The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026). The drivers named by analysts are the collapse of US–Iran talks over the Strait of Hormuz, Brent crude above $91 a barrel, a US national debt that has doubled in a decade to $40tn, heavy borrowing by technology companies for data centres, and unpredictability around the administration's tax and spending plans (The Guardian, 20 Aug 2026).

The Federal Reserve added its own pressure the same day. Minutes of the July meeting, released on 19 August, showed the committee holding rates at 3.5–3.75% while three members argued for increases if inflation, at 3.4% in July after 4.2% in May, fails to move towards the 2% target (Federal Reserve, 19 Aug 2026). Chair Kevin Warsh has scrapped forward guidance and declined to signal a direction, and markets currently expect a hold at the 15–16 September meeting (The Guardian, 16 Aug 2026).

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Five major sovereign-bond routs since 1994 ended in two distinct ways, and the difference was not the size of the announcement. It was whether the intervention changed actual supply or demand for bonds.

1994
The Fed's February 1994 tightening set off a nine-month rout: the 30-year Treasury climbed from about 6.2% to above 8% by November. Official reassurance changed nothing. Yields peaked in November 1994, once markets could see the end of the cycle.
2013
The taper tantrum: after Ben Bernanke's 22 May testimony, the 10-year Treasury rose from 1.9% to 3% by September. The Fed's surprise decision not to taper bought weeks, not a turn — yields were back at 3% by December and settled only once the taper was actually announced.
2020
The dash for cash: the 10-year yield roughly quadrupled from about 0.3% in nine trading days in March. The Fed had restarted large-scale purchases on 15 March and made them unlimited on 23 March; by then the panic peak in yields was already in.
2022
The mini-budget rout: 30-year gilt yields passed 5% before the Bank of England stepped in on 28 September with up to £65bn of emergency gilt purchases. Long yields fell about 100 basis points almost at once. The peak was that week.
2023
The 10-year Treasury touched 5.02% on 23 October. On 1 November the US Treasury slowed long-dated issuance and the Fed held; the yield ended the year near 3.9%. The peak came within days of the supply shift.
3 of 5
In five major bond routs since 1994, official action marked the yield peak three times, and in each the fix altered real supply or demand: unlimited Fed purchases in 2020, the Bank of England's £65bn gilt operation in 2022, the Treasury's issuance shift in 2023. Reassurance and half-measures, as in 1994 and 2013, let yields climb for months. Wednesday's buyback faded in a day.

3 of 5 routs peaked within days of intervention — every time the intervention changed real supply or demand. Words and half-measures, as in 1994 and 2013, let the rout run for months.

5 · The read

How to read it

The two markets bracket the same judgment: was Monday's 5.33% the top? The pattern is unhelpful to the Treasury. A doubled buyback recycles existing debt rather than cutting issuance, which places it closer to the 1994 and 2013 half-measures than to the three interventions that worked — and its one-day effect, already reversed by Thursday, is behaving like the failures did. With Brent above $91, three Fed members arguing for rises and a chair offering no guidance, the path of least resistance for yields has been up. That is why the new-high market at 5.50% carries the higher probability of the pair.

The honest counter-case is that the two working precedents from 2022 and 2023 both arrived as follow-up moves once the pain got loud enough: a central bank buying outright, or the Treasury cutting long-dated supply at a refunding. Either remains available before 11 September, and the July minutes' hawks could equally trigger a growth scare that pulls long yields down. The below-5% market fails if the Iran confrontation escalates further or the August US inflation print, due around the resolution date, comes in hot; the above-5.5% market fails if Washington follows the buyback with a genuine issuance shift.
