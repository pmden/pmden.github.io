Borrowing overshoots again. The next print decides if it is a trend.

May's public borrowing came in £5.6 billion above the OBR's forecast. Two months into the fiscal year, the overshoot is already £7.7 billion.

4 min read

£7.7 billion off course after two months

May borrowing hits £23.3bn, £5.6bn above the OBR forecast; debt interest is the highest for any May on record.

(ONS, 19 Jun 2026)

Public sector borrowing reached £23.3 billion in May 2026, £5.4 billion more than in May 2025 and £5.6 billion above the £17.7 billion the Office for Budget Responsibility had pencilled in (ONS, 19 Jun 2026). Central government debt interest hit £11.7 billion, up 54.4% on a year earlier and the highest for any May on record. Public sector net debt stood at 95.1% of GDP, territory last seen in the early 1960s.

The cumulative picture is the harder one for the Treasury. Borrowing in the financial year to May was £46.3 billion, £7.7 billion above the OBR's profile of £38.6 billion and 23.9% higher than the same period last year, driven by debt interest, higher spending on services and benefits, and receipts that failed to keep pace (ONS, 19 Jun 2026). Chancellor Rachel Reeves rebuilt her fiscal headroom with £26 billion of tax rises last November; the data shows that buffer eroding from the first quarter.

Two fixed points now shape the story. The OBR publishes its Fiscal Risks and Sustainability report on 7 July, examining long-run pressures from debt interest, the triple lock and demography (OBR, Jul 2026). Then on 21 July the ONS releases the June borrowing figures, the first hard signal of whether the spring overshoot is a blip or a trend. The autumn Budget has no date yet, and until the Treasury sets one there is nothing in the calendar for the fiscal argument to attach to.

The Pattern

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: every occasion since 2012 on which cumulative UK borrowing was running above the official forecast profile in the first months of a financial year, the shape May 2026 has now taken at £7.7 billion above. The test is single: was the overshoot still there at the next monthly ONS release, rather than washing out as a one-month quirk — the question market 1 asks of 21 July. Four cases meet the rule and all four are shown; the current overshoot is the case under test and is not counted. What each chancellor then did about it, months later, is recorded in the case lines as the second beat.

2025
Borrowing ran above the OBR profile through spring and summer. The November Budget answered with £26 billion of tax rises, taking the tax take toward a record share of GDP.
2024
The new government declared a £22 billion in-year shortfall in July. The October Budget raised taxes by £40 billion, the largest single increase since 1993.
2022
Borrowing deteriorated sharply after the September mini-Budget crisis. The November Autumn Statement delivered a £55 billion consolidation of tax rises and spending restraint.
2012
Borrowing overshot plans mid-year, but the Autumn Statement avoided net tax rises: George Osborne extended austerity by a further year to 2018 and let the debt target slip instead.
4 of 4
All four early-year borrowing overshoots since 2012 were still running at the next monthly ONS release, and the window and the full record are the same four cases. The mechanism is dull and reliable: the drivers of an early-year gap are debt interest on index-linked gilts and spending settlements already signed, and neither reverses inside four weeks. Four cases is the floor for publishing a number at all, and a unanimous four carries a wide margin of error, so the honest reading is that the direction is well established and the strength is not measurable from this sample. What the chancellor then does is the slower question: three of the four overshoots ended in autumn tax rises, and the exception, 2012, extended spending cuts instead.

In all four early-year overshoots since 2012 the gap was still there at the next monthly print; three of the four then ended in autumn tax rises, and the exception chose longer austerity instead.

What this pattern excludes

The autumn Budget as the object
There is no Budget date in the calendar on 20 June, and any autumn event sits far beyond the market horizon. The action in the news is borrowing running above the OBR profile two months into the year, so the first test is whether that gap survives the next print.
Financial years that opened at or below the OBR profile
Most do. They answer a different question — what happens when the numbers behave — and would dilute the test market 1 asks.
Overshoots created by one-off reclassifications
Where the gap comes from a statistical reclassification rather than receipts and spending, the next print reflects an accounting decision, not fiscal behaviour.

The Read

The weighted case is continuation. Every month of this financial year has printed above the OBR's profile, and the drivers are structural rather than one-off: debt interest tracks index-linked gilts and elevated rates, and the spending side reflects settlements already signed, including the NHS pay deals. That is why the cumulative market sits at 80% — four of four early-year overshoots since 2012 were still there a month later, and none of the 2026 components has turned. A hot 21 July print, landing two weeks after an OBR report on long-run debt risks, would fix the autumn Budget narrative before August.

The counter-case rests on timing and revision. Early-year borrowing data is the least reliable of the fiscal calendar: receipts arrive lumpily, and the ONS has a record of revising first prints down by billions as self-assessment and corporation tax data firm up. June also captures less debt-interest pain than May, because the RPI uplift on index-linked gilts falls differently across months — which is exactly why the single-month market sits at 63% rather than higher. On the Budget date, history points to patience: recent dates have been confirmed in late summer, not in June, so 55% is close to a coin toss on the Treasury moving early.
