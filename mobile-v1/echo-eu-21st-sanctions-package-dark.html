Sanctions25 Jun 2026

Twenty packages passed. The twenty-first meets the pipeline politics.

The EU has extended its economic sanctions on Russia for a full year and adopted a mini package of listings. The heavyweight 21st package, targeting energy, crypto and the shadow fleet, is still on the table.

4 min readEcho #116INTERNATIONAL
1 · Today

The Council extended EU economic sanctions on Russia for twelve months, to 31 July 2027, the first annual renewal instead of the usual six-month roll-over.

Council of the EU, 25 Jun 2026
2 · The event

What happened

On 9 June, Commission President Ursula von der Leyen announced a 21st sanctions package against Russia, aimed at energy, financial services and trade (European Commission, 10 June 2026). The proposal pauses the oil price cap adjustment mechanism until January 2027, lists 30 additional shadow-fleet vessels, targets 20 third-country entities including banks, crypto platforms and oil traders accused of circumvention, and for the first time sanctions Russian fisheries, with a complete ban on cod.

Two adopted measures followed in quick succession. On 15 June the Council approved a mini package designating a further 34 individuals and 47 entities, including 24 entities tied to the export of Russian crude through the shadow fleet (Council of the EU, 15 June 2026). On 25 June the Council renewed the entire economic sanctions regime for twelve months, to 31 July 2027, abandoning the six-month roll-over that had given Budapest a veto opportunity twice a year (Council of the EU, 25 June 2026).

The full 21st package still requires unanimity, and the recent history is fresh. Hungary and Slovakia blocked the 20th package from late February over the Druzhba pipeline shutdown, and only lifted their veto after oil flows were restored on 23 April (The Guardian, 22 Apr 2026). The 21st package's maritime and energy measures cut close to the same interests that produced that standoff.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Twenty sanctions packages in four years, and every one eventually adopted. But the tail of the process has grown: where early packages cleared in days, recent ones have been held for weeks or months by one or two capitals extracting a price. The record on the last five is instructive.

2026
The 20th package was blocked by Slovakia and Hungary from 23 February over the Druzhba pipeline shutdown, and adopted only in May, roughly ten weeks later, after oil flows were restored on 23 April.
2025
The 19th package was adopted on 23 October after Slovak reservations delayed sign-off; it took the EU's first measures against Russian LNG imports.
2025
The 18th package was proposed on 10 June and adopted on 18 July, nearly six weeks later, after Slovakia held it over gas phase-out plans and dropped its veto in exchange for written assurances.
2024
The 14th package was adopted on 24 June with its anti-circumvention clause softened during negotiation to bring Germany and others on board.
2023
The 11th package was adopted on 23 June after Hungary held out for roughly five weeks over Ukraine's listing of OTP Bank as a war sponsor.
20 of 20
Twenty of twenty packages were eventually adopted, a perfect record on the destination. The split is on the journey: four of the last five were held up by a veto, and holdouts have added five to ten weeks. Adoption within a month of proposal has become the exception, not the rule.

All 20 packages were eventually adopted, but four of the last five were delayed by a member-state holdout, typically by five to ten weeks.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case leans towards adoption, but late. The EU has never failed to pass a package, the twelve-month renewal on 25 June removed the recurring expiry hostage, and the mini package shows the machinery still turns even while the main text is negotiated. Von der Leyen announced the package with adoption before the August institutional pause as the working assumption, and the Commission has form in buying off holdouts with targeted derogations. The multi market's lean towards "adopted with carve-outs" reflects exactly how the 18th and 20th packages ended.

The honest counter-case is the calendar and the pipeline. Five weeks is the fast end of recent holdout arithmetic, and the 21st package touches maritime oil logistics and energy revenues, the precise terrain of the Druzhba standoff that froze the 20th package for ten weeks. Budapest faces elections and has diminishing reasons to hand Brussels a quick win, and August is when EU decision-making slows to a crawl. A package proposed in June and adopted in September would surprise nobody who has watched the last three.
