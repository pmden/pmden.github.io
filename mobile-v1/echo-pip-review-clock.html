The PIP review inches forward. Autumn holds the answer.

The Timms Review's co-chairs published a progress update on 8 June, keeping the redesign of disability benefit assessment on course to report in autumn 2026.

4 min read

The review that carries the welfare bill's unfinished business

Timms Review co-chairs publish a progress update on the PIP assessment review, which aims to report to the Work and Pensions Secretary in autumn 2026.

(GOV.UK, 8 Jun 2026)

The co-chairs of the Timms Review — Sir Stephen Timms, Minister for Social Security and Disability, alongside Sharon Brennan and Dr Clenton Farquharson — published a formal progress update on 8 June, the review's main public milestone of the summer (GOV.UK, 8 Jun 2026). The review is examining the role of Personal Independence Payment, its assessment and its criteria, co-produced with disabled people, and aims to report to the Secretary of State for Work and Pensions in autumn 2026 (GOV.UK, Jun 2026).

The review exists because the government's original plan failed in the Commons. In July 2025, a rebellion of Labour backbenchers forced ministers to strip the tightened PIP eligibility rules — and most of the projected savings — out of the welfare bill, deferring the entire question of PIP's design to the Timms process. What survived was a slimmer package: in December 2025 the DWP announced operational reforms, including extended intervals between PIP award reviews and a rise in face-to-face assessments to 30%, projected to save £1.9 billion by the end of 2030/31, with changes taking effect from April 2026 (GOV.UK, 18 Dec 2025).

The autumn timetable now collides with the fiscal calendar. Whatever the review recommends will land as the Treasury assembles a Budget under pressure from above-forecast borrowing, and any change to PIP assessment flows directly into the welfare spending line the OBR scores. The June update keeps the review on schedule; it does not say what it will conclude, and ministers have made no commitment to adopt its recommendations in full.

The Pattern

Governments of both parties have repeatedly announced multi-billion-pound welfare savings. The pattern of what happens next is genuinely split — some packages stick, but most are reversed, diluted, or quietly outgrown by rising caseloads.

2025
Planned PIP eligibility cuts worth around £4.5 billion were stripped from the welfare bill after more than 120 Labour MPs threatened rebellion; the savings were deferred to the Timms Review.
2015
George Osborne's £4.4 billion tax credit cuts passed the Commons, were defeated in the Lords, and were abandoned entirely within four months.
2013
The DLA-to-PIP transition was scored to cut working-age caseloads by 20%. Caseloads and costs rose instead; the projected savings never materialised.
2013
The under-occupancy charge — the bedroom tax — was implemented, survived legal challenge, and delivered roughly £500 million a year, close to plan. The rare case that stuck.
1997
The lone parent benefit cut passed despite 47 Labour rebels, then was effectively offset within two years by higher child benefit and tax credits.
1 of 5
1 of 5 major announced welfare-savings packages since 1997 delivered its savings roughly as planned. The pattern is honestly split between outright reversal and slow dilution — full delivery is the outlier.

Split pattern: 1 of 5 headline welfare-savings packages since 1997 delivered broadly as announced; the rest were reversed, diluted or overtaken by caseload growth.

The Read

The 7% forecast on an early report reflects the stated timetable and the machinery behind it. The review's own June update points to autumn, co-production with disabled people's organisations is deliberately slow, and government reviews of this scale almost never report early — the incentive runs the other way, since publishing in August would hand the Treasury a contested welfare document weeks before a difficult Budget with no Commons sitting to manage the response. Every institutional force points to publication after Parliament returns.

The counter-case is fiscal gravity. Borrowing is running £7.7 billion above forecast, the OBR's July report catalogues long-run welfare pressures, and the Treasury needs scoreable measures for the autumn. That creates real pressure to accelerate the review or pre-announce its direction — and a government that has already lost one welfare vote may prefer to socialise difficult conclusions early rather than spring them alongside a Budget. Even so, that argues for a September or October report, not an August one. The larger question — whether any recommended savings survive the pattern above — resolves over quarters, not weeks.
