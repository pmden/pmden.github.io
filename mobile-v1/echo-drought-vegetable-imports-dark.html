Drought01 Aug 2026

The fields ran dry in July. History says the till pays in winter.

Britain is importing lettuce and broccoli mid-season after the driest July on record for England and Wales. The first official test lands on 19 August.

4 min read

What happened

Supermarkets, pubs and restaurants are buying lettuce and broccoli from Spain in what should be the peak of the British season, as drought and sustained heat cut yields and drive wholesale vegetable prices sharply higher.

(The Guardian, 1 Aug 2026)

British buyers are importing salad and brassicas from Spain and the Netherlands in the weeks when home-grown supply normally dominates. Wholesale prices are up more than 60% for tomatoes, 90% for iceberg lettuce and over 40% for potatoes, broccoli yields have roughly halved, and the English pea harvest is down by about a third (The Guardian, 1 August 2026). A Yorkshire grower responsible for around 4% of the national carrot crop described conditions as the worst in his experience, and the trade expects British carrots to run out months early, forcing spring imports.

The weather backdrop is genuinely without precedent. Provisional Met Office figures show England received about 6.5mm of rain in July, roughly a tenth of the monthly average, making it the driest July on record for England and Wales; southern England saw just 1.9mm (Met Office climate summaries, August 2026). Drought has been declared across a swathe of England, and around 23 million people are under hosepipe bans announced by the water companies, including Thames Water's 10 million customers.

The economic question is not whether this reaches shop prices but when. The most recent official reading points the other way: food and non-alcoholic drink prices rose 1.7% in the year to June, down from 2.2% in May and the lowest annual rate since August 2024 (ONS, June 2026). Wholesale markets reprice in days. Supermarket contracts, which cover most of what households actually pay, do not.

One channel could shorten the lag this time. The heat has also cut harvests across mainland Europe, so the imports now substituting for British produce are themselves dearer than usual (The Guardian, 1 August 2026). The escape valve that muted past drought summers is partly closed.

The Pattern

Four modern British drought summers offer a clean test of how fast dry fields become dear food. The answer is usually slower than the headlines suggest, and once it was not.

1976
The benchmark drought decimated the potato crop, with ware potatoes hitting £250 a tonne and Dutch imports covering an estimated 2 million tonne shortfall (Farmers Weekly retrospective; NFU). Shortages ran deep into 1977, and annual food inflation peaked in August 1977, roughly a year after the drought broke, at a level not seen again until 2023 (ONS historical series).
1995
The driest England and Wales summer on record at 73.3mm of rain, ahead of 1976, with eleven drought orders still extant in the Yorkshire Water area by April 1996. Food CPI went from 2.9% in July to 4.4% in August and 5.7% in September, with RPI vegetables up 16.4% in September while all-items inflation barely moved (Met Office England and Wales rainfall series; Hansard, 1 April 1996; ONS series D7G8, CZBW and CZBH).
2018
The summer heatwave sent wholesale carrot prices up about 80% and lettuce over 60% between March and July, with trade estimates of roughly £86 added to annual grocery bills. Yet the official food CPI rate fell through the second half of 2018, from about 2.0% in June to under 1.5% by autumn, as supermarket contracts absorbed the shock (ONS series D7G8).
2022
Drought was declared across much of England in August 2022, with farmers warning of onion, carrot, cabbage and potato losses. The vegetable shortages materialised in February and March 2023, with rationing of salad crops and food inflation peaking above 19% in March 2023, around seven months later, amplified by the separate energy shock (ONS; industry reporting).
3 of 4
In 3 of 4 UK drought summers since 1976, the big retail food-price move came with a lag of five months or more. The exception is the deepest one. 1995 was the driest England and Wales summer on record, and food CPI ran 2.9% in July, 4.4% in August and 5.7% in September. Wholesale panic and till-price reality usually run on different clocks, but the driest summer in the set did not wait.

In three of the four drought summers since 1976, the retail food-price surge arrived months after the fields dried. In the driest of them, 1995, the next prints jumped.

The Read

The weighted case says the July print does not clear 2.0%. The base rate is 3 of 4 for a lagged response, the June rate was falling, and most retail food prices sit inside supermarket contracts agreed months ago. The July collection window also closed before the worst of the wholesale spike, since ONS price collection happens mid-month and the sharpest import substitution came in late July. That is why the 28% on YES sits well below where the headlines would put it.

The honest counter-case has two legs. Fresh vegetables are the fastest pass-through category in the food basket, and this drought differs from 2018 in one important way: Europe is dry too, so import substitution is expensive rather than cheap, closing the valve that muted 2018. There is also a precedent for the fast response: in 1995, the driest England and Wales summer on record, food CPI jumped from 2.9% in July to 5.7% by September. If the July print does clear 2.0%, the lag thesis is wrong in exactly the way that matters, and the follow-on markets for the August and September prints should be repriced hard. A print below 1.5% would mean even the fresh-produce channel is being fully absorbed, which no drought summer has shown before.
