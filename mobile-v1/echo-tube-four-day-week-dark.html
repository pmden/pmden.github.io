Two strike days done. The dispute isn't.

RMT members shut down swathes of the Underground on 2 and 4 June over TfL's compressed four-day week trial for Tube drivers. The walkouts have ended; the disagreement has not.

4 min readEcho #116LONDON
1 · Today

RMT members completed a second 24-hour walkout over TfL's four-day compressed week trial. The union says the dispute is unresolved and further action remains possible.

The Guardian, 4 Jun 2026
2 · The event

What happened

RMT members on the London Underground staged two 24-hour strikes, on Tuesday 2 June and Thursday 4 June, in protest at a compressed four-day working week trial for Tube drivers on the Bakerloo line. The Circle and Piccadilly lines ran no service, along with central sections of the Metropolitan and Central lines, with disruption rippling across the whole network (The Guardian, 4 Jun 2026).

The union argues that compressing five days of driving into four means longer shifts, more fatigue and less flexibility in a safety-critical role. TfL counters that the trial is optional, does not increase contractual hours, and is intended to modernise working patterns. Talks at ACAS before the strikes failed to produce the assurances the RMT wanted (RMT, June 2026).

The June walkouts were themselves the second act: strike dates set for 19 and 21 May and 16 and 18 June were cancelled on 18 May after late talks, before the union pressed ahead with the 2 and 4 June dates. No further dates have been announced, but the RMT has not settled, and TfL's own strike page keeps the dispute listed as open (TfL, July 2026).

3 · The pattern

The pattern

The question is whether a paused RMT dispute stays paused. Recent Underground disputes split into two families: those that settled after a single round or a late concession, and those that kept producing new strike dates until a deal landed.

2025
September 2025 — the RMT staged five days of rolling strikes over pay and fatigue, largely shutting the Underground from 7 to 11 September after months of talks failed to move TfL.
2024
January 2024 — a planned week of Tube strikes was called off at the last minute after TfL improved its pay offer. The dispute ended without a single walkout.
2023
March 2023 — a one-day strike over pensions and job cuts went ahead on 15 March, closing nearly the whole network; the underlying dispute rumbled on for months without another Tube-wide walkout.
2022
June 2022 — a one-day strike on 6 June over pensions and staffing went ahead, and the same dispute returned with another network shutdown on 19 August.
2015
2015–16 — the Night Tube dispute produced two full network shutdowns in July and August 2015, delayed the launch by roughly a year, and settled only in 2016.
3 of 5
Three of the last five major RMT Underground disputes produced further strike dates after an initial round or suspension. Two settled without another walkout. The pattern is genuinely split — it leans towards more action, but a quiet settlement is far from rare.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case for more strike dates rests on structure, not mood. The RMT has already called and cancelled two rounds and executed a third; that is a union keeping its mandate warm, not winding a dispute down. TfL has framed the four-day trial as optional and cost-neutral, which gives it little room to concede the substance, and the union's core objection — fatigue in a safety-critical grade — is not the kind of concern a modest sweetener dissolves. September 2025 showed how this script ends when neither side moves: rolling strikes after a long stalemate.

The counter-case is January 2024. When TfL wants a dispute gone, it can move late and fast, and a trial scheme is easier to soften than a pay award — extend the evaluation, add fatigue reviews, make the opt-in explicit, and the RMT can bank a win without another picket. Two of the last five disputes ended exactly this way. Summer timetables also cut both ways: strikes hurt more in term time, which gives the union a reason to wait, and a reason why no news through July would not mean the dispute is dead.
