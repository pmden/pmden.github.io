Ashley reaches nearly 48% of Hugo Boss. German law prices the rest.

Frasers Group's tender offer closed with 47.9% of Hugo Boss, just short of formal control. Under German takeover rules the completed offer lets it keep buying.

4 min read

What happened

Frasers Group increased its shareholding in Hugo Boss to nearly 48% after holders of 17.6% of the shares accepted its €38-a-share offer, a week after the group bought the Harvey Nichols chain out of administration for about £40m.

(The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026)

The mechanics matter more than the headline number. Frasers announced on 18 August that acceptances covering 17.6% of Hugo Boss shares at €38 each had taken its stake to 47.9%, leaving it just short of formal control but, in analysts' framing, firmly in the driving seat (The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026). The path ran through a rejection: Hugo Boss's board dismissed a €2bn takeover proposal as inadequate in June, Frasers published its offer anyway, and the two boards issued a joint reasoned statement on 9 July before the results were confirmed on 18 August (Hugo Boss IR, 18 Aug 2026). Chair Stephan Sturm has since struck a constructive tone about the relationship (The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026).

German takeover law now works for Frasers rather than against it. Crossing 30% of a listed company normally forces a mandatory offer to all shareholders, but a completed voluntary takeover offer discharges that duty, so from 48% Frasers may keep buying in the market with no obligation to bid again. In practice 48% delivers a working majority at most AGMs, where turnout rarely approaches full attendance; the meaningful thresholds left are 50% of the register, then 75% of votes present for a domination and profit-transfer agreement that would give direct access to Hugo Boss's cash flows. The last two points to a majority are the cheap part.

Hugo Boss is one piece of a rapid luxury build-out. Frasers bought Harvey Nichols, with 13 stores and 1,200 staff, out of administration for about £40m the previous week, took a 4.2% stake in Burberry worth roughly £160m last month, and holds a position in Mulberry from its earlier campaign there; Mike Ashley retains 73% of Frasers itself (The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026). Frasers first bought into Hugo Boss in June 2020 and has run an activist position since (The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026).

The Pattern

Frasers has pressed five stake-building campaigns at listed retailers to a conclusion since 2019. The decisive variable was never price. It was whether a blocking shareholder base stood in the way.

2019
Game Digital: a stake built past the 30% mandatory-bid line forced a 30p-a-share offer valuing the retailer at about £52m. Frasers took full control that summer.
2022
Studio Retail and MySale: two stakes of roughly 29% converted into ownership in the same year — Studio bought out of administration for about £27m in February, MySale through a low-priced offer that went unconditional in the autumn.
2024
Mulberry: from a 37% holding, Frasers proposed about £83m, raised it to roughly £111m, was rebuffed by the founder-aligned majority holder with about 56%, and withdrew.
2024
boohoo: a stake of about 27% and a campaign to put Mike Ashley on the board were voted down at a December general meeting. Stake-building without a path past hostile holders bought influence, not control.
3 of 5
Frasers has pressed five stake-building campaigns to a decision since 2019 and won control in three: Game Digital, Studio Retail and MySale. The two failures, Mulberry and boohoo, each collided with a shareholder bloc Frasers could not out-vote. Hugo Boss has no majority blocker, its chair is talking constructively, and Frasers already holds 48% with the freedom to keep buying.

3 of 5 campaigns ended with Frasers owning the company. Both failures ran into a shareholder bloc it could not out-vote. Hugo Boss has no such bloc — and Frasers already holds 48%.

The Read

The case for YES on the 50% market is that every obstacle that stopped Frasers before is absent here. There is no majority blocker as at Mulberry, no hostile register as at boohoo, and no fresh regulatory gate: the completed offer means further purchases carry no bid obligation, and the shortest route from 48% to a majority is simply the order book. Ashley's pattern is to move within weeks of a position hardening — the Harvey Nichols and Burberry purchases came inside two months — and a stake stuck at 48% earns him neither consolidation nor the domination agreement that would justify the outlay. The board seat follows the same logic at a discount: German supervisory-board changes need a meeting, so the calendar, not the politics, is the main drag on the October market.

The honest counter-case is that 48% may be exactly where Frasers wants to sit. Passing 50% consolidates Hugo Boss's debt onto Frasers' balance sheet, and holding just below keeps the driving seat without the accounting weight, a trick Ashley has used with long-held minority stakes elsewhere. Shares trading above the €38 offer price would also make creeping dearer than the tender just closed. The 50% market fails if Frasers pauses to digest Harvey Nichols; the board market fails if Sturm's constructive tone converts into informal cooperation that makes a formal seat unnecessary before an EGM is ever called.
