Australia's Senate blocked a health insurance cut. Will it pass by 26 November?

Australia's Senate threw out a bill to cut the health insurance rebate for over-65s on 14 October. The government sent it back a day later.

4 min read

What happened

The Greens, the Coalition and crossbench senators blocked Labor’s bill to cut the private health insurance rebate for over-65s and discharged it from the Senate. Health minister Mark Butler said the same bill would be reintroduced the next day.

In Australia a bill must pass both chambers of parliament, and the Labor government has no majority in the Senate, the upper one. It needs the Greens or the Liberal-National Coalition to pass anything there. When the Senate throws a bill out, the government can bring the same text back as a second bill and try again.

On 14 October the Greens, the Coalition and independent senators joined to block Labor’s bill cutting the private health insurance rebate for people aged 65 and over, who now get a larger rebate than younger people. In a rare step the bill was discharged from the Senate, so it must be introduced again. The change was due to start in April 2027 and save the federal budget $3bn. Health department modelling released under freedom of information laws says almost 80,000 older Australians would give up private cover by 2028-29.

Health minister Mark Butler said the question should not be held hostage to political stunts and reintroduced the same bill in the lower chamber on 15 October, where it passed its first reading. Greens leader David Shoebridge named his price: carving out 1.2 million pensioners, after which we will pass it in a heartbeat. Butler told the ABC on 15 October we don’t have a majority in the Senate and did not rule out exempting people on the full pension. Jane Hume, deputy leader of the Liberal party, said the Coalition don’t like any cut and is not interested in partial exemptions.

Parliament Sitting - Both Houses

Parliament of Australia, 2026 sitting calendar, 23 to 26 November

The calendar is tight. The Senate sits alone from 16 to 20 November and both houses sit from 23 to 26 November, and no later sittings are listed for 2026. Shadow health minister Anne Ruston said the policy would hit up to 1.5 million pensioners, and state health ministers from Victoria, New South Wales, Queensland and Tasmania said the cut would push older people onto public hospitals.

Sources: The Guardian (2), Parliament of Australia, Parliament of Australia

The Pattern

We looked for cases where the Australian Senate had rejected a government bill that cut a payment or benefit to save money, and the same government brought the bill back in the same parliament. We found six such bills since 1996. The Senate passed one of them before the end of that year, in 2014, after the government agreed a third text with the senators holding the balance of power. Across all 26 bills brought back after a Senate rejection, whatever their subject, nine passed, four of them within 42 days.

The Read

The clock favours the government only if the Greens move. Forty-two days separate the reintroduction from 26 November, the last sitting day of 2026. Of the nine bills that passed after being brought back, four passed within 42 days, and two more, the housing fund in 2023 and the Help to Buy scheme in 2024, passed in the year’s last sitting week on the 43rd and 49th day, both after concessions to the Greens. Shoebridge has named a price, and Butler has not refused it.

There are reasons for doubt. Five of the six savings bills most like this one did not pass that year: three were voted down again after about 100 days, one was never put to a vote, and the one that passed in 2014 needed a third text and 71 days. Those six were rejected at a vote on the bill, while this one was discharged by a procedural vote, a difference in form. The cut is not due to start until April 2027, so the government loses nothing by waiting for the 2027 sittings, and the Coalition has said partial exemptions will not win its votes.
