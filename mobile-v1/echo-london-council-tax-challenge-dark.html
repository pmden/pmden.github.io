Wandsworth threatens 160%. The permission sits in Whitehall.

Four London boroughs have opened a formal challenge to the government's funding reallocation. Wandsworth is preparing a 160% council tax rise for April 2027.

4 min read

A 40% grant cut, a 160% bill, and a statutory appeal

Wandsworth, Westminster, Kensington and Chelsea and Richmond launch a formal challenge to the government's local government funding reform under the Sustainable Communities Act.

(PoliticsHome, 26 Aug 2026)

Four London boroughs, three Conservative-run and one Liberal Democrat, have begun a formal appeal against the government's redistribution of local government grant towards more deprived areas, using the route provided by the Sustainable Communities Act (PoliticsHome, 26 Aug 2026). Wandsworth puts its own loss at around 40 per cent of grant funding and will consult residents before submitting. Westminster has warned of increases up to 200 per cent, and Wandsworth had earlier floated 230 per cent without service cuts.

Wandsworth's working number is a 160 per cent rise from April 2027, then a freeze for the rest of the council term, against a base that is currently one of the lowest in England (PoliticsHome, 17 Aug 2026). The council's spending review concludes in September 2026. Roughly half of a Wandsworth bill is the Greater London Authority precept, which the borough does not set.

The constraint is statutory rather than political. Since the Localism Act 2011, an English billing authority proposing an increase above the annual referendum principle must either hold a local referendum or obtain a bespoke principle from the Secretary of State. The government's policy statement for 2026-27 to 2028-29 keeps the core principle at 3 per cent with a further 2 per cent for adult social care, says it will consider bespoke flexibility for authorities in significant financial difficulty, and states it will not agree such requests where council taxpayers already pay more than average (MHCLG, 2026). Wandsworth's low base places it on the right side of that test.

The Pattern

Inclusion rule, fixed in advance: this pattern counts every request by an English authority for a bespoke council tax referendum principle whose outcome is on the public record for the 2025-26 settlement, as confirmed in the final settlement statement of 3 February 2025. For each, the test is whether the request was granted at the level the authority asked for. Referendum outcomes are excluded, because they are decided by voters rather than by a minister. Requests made and withdrawn without a recorded ministerial decision are excluded, because there is nothing to score. Seven requests are on that record and they are set out below, grouped by the size of the flexibility granted.

2025
Bradford was allowed an additional 5% above the standard principle, the largest bespoke figure granted in the round, and less than the council had sought.
2025
Windsor and Maidenhead and Newham were each allowed an additional 4%, both authorities in receipt of exceptional financial support.
2025
Birmingham, Somerset and Trafford were each allowed an additional 2.5%, in every case below the figure requested.
2025
Hampshire's request, which the county costed at a 15% increase, was refused outright.
6 of 7
6 of 7 requests granted, none in full. The limitation to state plainly is that all seven decisions were taken by the same department in the same settlement round, so they are correlated and this is closer to one decision-maker's revealed policy than to seven independent trials.

6 of the 7 requests decided in the 2025-26 settlement were granted, and not one was granted at the level the council asked for.

What this pattern excludes

Bedfordshire Police and Crime Commissioner referendum, 7 May 2015
The only council tax referendum held since the Localism Act 2011 requirement began, and it was decided by voters rather than by a minister, so it does not belong in a set about ministerial decisions. Voters rejected the 15.8% rise by 69.5% to 30.5%.
Bespoke principles granted to Thurrock, Slough and Woking in the 2024-25 settlement
All three were granted a 10% principle, but the published record does not state what each authority originally requested, so the grant-versus-request test cannot be applied.
Requests never publicly confirmed
Councils negotiate exceptional financial support privately and some approaches are never disclosed, so any request without a recorded outcome is left out rather than inferred.

The Read

The weighted case is that Wandsworth gets permission and does not get 160 per cent. The department has published the test it will apply, and a borough with one of the lowest bills in England passes the part about taxpayers already paying more than average, which is why the market on a bespoke principle sits above evens. The same record says the figure will be cut: every grant in the 2025-26 round came in below what the council asked for, and the largest was 5 per cent. A three-figure increase has no precedent in the period, and the department has a standing reason to avoid setting one.

The counter-case rests on the funding change being larger than anything the bespoke mechanism was designed for. A 40 per cent grant reduction is a different order of problem from the distressed-authority cases in the base, and the boroughs are pursuing the Sustainable Communities Act route precisely to reopen the formula rather than negotiate an exemption within it. If the government widens transitional protection in the December settlement, the pressure for an extreme council tax figure disappears and the September number becomes a bargaining position rather than a plan. That is the outcome that would make this reading wrong.
