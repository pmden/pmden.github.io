London wrote a heat plan. The heat isn't waiting.

With forecasts touching 40C and only the second Red Heat-Health Alert since the system began, City Hall launched Heat Ready London — the capital's first ever heat plan. July will test it almost immediately.

3 min readEcho #116LONDON
1 · Today

The Mayor launched Heat Ready London, the capital's first heat plan, as forecasters predicted temperatures up to 40C and the second Red Heat-Health Alert on record took effect.

London City Hall, 25 Jun 2026
2 · The event

What happened

City Hall launched Heat Ready London on 25 June, the capital's first dedicated heat plan, as forecasters predicted temperatures reaching 40C and record-breaking June heat triggered only the second Red Heat-Health Alert issued since the current warning system began. The plan spans cooling spaces and public drinking water, retrofitting high-risk homes against overheating, urban greening, safer access to swimming and waterways, and resilience work across the NHS and transport network, with London Councils, TfL and the London Fire Brigade named as delivery partners (London City Hall, 25 June 2026).

The launch capped a punishing week. On 22 June, Sadiq Khan triggered a high air pollution alert on advice from Imperial College London forecasters, warning that strong sunshine would cook nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds into ground-level ozone; Londoners were urged not to idle engines or burn garden waste (London City Hall, 22 June 2026).

"Rising temperatures are no longer a future threat — they are becoming a growing reality for Londoners," Khan said, framing the plan as a call to action for partners to protect the most vulnerable. The framework now faces an immediate test: whether July and early August bring the 35C-plus extremes the document was written for (London City Hall, June 2026).

3 · The pattern

The pattern

London's extreme heat has a signature: it arrives in short, sharp spikes, usually in late July or August, and a hot June is no guarantee the peak has passed. The record heat events of the past two decades nearly all landed after the June solstice heat had faded.

2022
19 July 2022 — Heathrow hit 40.2C, London's first 40C reading, during the UK's first Red Heat-Health Alert. June that year had already been hot; the record still arrived in July.
2020
31 July 2020 — Heathrow reached 37.8C in a short, sharp spike at the very end of July, after a largely unremarkable early summer.
2019
25 July 2019 — the UK set a then-record 38.7C at Cambridge, with London's Heathrow passing 36C the same afternoon.
2003
August 2003 — the first UK reading above 38C and more than 2,000 excess deaths in England: the episode that created modern heat-health planning.
3 of 10
London passed 35C in three of the ten summers before this one — 2019, 2020 and 2022. A near-40C June shifts the picture, but the honest base rate is that most London summers never touch 35C, and June heat has been a weak predictor of the July peak.

London's record heat spikes land in late July and August — after Junes that ranged from hot to ordinary.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case for more extreme heat is that 2026 already looks like a spike year. A June that forces the second Red Alert on record and near-40C forecasts is not a normal June — it signals the large-scale weather patterns that produce London's big peaks, and the climate trend keeps loading that side of the scale. Every 35C-plus summer in the past decade came in a year that was already running hot, and the historical timing points forward: the 2022, 2020 and 2019 records all landed between 19 July and 1 August, squarely inside this market's window.

The honest counter-case is the base rate. Seven of the past ten summers never reached 35C in London, and heat spikes are short — a two-day ridge that peaks at 34.5C loses the binary while still feeling brutal on the ground. British summers also break: 2019's record week gave way to a wet August, and a hot June collapsing into an unsettled July is a well-worn pattern. At 61%, the market is paying real money for the possibility that June was the summer's peak, not its preview.
