Scotland29 Aug 2026

Scotland publishes a referendum Bill it cannot introduce. Three of four refused.

The Scottish Government published a draft Independence Referendum Bill on 28 August. It can only be introduced once the UK Government transfers the power.

4 min read

What happened

The Scottish Government publishes a draft Independence Referendum Bill with the question "Should Scotland be an independent country? Yes or No", which cannot be introduced until referendum powers are devolved.

(Scottish Government, 28 Aug 2026)

The First Minister published the draft Bill on 28 August, calling it simple, straightforward and direct: it proposes the question of whether Scotland should be an independent country, answered Yes or No, defines who can vote and will carry a date on introduction (Scottish Government, 28 Aug 2026). The Bill will be introduced once the necessary powers are secured, and publishing it was a commitment for the government's first hundred days. Swinney said the largest number of pro-independence MSPs in history had voted in May to support the call for referendum powers to be devolved, and that he would have more to say on Scotland's constitutional future in the coming weeks.

The reason it is a draft rather than a Bill is a judgment. In 2022 the UK Supreme Court ruled that any further vote would require the agreement of the UK Government (BBC, 28 Aug 2026). That agreement takes the form of an Order in Council under section 30 of the Scotland Act 1998, the instrument Downing Street described as passing power from Westminster to Holyrood when David Cameron and Alex Salmond signed the Edinburgh Agreement (UK Government, 15 Oct 2012).

The answer arrived the same day. A UK government spokesperson said the Prime Minister had made clear to the First Minister at their first meeting that another referendum was off the table, because it would take focus away from growing the economy and helping families with the cost of living (BBC, 28 Aug 2026). Downing Street had said the same after Andy Burnham met Swinney before the Commonwealth Games opening ceremony in July. A spokesperson is not an instrument, and what the record shows is how each previous request was answered, and how fast.

The Pattern

Action type: the Scottish Government formally seeks a transfer of the power to hold an independence referendum, by letter, agreement or published plan put to the UK Government. Four episodes since devolution: 2012, 2017, 2019 and 2022. The test is whether the UK Government agreed to transfer the power within 18 months. Orders under section 30 on other subjects, of which there have been fifteen, are the instrument rather than the request and are not counted.

2022
On 28 June 2022 Nicola Sturgeon proposed 19 October 2023 as the referendum date and wrote to Boris Johnson saying she was ready to negotiate the formal consent for the vote, while the Lord Advocate referred the question of Holyrood's power to the Supreme Court the same day. Johnson's letter of 6 July 2022 said he could not agree that now was the time to return to the question. No transfer followed, and the Supreme Court ruled in November 2022 that a vote needs UK agreement.
2019
On 19 December 2019, after the SNP won 48 of the 59 Scottish seats at the general election, Nicola Sturgeon formally requested a section 30 order for a referendum in 2020. Boris Johnson's written reply of 14 January 2020 refused, citing the once in a generation promise and the Edinburgh Agreement's commitment to respect the 2014 result.
2017
On 31 March 2017, after the Scottish Parliament had voted for a referendum, Nicola Sturgeon wrote to Theresa May asking for a section 30 order so that Scotland could choose between following the UK out of the EU and independence. May's verdict was that now was not the time, and on 27 June 2017, after the general election, Sturgeon put the plans on the back burner. No order was made.
2012
On 10 January 2012 Scottish Secretary Michael Moore offered a section 30 transfer in the Commons while the Scottish Cabinet signed off its referendum consultation and Alex Salmond named autumn 2014; on 25 January Salmond set out his question. David Cameron and Salmond signed the Edinburgh Agreement on 15 October 2012, which Downing Street described as facilitated by a section 30 order passing power to Holyrood.
1 of 4
One of the four requests since 2012 was granted. In 2012 the two governments signed an agreement nine months after the Scottish consultation and the order followed in February 2013. In 2017, 2019 and 2022 the UK Government refused: by a stated position in 2017, by letter after 26 days in January 2020 and by letter after eight days in July 2022. Four cases: History is not shown below six, and all three refusals came under one party at Westminster; the 2026 request is the first put to the current Prime Minister.

One of the four requests since 2012 produced a transfer of power, and it came with an agreement signed by both governments nine months after the Scottish plan was published. The other three were refused: two by letter within four weeks, one by a stated position that stood until the plans were shelved.

What this pattern excludes

The fifteen section 30 orders on other subjects since 1999
They are the instrument, not the request. The previous version of this Echo counted them; they measure what the mechanism is used for, which is a neighbouring question to what follows a Scottish demand for it.
The February 2010 draft Referendum Bill
Published for consultation under a minority government. The 2012 case counts the formal exchange with the UK Government that followed; the 2010 draft had no such exchange in the record used here.
Party roadmaps and Holyrood votes without a request to the UK Government
A mandate claimed is not a request made. The count starts when the Scottish Government puts the ask to the UK Government, which is why the May 2026 vote is context for this Echo rather than a case.

The Read

The near markets are about tempo. Whitehall answered the 2019 and 2022 requests with letters inside four weeks, and both became public because the First Minister published them; the difference this time is that the refusal was given to journalists on the day of publication, which removes the need for a letter. That is why the gov.uk market sits low. The Holyrood market sits high because Swinney has said more is coming and controls the chamber's diary.

The November market is where the record is plainest. One of four requests was granted, and the one that was came after both governments wanted the vote and signed for it before any order was laid. Nothing in the 28 August exchange resembles that: the Prime Minister's position was stated in July and repeated in August. The counter-case is that the 2026 request rests on a May vote by the largest pro-independence group of MSPs yet; the 2017 request rested on a Holyrood majority too, and was refused. What would move the November market is a UK statement that engages with terms rather than with the principle.
