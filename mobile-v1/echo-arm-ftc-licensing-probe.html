Arm built its own chip. Washington opened a file.

The US Federal Trade Commission is investigating whether Arm's licensing of its semiconductor designs amounts to attempted monopolisation.

4 min read

The licensor becomes a competitor

Arm faces a US antitrust probe over the licensing of its chip technology, with the FTC examining whether the company is trying to monopolise parts of the semiconductor market.

(Bloomberg, 15 May 2026)

Bloomberg reported on 15 May that the Federal Trade Commission is investigating Arm's licensing practices, examining whether the UK-headquartered company would refuse licences, or lower their quality, for the CPU blueprints its customers depend on while accelerating its own chip-building ambitions (Bloomberg, 15 May 2026).

The probe lands on an obvious tension. In March, chief executive Rene Haas unveiled the AGI CPU, Arm's first flagship data-centre processor, at the company's 'Arm Everywhere' event, telling investors the chip line could generate six times the roughly $4 billion Arm earned in 2025 by 2031. Shares jumped 16 per cent on the day, and analysts called it the most significant shift in the company's history (Reuters, 25 Mar 2026).

That shift is exactly what worries licensees. Arm's business was built on selling neutral designs to everyone, including Apple, Qualcomm, Nvidia and Amazon; a vertically integrated Arm competes with its own customers. The company already fought one licensing war, with Qualcomm, before a Delaware jury largely sided with Qualcomm in December 2024. The FTC inquiry is at an early stage and may not produce a formal case.

The Pattern

Antitrust actions against semiconductor firms are slow weapons. The record since 2009 shows years between probe and complaint, and complaints that are as likely to die on appeal as to stick.

2024
The US Department of Justice began examining Nvidia's AI-chip dominance, with subpoena reports surfacing in September 2024; more than a year on, no formal complaint has been filed.
2021
The FTC sued to block Nvidia's $40bn acquisition of Arm in December 2021, roughly 15 months after the deal was announced; the companies abandoned the transaction in February 2022.
2017
The FTC sued Qualcomm over its licensing model in January 2017, won at district court in 2019, then lost when the Ninth Circuit reversed in August 2020. Qualcomm's model survived.
2009
The European Commission fined Intel €1.06bn in May 2009 for exclusionary rebates; the EU General Court annulled the core of the fine in 2022, and a reduced €376m penalty was imposed in 2023, fourteen years after the original decision.
1 of 4
1 of 4 major chip antitrust actions since 2009 ended in a durable win for the regulator, and the probe-to-complaint stage alone has never taken less than a year. The near-term threat to Arm is headline risk, not legal risk.

Of four major antitrust actions against chip firms since 2009, one produced a lasting regulator win, and none moved from probe to formal complaint in under a year.

The Read

The weighted case is that the probe changes little before autumn. FTC investigations of chip firms have historically taken one to three years to mature into complaints, when they mature at all, and the agency's Nvidia file has sat without a case for over a year. Meanwhile Arm's operating momentum is the live variable: the company guided to roughly $1.18bn for the January-to-March quarter and has beaten its own midpoint in most quarters since listing, so the earnings market carries the real near-term signal.

The honest counter is that Arm is a cleaner target than Qualcomm ever was. Its designs sit under an estimated 99 per cent of smartphones, the Delaware jury loss to Qualcomm left discovery material about licensing conduct on the record, and the AGI CPU gives the FTC a simple story: a monopolist supplier moving to compete with captive customers. Probes with a clean narrative and pre-assembled evidence can move faster than the base rate, and even a formal complaint filed within months would reprice Arm's licensing moat overnight.
