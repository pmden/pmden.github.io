The hold was 7–2. July decides who was right.

The Bank of England kept Bank Rate at 3.75% in June, but two members voted to raise it. The next decision, on 30 July, comes with a full forecast round and a live question: do the hawks win the argument?

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The MPC voted 7–2 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75%, with two members preferring an immediate rise to 4% as energy prices stay elevated and unstable.

Bank of England, 18 Jun 2026
2 · The event

A hold with an argument inside it

At its meeting ending on 17 June, the Monetary Policy Committee voted by a majority of 7–2 to maintain Bank Rate at 3.75%, with two members preferring an immediate increase to 4% (Bank of England, 18 June 2026). The dissent is the story: it is the clearest signal yet that part of the committee believes the energy-price shock that pushed CPI to 3.3% in March has not fully worked through, even as headline inflation has eased back to 2.8%.

The committee's caution cuts both ways. Global energy prices have declined since the May meeting following developments in the Middle East, but remain elevated and unstable compared with pre-conflict levels, and the Bank expects inflation could rise later this year as earlier energy increases continue to feed through. Against that, the labour market is cooling and June's composite PMI fell to a 14-month low of 49.4, signalling contraction — an economy that looks poorly placed to absorb tighter policy.

The next decision lands on 30 July, alongside the quarterly Monetary Policy Report and fresh forecasts (Bank of England, MPC dates). Forecast rounds are when committees move: the August-cycle meeting gives the hawks their best platform to convert two votes into five, and gives the majority its best cover to hold again with updated numbers.

3 · The pattern

When two dissenters vote for a hike, does the committee follow?

A 7–2 hold with hawkish dissent has a specific history on Threadneedle Street. Sometimes the dissenters are the committee's leading edge, vindicated within a meeting or two. Sometimes they are a faction that never converts, and the next move goes the other way entirely. The record since 2011 is genuinely split.

2021
November 2021: Saunders and Ramsden voted to raise Bank Rate in a 7–2 hold. One meeting later, in December, the committee hiked to 0.25% — the dissenters front-ran the entire tightening cycle.
2018
June 2018: three members — McCafferty, Saunders and Haldane — voted for an immediate rise in a 6–3 hold. In August the MPC raised Bank Rate to 0.75%, unanimously. Dissent converted within two meetings.
2014
August–December 2014: Weale and McCafferty voted for a hike at five consecutive meetings. No hike ever came. The next change in Bank Rate was a cut, in August 2016.
2011
Early 2011: up to three members — Sentance, Weale and Dale — voted for increases as CPI headed above 5%. The committee never followed; the next moves were more QE, and the next rate change was the 2016 cut.
2 of 4
In 2 of 4 modern episodes of sustained hawkish dissent on the MPC, the committee followed with a hike within two meetings. In the other 2, the dissenters lost and the next move was a cut. The pattern is a genuine coin-flip — dissent tells you the argument exists, not who wins it.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case is a hold. Seven votes is a comfortable majority, headline CPI at 2.8% sits close to target, the labour market is cooling, and June's PMI signalled outright contraction. A committee publishing fresh forecasts into a weakening economy and an unresolved Westminster leadership contest has every institutional reason to wait. The 2014 and 2011 precedents show hawkish factions can dissent for months without ever converting.

The counter-case is that forecast rounds are exactly when this committee moves, and the two dissenters in 2021 and 2018 were vindicated within two meetings. If the July Monetary Policy Report shows the energy pass-through lifting the inflation forecast above 3% into the autumn, the majority's own numbers start arguing for the hawks. A 27% price on a hike is not noise — it is the market taking the Bank's 'could rise later this year' language at face value.
