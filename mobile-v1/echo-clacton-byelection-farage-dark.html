Farage put his own seat on the ballot. That bet has failed twice in 19 tries.

Nigel Farage resigned as MP for Clacton on 7 July to force a by-election he intends to win back. On 13 August he faces a record 34 candidates, and none of them comes from a major party.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

Ten days before polling, a crowded field of independent candidates and the novelty campaigner Count Binface are lining up against Nigel Farage in Clacton, with every major party refusing to stand.

2 · The event

What happened

Nigel Farage resigned as MP for Clacton on 7 July and announced in the same breath that he would fight the resulting by-election (CNBC, 7 July 2026). The resignation came amid a parliamentary standards inquiry into his finances, including a reported £5 million gift from the crypto investor Christopher Harborne and undeclared support from George Cottrell, a financier convicted of fraud in the US (Al Jazeera, 8 July 2026). The inquiry pauses for the campaign and resumes if he returns.

The major parties stood aside. Labour, the Conservatives, the Liberal Democrats and the Greens are not fielding candidates, leaving Count Binface as the leading challenger in a field of 34, the largest in UK parliamentary history, past the record of 26 set at Haltemprice and Howden in 2008 (Bloomberg, 17 July 2026). The ballot paper runs to 90 centimetres.

Polling day is 13 August, confirmed after the writ was moved in the week of his resignation (ITV News Anglia, 10 July 2026). The only constituency poll so far, from Survation on 2 August, puts Farage on 73% and Binface on 20%, on a sample of just 265 voters (PollCheck, 2 August 2026).

Farage frames the contest as the people against the establishment. The pattern question is simpler. When an MP resigns to re-fight their own seat, do the voters send them back? The record since 1982 is long enough to answer.

3 · The pattern

The Echo

Since 1982, nineteen MPs have resigned their seats and stood again in the by-election they triggered. The record leans hard one way, and both exceptions share a feature Clacton lacks.

1982
Bruce Douglas-Mann, Labour MP for Mitcham and Morden, defected to the SDP and resigned to seek a fresh mandate. He lost, taking 29.4% against 43.4% for the Conservative Angela Rumbold. He remains the clearest warning case.
1986
Fifteen unionist MPs resigned together in protest at the Anglo-Irish Agreement and re-fought their seats on 23 January. Fourteen won, often by wide margins. The one loss came in Newry and Armagh, where the SDLP's Seamus Mallon beat Jim Nicholson 45.5% to 40.3%.
2008
David Davis resigned Haltemprice and Howden over 42-day detention. Labour and the Liberal Democrats stood aside, 26 candidates filled the ballot, and Davis took 71.6% on a 34.5% turnout. The closest structural match to Clacton.
2014
Douglas Carswell defected from the Conservatives to UKIP and resigned Clacton itself. He won the by-election with 59.7% and a majority of 12,404 (BBC, 10 October 2014).
2014
Mark Reckless made the same move in Rochester and Strood a month later and won with 42.1%, despite a full Conservative machine campaigning against him.
17 of 19
Both losses came when a major party machine organised against the returning incumbent: Rumbold's Conservatives in 1982, Mallon's SDLP in 1986. No such machine is standing in Clacton. The nearest precedent is Davis in 2008, who took 71.6% when the big parties stayed home.

17 of 19 resigning MPs since 1982 won their seat back; both losses came when a major party organised against them.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case is strong. The base rate is 17 of 19, both misses required an organised rival machine, and Survation has Farage 53 points clear. In 2024 he won this seat with 46.2% against a full field; this time the field is 33 candidates without a major party among them. The 90% call prices a near-certainty while leaving a margin for the genuinely unprecedented.

The honest counter-case rests on thin data. The single constituency poll sampled 265 people, and Ipsos found 33% of Britons nationally wanted Binface to win against 21% for Farage, so the protest vote is real and low-turnout by-elections amplify it. Only an external shock flips the top market: a National Crime Agency development before polling day, or Farage becoming unable to serve. Nominations closed on 17 July, so the ballot itself cannot change.
