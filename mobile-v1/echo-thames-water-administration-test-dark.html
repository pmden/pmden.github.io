Utilities31 Aug 2026

The creditors have named a board. Ofwat has not said yes.

London & Valley Water, the consortium of Thames Water's senior lenders, named the first four non-executive directors it would install if its takeover proceeds. Ofwat has yet to approve the plan, and the government told the regulator in June that it was not convinced.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The consortium named Mike McTighe, Liz Barber, Clive Selley and Dame Bernadette Kelly as the first proposed non-executive directors of Thames Water Utilities, subject to its acquisition proceeding and to Ofwat's onboarding process for directors.

London & Valley Water, 24 Aug 2026
2 · The event

A board in waiting, a regulator in consultation

London & Valley Water is a group of around 100 financial institutions holding more than £17 billion of Thames Water's Class A and super senior debt. On 24 August it named the first cohort of proposed non-executive directors — Mike McTighe, Liz Barber, Clive Selley and Dame Bernadette Kelly — who would oversee a ten-year turnaround if the consortium's acquisition and recapitalisation proceeds and if Ofwat completes its onboarding process for directors (London &amp; Valley Water, 24 Aug 2026).

The obstacle is not the board. In June the Environment Secretary wrote to Ofwat saying she was not yet convinced the proposal protected consumers' interests, objecting to what she called an unprecedented level of regulatory adjustments, to bill increases in this price review period and the next, and to investments deferred in some cases by up to a decade (Defra, 16 Jun 2026). Any restructuring the regulator does support still requires public consultation and a High Court sanction before it can take effect.

The alternative on the table is the water industry special administration regime, under which Ofwat or the Secretary of State petitions the court to have the company run by a court-appointed administrator while services continue. Thames Water has already tested the boundary once: its interim £3 billion restructuring plan was sanctioned by the High Court in February 2025 and upheld on appeal that April, in a case argued expressly on whether special administration was the better outcome (UK Supreme Court, Maynard v Thames Water Utilities Holdings).

3 · The pattern

What happens when a water company runs out of money

This is a decision. Ofwat and the Secretary of State hold the petition; creditors, the court and the government hold the alternative. Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: every water and sewerage company appointed under the Water Industry Act in England and Wales, from privatisation in 1989 to today, whose owner became unable to carry its debt and where the outcome was a forced change of control or a court-sanctioned restructuring. The test is whether the appointed company entered the statutory special administration regime or was resolved another way.

2001
Hyder, owner of Welsh Water, could not carry its borrowing after the utilities windfall tax and the 1999 price review. Western Power Distribution bought Hyder and sold Welsh Water, with £1.85bn of debt attached, to the not-for-profit Glas Cymru for £1, refinanced by a £1.9bn bond. No special administration.
2002
Enron's collapse left Azurix unable to hold Wessex Water, and Ofwat's ring-fence held. YTL Power International bought the company for £1.24bn, completing on 21 May 2002 (Guardian, Aug 2002). No special administration.
2021
Southern Water breached its licence and faced a funding gap it could not close from existing shareholders. Macquarie Asset Management injected more than £1bn for a majority stake, and Ofwat welcomed the investment rather than petition the court. No special administration.
2025
Thames Water's own interim restructuring plan, worth £3bn, was sanctioned by the High Court in February and upheld by the Court of Appeal in April against creditor and public-interest challenges that argued special administration was preferable. No special administration.
4 of 4
Four of four. The record is unanimous but thin, and the reason it is unanimous matters more than the count: in each case a buyer or a creditor group was willing to take the company on terms the regulator could live with, so the state never had to choose. Thames Water is the first case in which the government has put its objections in writing before the regulator decided. If Ofwat concludes the consortium's terms are worse for customers than the alternative, the base rate offers no protection, because no previous case reached that point.

In 4 of 4 cases since 1989, a water company that could not carry its debt was recapitalised, sold or restructured rather than placed in special administration.

What this pattern excludes

Bulb Energy, November 2021
The Energy Supply Company Administration was used, and used successfully, but it sits under different legislation with a different regulator and no long-run capital programme obligations attached to the licence. The mechanism does not transfer.
Railtrack, October 2001
Railway administration was used on a Secretary of State petition, but the rail regulator had no equivalent power to approve a creditor recapitalisation instead. The decision set that Thames Water faces did not exist in that case.
Kemble Water Finance default, April 2024
The default sat at the holding company above the regulated business. The special administration regime attaches to the appointed company, so a holding company failure is not an observation of the same decision.
Scottish Water and Northern Ireland Water
Both are publicly owned, so the failure mechanism being tested here does not exist for them and there is nothing for the regulator to decide.

5 · The read

The read

The weighted case is the consortium. Naming four experienced infrastructure directors five weeks before the Budget is a solvency signal aimed at the regulator and at ministers, not at customers, and it follows the pattern of every previous case: the state takes the outcome that arrives with its own capital attached. The consortium holds more than £17 billion of senior debt, which means it controls the only realistic alternative to a state-run process, and Ofwat has consistently preferred a funded private solution when one has been on the table.

The counter-case is that this time the government has spoken first. The Environment Secretary's June letter objected to bill increases, weakened performance standards and investment deferred by up to a decade, and those are precisely the concessions the consortium needs to make its numbers work. A regulator that grants them owns the political consequence; one that refuses them may find the consortium walks. That is the route by which the first special administration in the sector's history becomes possible, and it is why the market prices a quarter chance on it over the next ten months rather than the near-zero the raw base rate implies.
