Defence28 Aug 2026

Bulwark sails for Brazil. The replacement has no in-service date.

The Ministry of Defence confirmed the sale of HMS Bulwark to the Brazilian Navy after more than two decades of service. Her sister ship HMS Albion is at Devonport awaiting disposal, and the ships that replace them have not been built.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The Ministry of Defence confirmed HMS Bulwark will be sold to the Brazilian Navy, ending more than 20 years of Royal Navy service, with sister ship HMS Albion at Devonport awaiting disposal.

GOV.UK, 28 Aug 2026
2 · The event

What happened

HMS Bulwark, an Albion-class landing platform dock commissioned in 2005, is to be sold to the Brazilian Navy. She was Royal Navy fleet flagship from 2011 to 2015, evacuated civilians from Lebanon in 2006 and provided security for the 2012 Olympic sailing events. Her sister ship HMS Albion sits at Devonport awaiting disposal (GOV.UK, 28 Aug 2026).

The replacement is the Multi Role Support Ship programme. In July 2026 the UK and the Netherlands signed a £2.4bn maritime partnership covering eight vessels, four for each navy, each 160 metres long and displacing 15,000 tonnes, with flight decks designed for drones and autonomous systems. The UK has confirmed £1.5bn through its Defence Investment Plan and the ships are to be built in British yards with Dutch industry (GOV.UK, 28 Aug 2026).

Lord Coaker, Minister of State for Defence, framed the partnership as a vote of confidence in British shipbuilding. The announcement names a budget, a partner, a tonnage and a hull count. It does not name a shipyard, a contract signature or a date on which the first ship enters service.

3 · The pattern

The pattern

The Royal Navy has done this three times in living memory: retire or sell a capability on the promise of a successor, then live without it. Each time the promise was real and each time the wait ran into years.

2010
The Strategic Defence and Security Review retired HMS Ark Royal and the Joint Force Harrier. The last Harrier launched from her deck in November 2010 (GOV.UK, Nov 2010) and she paid off the following month (GOV.UK, Dec 2010). Carrier strike did not return until the F-35B and HMS Queen Elizabeth, around a decade later.
2010
The same review cancelled the Nimrod MRA4 after the aircraft ran roughly eight years late and about £800m over budget. The UK had no maritime patrol aircraft of its own until the P-8 Poseidon entered service at the end of the decade.
2018
HMS Ocean, the Navy's only helicopter carrier, decommissioned in March and was sold to the Brazilian Navy for around £84m, the proceeds reinvested in defence (GOV.UK, 2018). No dedicated replacement followed; the role passed to the new carriers.
2026
HMS Bulwark follows HMS Ocean to Brazil while HMS Albion waits at Devonport. The Multi Role Support Ship partnership is funded and signed with the Netherlands, and carries no published in-service date (GOV.UK, 28 Aug 2026).
3 of 3
In each of the three comparable cases since 2010 the capability went first and the replacement came late or not at all: carrier strike returned after roughly a decade, maritime patrol after most of one, and the helicopter carrier was never directly replaced. The Multi Role Support Ship programme is better funded and further advanced at this stage than any of them, with a partner navy and a confirmed budget. What it lacks is the thing the three precedents all lacked as well: a date.

3 of 3 previous British decisions to retire a capability before its replacement arrived produced a gap measured in years, not months.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case is that the ships are real and the dates are not, yet. The MRSS programme has what the 2010 cancellations never had: a signed international partnership, a confirmed budget line inside the Defence Investment Plan and an industrial argument about British yards that a government elected on regional investment wants to make loudly. That argument works best with a named yard attached, which is why the shipyard market prices well above the in-service date market. Governments announce workshares long before they announce delivery.

The honest counter is that the pattern is unbeaten and the mechanism behind it has not changed. Capability goes when the running costs bite; replacement arrives when the build slot, the budget profile and the yard capacity align, and those three rarely align on the timetable of the announcement. Albion has been alongside at Devonport rather than disposed of, which suggests the Navy is keeping options open rather than closing them. The prediction fails if the Defence Investment Plan publishes a delivery schedule this autumn, which would break the pattern at its weakest point.
