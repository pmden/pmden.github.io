Inflation17 Jun 2026

Inflation stalled at 2.8%. The Bank says the climb comes next.

UK CPI held at 2.8% in May, flattered by falling food prices and a lower energy cap while transport inflation hit its highest rate since 2022. The June print, due 22 July, tests whether the energy shock is really over.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

CPI rose 2.8% in the 12 months to May 2026, unchanged from April, as surging transport costs offset falling food prices.

ONS, 17 Jun 2026
2 · The event

A flat headline hiding a fight underneath

The Consumer Prices Index rose by 2.8% in the 12 months to May 2026, unchanged from April, the Office for National Statistics reported on 17 June (ONS, 17 June 2026). The flat headline conceals two forces cancelling each other out: transport prices rose 6.8% on the year — the highest annual rate since December 2022 — while food and non-alcoholic beverages delivered the largest downward pull. The broader CPIH measure held at 3.0%.

The recent path explains the tension. CPI hit 3.3% in March, driven by the energy-price shock that followed the Iran–US conflict, then fell to 2.8% in April as a lower Ofgem price cap and the temporary removal of renewable-energy levies cut household bills (ONS, 20 May 2026). May's hold means the retreat has paused rather than continued — and services prices are still running hot (ONS, 17 June 2026).

The Bank of England expects CPI could rise later this year as earlier energy increases continue to feed through the pipeline. That makes the June figure, due from the ONS at 7am on 22 July (ONS release calendar), the first hard test of whether 2.8% was a floor or a pause — and it lands eight days before the MPC's 30 July rate decision.

3 · The pattern

After an energy spike recedes, does inflation come back?

The UK has been through four major energy-driven inflation spikes in the modern record. In each, the question after the first retreat was the same one facing the ONS release on 22 July: does the fall hold, or does a second wave build? The answer has usually been that the retreat holds — with one very large exception.

2022
CPI peaked at 11.1% in October 2022 after the gas shock, then fell in a broadly straight line to 3.9% by November 2023. Core prices stayed sticky, but no second headline wave came.
2011
CPI peaked at 5.2% in September 2011 on energy and VAT effects, then fell to 2.2% by September 2012. The retreat held throughout.
2008
The oil spike took CPI to 5.2% in September 2008; within a year it had collapsed to 1.1% as the financial crisis crushed demand.
1975
The exception. After the 1974 oil shock, inflation retreated briefly — then wage indexation drove a second wave that peaked near 27% in August 1975, the worst in modern UK history.
1 of 4
Only 1 of 4 modern UK energy-shock retreats was followed by a second inflation wave. The base rate favours the fall holding — but the single exception, 1975, is the episode central bankers are paid to remember, which is why the Bank keeps warning the climb may come.

In 3 of 4 UK energy-shock episodes the first retreat held; the one second wave, in 1975, was the century's worst.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case is a modest rise. The Bank of England itself expects earlier energy increases to keep feeding through, transport inflation is accelerating rather than fading, and services prices surged in the May data. The market's 41% on a 2.9–3.1% print reflects the mechanical arithmetic: April's energy-cap relief drops out of comparisons over the coming months, and there is little in the pipeline pushing the other way beyond food.

The honest counter is that the base rate leans the other way. Three of four modern energy-shock retreats simply held, the economy is contracting on the June PMI reading, and food prices are still falling. A cooling labour market is the classic disinflation engine, and 1975-style second waves needed wage indexation Britain no longer has. If June prints at 2.8% or below, the hawks' case at the MPC weakens eight days before the rate decision.
