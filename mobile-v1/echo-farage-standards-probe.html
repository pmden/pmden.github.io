Reform UK02 Jul 2026

Farage claimed the Bank blinked. Now the watchdog reads his diary.

A Labour MP has reported Nigel Farage to the Parliamentary Commissioner for Standards over his private lobbying of the Bank of England against a digital pound.

4 min read

Two threads, one watchdog

Labour MP Phil Brickell asked Standards Commissioner Daniel Greenberg to examine Farage's September 2025 meeting with Andrew Bailey, alleging undisclosed lobbying against a UK central bank digital currency.

(The Guardian, 2 Jul 2026)

On 2 July, Labour MP Phil Brickell formally reported Nigel Farage to Parliamentary Commissioner for Standards Daniel Greenberg, asking him to review a private September 2025 meeting between Farage and Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey. The complaint alleges Farage urged the Bank to scrap its plans for a central bank digital currency; Farage later claimed credit when the Bank softened its approach and dropped a proposed £20,000 cap on individual stablecoin holdings that he had publicly attacked (The Guardian, 2 Jul 2026).

Greenberg is not starting from zero. Since 13 May he has had an open Rule 5 failure-to-register inquiry into whether Farage should have declared an alleged £5 million personal gift from crypto investor Christopher Harborne, received shortly before Farage was sworn into Parliament. Electoral Commission records separately show £25,000 payments from Harborne to Farage in February 2026. Farage insists he broke no parliamentary rules because the lump sum was not used for political purposes (BBC News, May 2026). No wrongdoing has been established.

The scrutiny lands on a party that still leads the country. Ipsos polling in June put Reform UK on 26% of the vote, ahead of every rival — even as the same survey found 63% of Britons dissatisfied with Farage personally, up from 49% a year earlier (Ipsos, 3 Jul 2026). In the same month, Farage declared £270,000 in earnings for advertising gold bullion — four hours' work a month over three months.

The Pattern

Recent high-profile standards and privileges cases against prominent MPs point one way on outcomes: findings usually land, and most subjects leave the Commons within a year. They say much less about how fast a complaint becomes a formal inquiry.

2023
Boris Johnson: the Privileges Committee found he misled Parliament and recommended a 90-day suspension; he resigned his seat before the vote.
2023
Scott Benton: caught in a lobbying sting, suspended for 35 days; facing a recall petition, he resigned.
2021
Owen Paterson: paid advocacy for two companies paying him over £100,000 a year; a 30-day suspension was recommended, a government attempt to save him collapsed within 24 hours, and he resigned.
2019
Keith Vaz: suspended for 6 months, the longest in modern Commons history; he stood down at that year's election.
2018
Ian Paisley: suspended 30 sitting days over undeclared holidays paid for by Sri Lanka; the recall petition failed at 9.4% and he kept his seat — the one survivor in the set.
4 of 5
Four of five recent high-profile standards cases ended with the MP out of the Commons within a year of an adverse finding. But every one of those took months to get from complaint to verdict — the near-term question is only whether a second formal inquiry opens at all.

In 4 of 5 recent high-profile standards cases, the MP left the Commons within a year of the finding.

The Read

The case for a second inquiry: the Commissioner already has Farage's file open, the complaint comes with a paper trail — a documented private meeting, public claims of credit, a policy that then moved — and Greenberg has shown he will list inquiries against the most prominent name in British politics. The 13 May Rule 5 inquiry proves the watchdog is not deterred by Reform's poll lead. A complaint this specific is easier to open than to dismiss.

The counter-case: lobbying complaints are structurally harder than registration ones. Rule 5 is a paperwork test; the lobbying rules require showing Farage sought a benefit connected to a registered interest, and a meeting with a central bank governor about public policy sits in contested territory. Commissioners routinely decline complaints that read as political warfare between parties — and this one comes from a Labour MP against Labour's chief electoral threat. A quiet 'no inquiry' or simple silence past 7 August is the cheaper institutional path, and silence resolves NO.
