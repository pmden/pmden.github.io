Scotland03 Sep 2026

Scotland publishes a Bill it cannot introduce. Eight orders in twenty-seven years say why.

The Scottish Government published a draft Independence Referendum Bill on 28 August. The Supreme Court has already ruled that Holyrood cannot legislate for such a referendum without an Order in Council modifying the reserved matters, and the record of those orders is short and specific.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The Scottish Government publishes a draft Independence Referendum Bill with the question "Should Scotland be an independent country? Yes or No", which cannot be introduced until referendum powers are devolved.

Scottish Government, 28 Aug 2026
2 · The event

A draft Bill, a question, and a missing power

The First Minister published the draft Bill on 28 August, describing it as simple, straightforward and direct, proposing a clear and unambiguous question, setting out who can vote and, once agreed, carrying the date on introduction (Scottish Government, 28 Aug 2026). The question is whether Scotland should be an independent country, answered Yes or No. John Swinney said more would follow about Scotland's constitutional future in the coming weeks.

The reason it is a draft rather than a Bill is a judgment. In November 2022 the UK Supreme Court held unanimously that, in the absence of any modification of the definition of reserved matters by an Order in Council or otherwise, the Scottish Parliament does not have the power to legislate for a referendum on Scottish independence, and recorded that the UK Government was then unwilling to agree to such an order (National Archives, 23 Nov 2022).

So the operative question is not what the Bill says. It is whether a UK minister will lay an order under section 30 of the Scotland Act 1998 modifying Schedule 5. A UK government spokesperson answered on the day of publication that the Prime Minister had made clear to the First Minister in their first meeting that another referendum was off the table (BBC, 28 Aug 2026). A spokesperson is not an instrument, and an instrument is what the Bill needs. That makes this one of the cleanest things in British constitutional politics to observe and count.

3 · The pattern

Every order that has modified Scotland's reserved matters

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: this pattern lists every statutory instrument made under section 30 of the Scotland Act 1998 that modified Schedule 5, from the opening of the Scottish Parliament to today, as published on the statute book. The test is what each order actually devolved. No order is excluded for being small and none for being large. Orders that modified only Schedule 4, and orders transferring executive functions rather than legislative competence, are outside the rule because they do not change what Holyrood may legislate about.

2001
Made 11 April 2001. An exception to the insolvency reservation covering moratoriums and property management for social landlords. The first use of the power, and a narrow one.
2013
Made 12 February 2013. The independence referendum order, inserting paragraph 5A with three conditions: no clash with another referendum, a poll no later than 31 December 2014, and one ballot paper with exactly two responses. A second order made the same day devolved occasional financial assistance in social security.
2015
Four orders across 2014 and 2015: food definitions and veterinary medicines, votes at 16 for Scottish Parliament and local elections, the construction of vehicles carrying pupils to school, and the day of the poll at the 2016 election.
2026
Made 10 March 2026, in force the next day. Assisted dying: the identification and regulation of substances and devices, limited to legislation resulting from Bills passed before 7 May 2026. The most recent use of the power, and time-limited like the referendum order.
1 of 8
1 of 8. Read the denominator carefully: it counts orders made, not requests answered, so it says how the power has been used rather than how often it is asked for. On the requests side the record is shorter and one-directional. Nicola Sturgeon wrote to Theresa May on 31 March 2017 and to Boris Johnson on 19 December 2019, and both were refused; the third route, in 2022, went to the Supreme Court instead and lost. The useful signal is the shape of the one constitutional order. It was agreed between two governments, it carried a deadline, and once that deadline passed the power went with it.

8 orders have modified Schedule 5 since 1999. 1 of them concerned the constitution, and it expired at the end of 2014. The other seven were about insolvency, social security, food, votes at 16, polling day, school buses and assisted dying.

What this pattern excludes

Section 63 orders transferring executive functions to Scottish Ministers
They move who exercises a power, not what the Parliament may legislate about. Counting them would inflate the record with instruments that could never have authorised a referendum.
The Scotland Acts of 2012 and 2016
They devolved substantial powers, including tax and welfare, but by primary legislation at Westminster rather than by order. Different mechanism, different politics, and no minister can do it alone.
Requests for an order that were refused
They belong in the story but not in this count, which is a count of instruments actually made. A separate base rate of requests and refusals would need a published record of every request, which does not exist.

5 · The read

The read

The two near-term markets are about tempo rather than outcome. Whitehall departments answer constitutional demands with silence far more often than with a document, and a draft Bill published in recess is easier to ignore than a Bill introduced in the chamber, which is why the gov.uk market sits low. The Swinney market sits high for the opposite reason: he said more would come in the coming weeks, the Parliament has just returned, and a First Minister who has published a draft Bill has a reason to keep the sequence moving.

The long market is the interesting one, and the record cuts against the intuition that section 30 is a dead letter. The power was used as recently as March 2026, for assisted dying, so the mechanism works and ministers reach for it when both governments want the same outcome. What the record also shows is what it gets used for: narrow, technical and agreed. The one time it carried a constitutional question, both governments had already signed the Edinburgh Agreement on 15 October 2012 before the order was laid, and the order expired with the poll. A new order is more likely to be about substances, vehicles or benefits than about the Union, and a market on any order being made is not the same as a market on the referendum.
