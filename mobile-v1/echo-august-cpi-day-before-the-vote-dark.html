Inflation20 Aug 2026

Inflation turned back up in July. The August print decides whether the turn holds.

CPI rose to 2.9 per cent in the year to July, the first increase since March, and the August figure is published on 16 September, the day before the Bank votes.

4 min read

Three tenths up on the headline, nothing on the core

CPI rose by 2.9% in the 12 months to July 2026, up from 2.6% in June, the first rise in the annual rate since March; CPIH 3.1%, core CPI unchanged at 2.6%.

(ONS, 19 Aug 2026)

The Office for National Statistics reported that CPI rose by 2.9 per cent in the 12 months to July 2026, up from 2.6 per cent in June, the first time since March that the annual rate had increased. CPIH rose to 3.1 per cent from 2.8 per cent. Core CPI, which excludes energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, was unchanged at 2.6 per cent (ONS, 19 Aug 2026). The next release is 16 September 2026.

The gap between the two numbers is the whole argument. A headline rate that moves three tenths while the core does not move at all points to a price change concentrated in the categories the core leaves out. The ONS attributes the largest upward contributions to housing and household services and to furniture, with transport pulling the other way; the goods rate rose from 1.7 to 2.2 per cent while the services rate eased from 3.6 to 3.4 (ONS, 19 Aug 2026). Core has now printed 2.6 in May, June and July after 3.1 in March (ONS, series DKO8).

The calendar makes the August figure unusually loaded. The Monetary Policy Committee held Bank Rate at 3.75 per cent on 30 July by six votes to three, with the three dissenters voting for a rise to 4 per cent, and said CPI was expected to rise later in the year as higher energy prices pass through (Bank of England, 30 Jul 2026). Its next announcement is 17 September, the day after the CPI release.

The Pattern

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: this pattern takes the ONS CPI annual rate series (D7G7) from January 1989 and counts every month in which the annual rate rose after at least three consecutive months in which it did not rise, the shape the July 2026 print has after falls in April and June and no change in May. The test is single: was the annual rate in the following month higher than in the turn-up month. Thirty-eight cases meet the rule; the last eight are shown and the full record is counted.

2025
The annual rate rose from 3.2 to 3.4 per cent in December 2025 after no rise in September, October or November; January 2026 printed 3.0, a fall of 0.4 (ONS, series D7G7).
2024
The annual rate rose from 2.0 to 2.2 per cent in July 2024 after falls in April and May and no change in June; August 2024 printed 2.2, unchanged (ONS, series D7G7).
2023
The annual rate rose from 3.9 to 4.0 per cent in December 2023 after no change in September and falls in October and November; January 2024 printed 4.0, unchanged (ONS, series D7G7).
2023
The annual rate rose from 10.1 to 10.4 per cent in February 2023 after falls in November, December and January; March 2023 printed 10.1, a fall of 0.3 (ONS, series D7G7).

The annual rate rose from 0.5 to 0.6 per cent in June 2020 after falls in March, April and May; July 2020 printed 1.0, a rise of 0.4 (ONS, series D7G7).
2020
The annual rate rose from 1.3 to 1.8 per cent in January 2020 after a fall in October, no change in November and a fall in December; February 2020 printed 1.7, a fall of 0.1 (ONS, series D7G7).

The annual rate rose from 1.8 to 1.9 per cent in February 2019 after falls in November, December and January; March 2019 printed 1.9, unchanged (ONS, series D7G7).

The annual rate rose from 2.4 to 2.5 per cent in July 2018 after a fall in April and no change in May or June; August 2018 printed 2.7, a rise of 0.2 (ONS, series D7G7).
2 of 8
Two of the last eight turn-ups carried into the following month: July 2018 (2.5 to 2.7) and June 2020 (0.6 to 1.0). Three held (February 2019, December 2023, July 2024) and three fell back (January 2020, February 2023, December 2025). In the full ONS series from January 1989 the annual rate has turned up after three or more months without a rise 38 times; the following month was higher in 16, unchanged in 7 and lower in 15.

Two of the last eight turn-ups were followed by a higher rate the next month; three held and three fell back. Across the full record since 1989 the following month was higher in 16 of 38 cases, unchanged in 7 and lower in 15.

What this pattern excludes

The size-of-move test used in the earlier version of this Echo
It measured what an August does to the annual rate in any year, a calendar question. The action in the news is a turn-up after a run of falls, so the cases are turn-ups, whatever the month.
CPIH
It is the ONS lead measure and moved further in July, from 2.8 to 3.1 per cent, but it is the measure the Bank does not target. Counting both would double the sample without doubling the evidence.
The three-month run length
It is taken from the news, no rise in April, May or June, and stated before the count. A shorter or longer run changes the case list, and the reader should know the rule was not chosen to produce the answer.

The Read

The market opens below evens because the record is against a second rise. Two of the last eight turn-ups carried into the next month and the full record is 16 of 38, a little over four in ten. July's move came from the categories the core leaves out, and the core has printed 2.6 three months running, so a higher August needs those same categories to move again rather than the underlying rate to shift.

What the pattern cannot see is the energy path the Bank itself expects. The Committee said on 30 July that CPI was expected to rise later in the year as higher energy prices pass through, and three of its nine members already wanted a higher rate. If August prints 3.0 or above the day before the vote, those three have their case handed to them; if it holds at 2.9 or falls back, the majority's reading, underlying disinflation with an energy bump on top, stands. The turn-up is less the story than whether it repeats.
