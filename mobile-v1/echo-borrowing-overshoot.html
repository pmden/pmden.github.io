Borrowing overshoots again. The Budget arithmetic hardens.

May's public borrowing came in £5.6 billion above the OBR's forecast, with debt interest at a record for the month. Two months into the fiscal year, the overshoot is already £7.7 billion.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

May borrowing hits £23.3bn, £5.6bn above the OBR forecast; debt interest is the highest for any May on record.

ONS, 19 Jun 2026
2 · The event

£7.7 billion off course after two months

Public sector borrowing reached £23.3 billion in May 2026, £5.4 billion more than in May 2025 and £5.6 billion above the £17.7 billion the Office for Budget Responsibility had pencilled in (ONS, 19 Jun 2026). Central government debt interest hit £11.7 billion, up 54.4% on a year earlier and the highest for any May on record. Public sector net debt stood at 95.1% of GDP, territory last seen in the early 1960s.

The cumulative picture is the harder one for the Treasury. Borrowing in the financial year to May was £46.3 billion, £7.7 billion above the OBR's profile of £38.6 billion and 23.9% higher than the same period last year, driven by debt interest, higher spending on services and benefits, and receipts that failed to keep pace (ONS, 19 Jun 2026). Chancellor Rachel Reeves rebuilt her fiscal headroom with £26 billion of tax rises last November; the data shows that buffer eroding from the first quarter.

Two fixed points now shape the story. The OBR publishes its Fiscal Risks and Sustainability report on 7 July, examining long-run pressures from debt interest, the triple lock and demography (OBR, Jul 2026). Then on 21 July the ONS releases the June borrowing figures, the first hard signal of whether the spring overshoot is a blip or a trend heading into the autumn Budget.

3 · The pattern

What summer overshoots do to autumn Budgets

A chancellor staring at above-forecast borrowing in early summer has three options by autumn: raise taxes, cut spending, or let the fiscal rules absorb the strain. The recent record leans heavily one way, with a single notable exception a decade back.

2025
Borrowing ran above the OBR profile through spring and summer. The November Budget answered with £26 billion of tax rises, taking the tax take toward a record share of GDP.
2024
The new government declared a £22 billion in-year shortfall in July. The October Budget raised taxes by £40 billion, the largest single increase since 1993.
2022
Borrowing deteriorated sharply after the September mini-Budget crisis. The November Autumn Statement delivered a £55 billion consolidation of tax rises and spending restraint.
2012
Borrowing overshot plans mid-year, but the Autumn Statement avoided net tax rises: George Osborne extended austerity by a further year to 2018 and let the debt target slip instead.
3 of 4
3 of the last 4 summer borrowing overshoots were followed by tax-raising autumn statements. The exception, 2012, extended spending cuts rather than raise taxes — a route this government has largely ruled out.

3 of the last 4 summer borrowing overshoots ended in autumn tax rises; the exception chose longer austerity over higher taxes.

5 · The read

The read

The weighted case is continuation. Every month of this financial year has printed above the OBR's profile, and the drivers are structural rather than one-off: debt interest tracks index-linked gilts and elevated rates, and the spending side reflects settlements already signed, including the NHS pay deals. June 2025's borrowing was £20.7 billion, and this year has run consistently ahead of last. That is why the market puts 63% on another £20bn-plus month and concentrates the overshoot market on the £5bn–£10bn band. A hot 21 July print, landing two weeks after an OBR report on long-run debt risks, would fix the autumn Budget narrative before August.

The counter-case rests on timing and revision. Early-year borrowing data is the least reliable of the fiscal calendar: receipts arrive lumpily, and the ONS has a record of revising first prints down by billions as self-assessment and corporation tax data firm up. June also captures less debt-interest pain than May when the RPI uplift on index-linked gilts falls differently across months. On the Budget date, history points to patience: the last two Budget dates were announced in early September, not high summer, which is why the market sits at 22% despite pressure on the Treasury to steady expectations early.
