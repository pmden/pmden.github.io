Inflation03 Sep 2026

Inflation turned back up in July. The August print lands the day before the Bank votes.

CPI rose to 2.9 per cent in the year to July, up from 2.6 per cent, the first increase since March. The August figure is published on 16 September.

4 min read

Three tenths up on the headline, nothing on the core

CPI rose by 2.9% in the 12 months to July 2026, up from 2.6% in June, with CPIH at 3.1% and core CPI unchanged at 2.6%.

(ONS, 19 Aug 2026)

The Office for National Statistics reported that CPI rose by 2.9 per cent in the 12 months to July 2026, up from 2.6 per cent the previous month, and that CPIH rose to 3.1 per cent from 2.8 per cent, its first increase since March. Core CPI, which excludes energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, was unchanged at 2.6 per cent (ONS, 19 Aug 2026). The next release is 16 September 2026.

The gap between the two numbers is the whole argument. A headline rate that moves three tenths while the core does not move at all is the signature of a price change concentrated in the excluded categories, which in 2026 means energy above all. Core has now printed 2.6, 2.6 and 2.6 in May, June and July, after falling from 3.1 per cent in March. On its own that is a disinflation story with an energy bump on top.

The calendar makes the August figure unusually loaded. The Monetary Policy Committee held Bank Rate at 3.75 per cent on 30 July by six votes to three, with the three dissenters voting for an increase rather than a cut, and its next announcement is 17 September, the day after the CPI release. The labour market figures land on 15 September and monthly GDP on 11 September, so the Committee will have three fresh readings in the week it votes.

The Pattern

Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: this pattern takes the published ONS CPI annual rate series and looks at every July to August change in the fifteen years from 2011 to 2025. The test is the size of the move, not its direction: did the annual rate change by more than 0.1 percentage points. Fifteen consecutive years, no year dropped and no window chosen to produce a tidier number. The base is the series itself, not a set of selected episodes.

2017
The rate rose from 2.6 to 2.9 per cent, a three tenths move and one of only three August increases of 0.2 points or more in the fifteen years.
2020
The rate fell from 1.0 to 0.2 per cent, the largest August fall in the set, driven by a restaurant discount scheme and a temporary VAT cut. A policy intervention, not a price signal.
2021
The rate jumped from 2.0 to 3.2 per cent, the largest August move in the set and almost entirely the previous year's discount dropping out of the comparison.
2025
The rate held at 3.8 per cent, unchanged, as it also did in August 2024 and August 2016. Three of the last eleven Augusts have moved the annual rate by nothing at all.
9 of 15
9 of 15 Augusts moved the annual rate by a tenth or less. Two caveats. Two of the six large moves, in 2020 and 2021, were the same policy event seen twice, so the count of genuinely independent large moves is nearer four than six. And 2026 has an energy story running through it, which is exactly the kind of thing that puts a print in the tail rather than in the middle.

9 of the 15 July to August changes were 0.1 percentage points or smaller. Three were increases of 0.2 or more and three were falls of 0.2 or more, and the two largest were both artefacts of the 2020 policy interventions.

What this pattern excludes

Years before 2011
The fifteen-year window is stated in advance and is long enough to contain a full inflation cycle, a pandemic and an energy shock. Extending it further back adds observations from a different monetary regime without adding information about how an August behaves now.
CPIH
It is the ONS lead measure and it moved further than CPI in July, from 2.8 to 3.1 per cent, but it is the measure the Bank does not target. Counting both would double the sample without doubling the evidence.
Monthly index changes
The market and the pattern are both about the 12-month rate, which is what is quoted and what the Committee reacts to. Switching between the monthly and annual measures inside one Echo would make the number unreadable.

The Read

The middle band carries most of the weight because the record says an August rarely moves far and because the July move already happened. A rate that has just jumped three tenths on energy does not usually jump again the following month unless the energy price moves again, and the October price cap increase announced by Ofgem in late August affects bills from October, not from August. That puts the base case at a rate sitting where it is, give or take a tenth.

The core market is the one that decides how the Committee reads the week. Core has been flat at 2.6 for three months, and in nine of the last fifteen years the August core moved by two tenths or less in either direction, so an unchanged print is the single most likely outcome. But five of the last ten Augusts saw core rise, and if it does rise on 16 September the three members who voted for a higher rate in July have their argument handed to them the day before they vote again. That is the combination worth watching: a headline that stays put and a core that does not.
