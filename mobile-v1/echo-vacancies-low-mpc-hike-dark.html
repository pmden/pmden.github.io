Vacancies at a twelve-year low. Markets still price one more hike.

Vacancies have fallen to 707,000, the lowest outside the pandemic since 2014, and private-sector pay growth is the weakest since 2020. Markets still price a Bank Rate rise to 4% by year-end. The record says cooling jobs markets usually win that argument.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

UK pay growth slowed in the three months to June as vacancies fell to a post-pandemic low, with economists at ING and Jefferies arguing the cooling jobs market undercuts the case for further Bank of England rate rises despite war-driven energy inflation.

The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026
2 · The event

What happened

707,000 vacancies in May to July is the lowest estimate outside the pandemic since 2014, down 6,000 on the previous three months, while payrolled employment fell by 13,000 in July, matching June's decline, and unemployment held at 4.9% (ONS, 18 Aug 2026). The direction is uniform: fewer openings, shrinking payrolls, a jobless rate 0.2 points higher than a year ago.

2.8% is private-sector regular pay growth, the weakest since October 2020; the whole-economy figure of 3.5% is held up by a 6.1% public-sector number flattered by the timing of the NHS award, and total pay growth slowed to 4.1% from 4.4% (The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026). Real pay is growing at 1.3%, a margin that thins as energy-led inflation climbs.

3.75% is where Bank Rate sits after the July meeting's 6–3 hold, and market pricing still points to one rise to 4% by the end of 2026 as the Iran war keeps energy costs and headline inflation elevated (The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026). ING's James Smith sees a high bar for tightening this year barring a severe energy spike, and Jefferies' Modupe Adegbembo finds little evidence of labour-market-driven inflation (The Guardian, 18 Aug 2026). The committee announces its next decision at noon on 17 September (Bank of England, Jul 2026).

3 · The pattern

The pattern

Four times since 2008 the MPC has faced a falling vacancy count and cooling pay while inflation sat at or above target. Three times the next move was a cut or nothing. The exception came with pay growth double today's.

2008
Vacancies slid from around 700,000 through the year while CPI climbed towards 5.2%. The MPC held at 5% all summer; its next move was October's emergency half-point cut.
2011
CPI hit 5.2% in September with vacancies near 450,000. No hike came — the committee held at 0.5% and voted a further £75bn of QE in October.
2019
Vacancies fell through the year from roughly 850,000 to about 800,000. Bank Rate stayed at 0.75% for all twelve months; the next move was down.
2023
The counter-case: vacancies had fallen for more than a year, yet the MPC raised Bank Rate to 5.25% in August 2023, its fourteenth consecutive rise — with total pay growth running above 8%.
3 of 4
In three of the four episodes since 2008 in which vacancies were falling and pay cooling while inflation sat above target, the MPC's next move was a cut or nothing: 2008, 2011 and 2019. The exception was 2023, when total pay growth above 8% kept the hikes coming. Today's 4.1% is barely half that, which is why September leans hold despite inflation at 2.9%.

3 of 4 falling-vacancy episodes since 2008 ended with a cut or a hold as the next move; the one exception had pay growth double today's 4.1%.

5 · The read

How to read it

14% on a September hike reflects an alignment of base rate and current voices: three of four falling-vacancy episodes ended without further tightening, two bank economists read this data as closing the door, and July's 6–3 hold means three votes must switch in five weeks of thin data. The vacancies market is the cleaner short-term call — a 6,000-a-quarter decline from 707,000 makes sub-700,000 a near coin flip on 15 September, and it resolves two days before the rate decision it would influence.

34% on 4% by December is where the honest tension lives. Market pricing still expects one hike this year because the exception case is live: the Iran war is doing to energy costs what no vacancy count can offset, and 2023 proved the MPC will tighten into a cooling jobs market when inflation frightens it. The prediction fails if energy prices spike hard enough to drag pay settlements up, if the September pay data revises upwards, or if the public-sector 6.1% proves stickier than the NHS timing effect suggests — any of which puts the December hike back on the table.
