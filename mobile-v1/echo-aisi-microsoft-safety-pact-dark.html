AI Safety05 May 2026

Microsoft opens its models. The Institute starts the clock.

The UK's AI Security Institute has struck a partnership with Microsoft to evaluate high-risk AI capabilities and test the safeguards around them. Government access deals with frontier labs have a patchy record of turning into published work.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

AISI announced a partnership with Microsoft to develop methods for evaluating high-risk AI capabilities and the effectiveness of safeguards, plus research into how conversational AI behaves in sensitive contexts.

AI Security Institute, 5 May 2026
2 · The event

A new name, a bigger lab on the books

The AI Security Institute, the UK government's frontier-model evaluation body, announced on 5 May a partnership with Microsoft focused on developing and advancing methods for evaluating high-risk AI capabilities and the effectiveness of the safeguards used to address them. The agreement also covers societal-resilience research, including how conversational AI systems interact with users in sensitive contexts (AISI, 5 May 2026).

The deal extends a pattern of formalised lab access that began at Bletchley in 2023 and survived the institute's October 2025 rebrand from Safety to Security, when the government narrowed its remit to risks with security implications (GOV.UK, 14 Feb 2025). Microsoft is a distinctive partner: it is not primarily a frontier-model developer but the deployment layer for OpenAI's models across Azure, Copilot and enterprise software, which puts safeguard effectiveness, rather than raw capability, at the centre of the work.

The announcement landed in a tense fortnight for the institute's own research. AISI estimates that the time horizon of autonomous AI cyber capability is doubling every 4.7 months, a sharp acceleration from its November 2025 estimate of 8 months, with recent models outpacing even that trend. The question the partnership now poses is the one that has dogged every such agreement: does access produce published evaluations, and how fast.

3 · The pattern

What government–lab access deals have actually delivered

Since Bletchley, governments and frontier labs have announced a steady stream of testing agreements, memoranda and partnerships. The record of announced access converting into publicly documented pre-deployment evaluations is genuinely split.

2025
Anthropic signed a memorandum of understanding with the UK government in February 2025 on AI for public services and continued safety collaboration; the UK institute's model-evaluation pipeline continued, though the rebrand narrowed its published remit to security risks.
2024
The UK and US institutes jointly published pre-deployment evaluations of Anthropic's upgraded Claude 3.5 Sonnet in November 2024 and OpenAI's o1 in December 2024, the clearest cases of access agreements producing public technical output.
2024
The US AI Safety Institute signed formal pre-deployment access agreements with OpenAI and Anthropic in August 2024; in April 2024 Politico had reported that most labs which pledged pre-release access at Bletchley had not yet provided it.
2023
At the November 2023 Bletchley summit, leading developers agreed in principle to pre-deployment testing of frontier models by the UK institute, the founding pledge against which later delivery is measured.
2 of 6
About 2 of 6 announced government–lab access agreements since 2023 led to publicly documented pre-deployment evaluations. The pattern is split: joint UK–US evaluations delivered, while several Bletchley-era pledges produced no public output at all.

Of roughly six announced government–lab access agreements since Bletchley, about two produced publicly documented pre-deployment evaluations.

5 · The read

How to read it

The weighted case for near-term output is reasonable but not strong. AISI publishes research at a steady clip, and its cyber time-horizon work shows an institution under pressure to demonstrate relevance as capabilities accelerate; a five-week window catching a named-model publication is close to a coin flip on the institute's recent cadence. On the partnership question, the base rate argues for patience: agreements of this kind cluster around summits and fiscal events, and none is scheduled inside the window, which is why 'no new agreement' carries the largest share.

The honest counter runs in both directions. Against output: the 2025 rebrand narrowed what AISI publishes, and the most sensitive evaluations, precisely the ones a Microsoft partnership enables, are the least likely to appear publicly within weeks. For a surprise: the institute's own 4.7-month doubling estimate is an argument for urgency that ministers have already used, and a government wanting to look ahead of the curve before the summer recess has one obvious lever, announcing the next lab deal early.
