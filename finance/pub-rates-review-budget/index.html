Business rates25 Aug 2026

A pub rates review reports after the Budget. Budgets rarely leave it waiting.

The Treasury opened an independent review of how pubs and hotels are valued for business rates on 24 August, due to report by the end of March 2027.

4 min read

What happened

HM Treasury launched an independent review of business rates valuation methodology for pubs and hotels, led by Jerry Schurder, with a call for evidence closing at 11:59pm on 16 October 2026 and a report to the Treasury by the end of March 2027.

(HM Treasury, 24 Aug 2026)

The review announced on 24 August examines how the Valuation Office arrives at rateable values for public houses and hotels, two classes valued on trading potential rather than floor space. Both saw significant increases at the 2026 revaluation, largely because pandemic-era valuations fell away, and the Treasury's own release says the current system is not felt to reflect the market (HM Treasury, 24 Aug 2026). Jerry Schurder leads the work and reports by the end of March 2027, in time for the next revaluation rather than for this Budget.

The consultation window is the part that matters for the autumn. The call for evidence opened on 24 August and closes at 11:59pm on 16 October, gathering submissions by email from landlords, brewers, hoteliers and valuation professionals (HM Treasury call for evidence, 24 Aug 2026). Twelve days later the Chancellor, John Healey, delivers a Budget he has confirmed for Wednesday 28 October (HM Treasury, 31 Jul 2026). Relief this autumn therefore has to come through the multiplier or a discount, not through valuation.

The sector is not starting from zero. On 23 July the Prime Minister announced a 20 per cent cut to bills for pubs, social clubs and live music venues in England from April 2027, worth an estimated £1,100 to the typical pub and reaching nearly 32,000 premises, with the largest music venues excluded and details to follow at the Budget; it sits on top of a 15 per cent relief for 2026-27 announced in January and a two-year real-terms freeze (Prime Minister's Office, 23 Jul 2026). Since April 2026 retail, hospitality and leisure properties under £500,000 have paid multipliers 5p below the national rates, legislated at the November 2025 Budget (MHCLG, 26 Nov 2025).

The Pattern

This is a decision, not a threshold: a chancellor either announces a business rates measure for the sector or does not. Inclusion rule, fixed before the count: every autumn fiscal event delivered by a UK chancellor from 2015 to 2025, ten events, read from the business rates information letter that the responsible department sends English billing authorities after each one. There was no autumn event in 2019. The test is whether the event carried a new or extended rates measure aimed at retail, hospitality, leisure or pubs in England; a general multiplier freeze or small business relief alone does not count. The last eight events are shown and the full ten are counted.

2025
Retail, hospitality and leisure multipliers set 5p below the national rates from April 2026 for properties under £500,000, funded by a higher multiplier on the largest properties, alongside a £4.3 billion relief package. Measure delivered.
2024
The retail, hospitality and leisure relief extended for 2025-26 at 40 per cent, down from 75, with permanently lower multipliers for the sector announced for 2026-27. Measure delivered, at reduced generosity.
2023
The 75 per cent retail, hospitality and leisure relief extended into 2024-25 with the £110,000 cap retained, and the small business multiplier frozen at 49.9p. Measure delivered.
2022
Retail, hospitality and leisure relief raised from 50 to 75 per cent for 2023-24, up to £110,000 per business, with the multipliers frozen. Measure delivered.
2021
A new 50 per cent relief for retail, hospitality and leisure properties in 2022-23, capped at £110,000 per business, with the multipliers frozen. Measure delivered.
2020
The business rates multiplier frozen for 2021-22; no retail, hospitality or pub measure, the expanded retail discount for 2021-22 coming only at the March 2021 Budget. No sector measure.
2018
A one-third discount for retail properties with rateable values below £51,000 for two years, plus an extension of the local newspaper discount. Measure delivered.
2017
The £1,000 discount for pubs with rateable values below £100,000 extended by a further year, alongside the switch to CPI indexation of the multiplier. Measure delivered.
7 of 8
Seven of the last eight autumn events carried a measure for the sector: the pubs discount extension of 2017, the retail discount of 2018, the 50, 75 and extended 75 per cent reliefs of 2021 to 2023, the cut to 40 per cent with permanent lower multipliers in 2024, and the multipliers themselves in 2025. The 2020 Spending Review is the exception; it froze the multiplier and the sector's relief came at the March 2021 Budget instead. Over the ten events since 2015 it is seven of ten, the 2015 and 2016 Autumn Statements having dealt with small business and rural relief only. Two of the seven, 2024 and 2025, were reductions in generosity dressed as continuity.

Seven of the last eight autumn fiscal events carried a new or extended rates measure for retail, hospitality, leisure or pubs; the 2020 Spending Review froze the multiplier and left the sector to the March Budget. Over the ten events since 2015 it is seven, the 2015 and 2016 Autumn Statements having passed the sector over.

What this pattern excludes

The Growth Plan, 23 September 2022
It carried no business rates measure and was substantially reversed within eight weeks by the November Autumn Statement. Counting both would place two events in one autumn and score the same decision twice.
Spring Statements and spring Budgets
The multiplier and relief settlement for the following April is made at the autumn event; the March 2021 Budget's expanded retail discount is the exception that proves it, arriving because the 2020 Spending Review had not acted. Spring events are not counted.
Autumn 2019
Parliament was dissolved for the December election and no autumn fiscal event took place. There is no decision to score.
Business rates in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland
Rates are devolved. The Budget sets policy for England only and the markets resolve on English measures.
A pattern built on review launches
The fetched information letters record two earlier Treasury business rates reviews sitting beside a fiscal event, the 2015 review reported back at Budget 2016 (letter 7/2015) and the Transforming Business Rates paper published with the October 2024 Budget (letter 4/2024). Two verifiable UK launch cases is below the four the rule requires, so the pattern is drawn from the Budget decisions the review sits beside.

The Read

The weighted case is another measure. Business rates has become the standing hospitality item of the autumn event, seven of the last eight have carried one, and the Treasury has now put its own name to the argument that pub and hotel valuations are wrong while setting a review that cannot report until five months after the Budget. Chancellors have generally filled gaps of that kind with a discount rather than let them run, and the sums are small in Budget terms: the July package is worth about £1,100 to the typical pub.

The counter-case is that the sector has already been paid twice. The 5p multipliers arrived in April, the 20 per cent cut is booked for April 2027 with details promised at the Budget, and a Chancellor who has said the Budget will be built on fiscal discipline has an argument for calling that the settlement. The one autumn event in the window that passed the sector over, 2020, did so with a general freeze and a promise of more later; a repeat would leave the review as the answer to every question and the market resolving NO on a Budget that still mentions pubs on every page.
