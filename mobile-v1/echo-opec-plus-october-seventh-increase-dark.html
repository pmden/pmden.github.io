OPEC+04 Sep 2026

Six monthly increases, then Kuwait under fire. OPEC+ decides October on Sunday.

Seven OPEC+ producers meet on 6 September to set October output after raising quotas every month since April, the last step 188,000 barrels a day for September.

4 min read

What happened

Seven OPEC+ countries agreed a production adjustment of 188,000 barrels a day for September 2026, completing the return of the voluntary cuts announced in April 2023, and scheduled their next monthly meeting for 6 September.

(OPEC, 2 Aug 2026)

On 2 August the seven OPEC+ countries running the voluntary cuts agreed to add 188,000 barrels a day from September, the sixth consecutive monthly increase since April and the step that completes the return of the 2023 adjustments (OPEC, 2 Aug 2026). Saudi Arabia and Russia carry 62,000 barrels each of the increase; the group meets again on 6 September to decide October (Al Jazeera, 6 Jul 2026).

The meeting comes in a different market from the one that set the cycle in motion. Oil had fallen back to pre-war levels after the June memorandum with Iran, then surged again in July when the US struck Iran over attacks on Hormuz shipping (Al Jazeera, 8 Jul 2026). This week Iran fired missiles and drones at US bases in Kuwait, Bahrain, Jordan and Iraq, and Kuwait's military described ongoing Iranian aggression as its air defences engaged (Al Jazeera, 3 Sep 2026).

The group's own statements in July acknowledged the risk, saying attacks on infrastructure and shipping increase market volatility, and members had signalled a pause for the fourth quarter before the summer escalation (Al Jazeera, 6 Jul 2026). Sunday tests whether the pause holds now that the cuts are fully unwound and one member's territory is under fire.

The Pattern

Four times since 2019 an attack on Gulf energy or military targets has landed within weeks of an OPEC+ decision. The record shows what the next meeting did.

2026
July 2026: after Iranian attacks on Hormuz shipping and US strikes in response (Al Jazeera, 8 Jul 2026), the group's next meeting on 2 August added 188,000 barrels a day for September (OPEC, 2 Aug 2026).
2025
June 2025: two weeks after the Israel-Iran war and US strikes on Iranian nuclear sites, eight OPEC+ countries agreed on 5 July to raise output by 548,000 barrels a day for August (OPEC, 5 Jul 2025).
2020
January 2020: after the killing of Qassem Soleimani and Iran's missile attack on al-Asad, the group's March meeting failed to agree and the alliance briefly collapsed; by April it was debating a 10 million barrel cut for reasons unrelated to the attack (Al Jazeera, 3 Apr 2020).
2019
September 2019: drones and missiles hit Saudi Arabia's Abqaiq and Khurais plants. OPEC+ did not meet in response; at its scheduled December meeting it deepened cuts by 500,000 barrels a day to balance a weak market (Al Jazeera, 5 Dec 2019).
0 of 4
In 0 of 4 cases since 2019 did OPEC+ respond to an attack on Gulf energy or military targets by tightening at its next meeting. Twice, in 2025 and 2026, the next meeting raised output; in 2020 the alliance fell apart over demand; in 2019 the December cut came three months later and was about inventories, not Abqaiq. The group prices the market, not the war.

Four attacks, no emergency tightening; the next decision followed the market, not the missiles.

The Read

The weighted case is a pause, and it is not because of the missiles. The cuts are fully unwound, so the monthly mechanism that produced six increases has run out of road, and members were signalling a fourth-quarter pause in July before the latest escalation. The attack history says the group follows the market, and the market now has both higher prices and a supply-risk premium the producers do not want to dilute. That is why the pause is priced above the increase.

The honest counter-case is 2025 and 2026 themselves: the two most recent attacks were followed by increases, because the Saudi strategy of reclaiming market share has outlasted every geopolitical shock. The pause prediction fails if Riyadh decides that a $95 barrel with Hormuz in dispute is the moment to add supply rather than hold it back, and the Brent market fails if Sunday's decision, whatever it is, calms rather than tightens the front of the curve.
