Gilts05 May 2026

The 30-year touched 1998. The retreat is now on trial.

UK 30-year gilt yields hit 5.78% in early May, the highest since 1998, before easing to around 5.45% by mid-June.

5 min read

A 28-year high, then an uneasy calm

Gilt yields are climbing again as leadership uncertainty builds: the 30-year hit 5.54% and the 10-year 4.78% on 19 June, with markets pricing a change at Number 11 as much as Number 10.

(The Guardian, 19 Jun 2026)

On 5 May the yield on 30-year gilts surged as much as 13 basis points to 5.78%, its highest level since 1998, as expectations of Bank of England rate rises collided with worries over local election results and the impact of soaring energy prices (Bloomberg, 5 May 2026). The selloff reached down the curve, with 10-year yields topping 5.10%, and formed part of a broader global rout in long-dated government debt (Bloomberg, 5 May 2026).

Since then the market has partially healed. By 19 June the 30-year traded at 5.54% and the 10-year at 4.78%, nudged higher by speculation over the government's future (The Guardian, 19 June 2026). But the damage to the public finances is done: higher debt-servicing costs since the onset of the Iran conflict have eroded Chancellor Rachel Reeves's fiscal headroom of just under £24 billion.

The next month decides whether the calm holds. The Labour leadership contest resolves the 'change in Westminster' the market is pricing, the MPC's 30 July decision could validate or kill the rate-hike expectations that lit the May fire, and every long-gilt auction between now and August tests demand at yields no chancellor has funded at since the late 1990s.

The Pattern

The May episode is the fourth time in four years that long-dated gilt yields have spiked to a multi-decade high on a fiscal or inflation scare. Each time, the spike retraced within roughly two months. But the pattern has a second, less comfortable half: every peak has been higher than the one before, and the level yields retreat to keeps rising.

2026
May 2026: the 30-year hit 5.78% on 5 May, the highest since 1998, then eased about 33 basis points to 5.45% by mid-June.
2025
September 2025: the 30-year reached roughly 5.75%, then the highest since 1998, before easing into the autumn Budget — which triggered a relief rally across the curve.
2025
January 2025: a global bond selloff took the 30-year to about 5.47%, a then 27-year high; by spring, yields had retraced most of the move.
2022
September 2022: the mini-Budget crisis drove the 30-year above 5% intraday; Bank of England intervention reversed more than 100 basis points within weeks.
4 of 4
In all 4 long-gilt yield spikes since 2022, yields retreated within roughly two months of the peak. The unbroken part of the pattern favours the calm holding through July. The uncomfortable part: each successive peak (5.1%, 5.47%, 5.75%, 5.78%) has been higher, so 'retreat' keeps meaning a higher resting level.

All 4 long-gilt spikes since 2022 retraced within about two months — and every peak was higher than the last.

The Read

The weighted case is that the calm holds and the 30-year finishes July in the 5.25–5.49% band. Four spikes in four years have all retraced on schedule, energy prices have come off their conflict highs, the June PMI points to a weakening economy that argues against the rate hikes markets feared in May, and a resolved Labour leadership removes one source of uncertainty premium.

The counter-case is concentrated in two dates. If the 30 July MPC decision brings a hike — or a forecast round that validates the two June dissenters — the rate-expectations leg of the May selloff reignites. And if the new occupant of Number 11 signals looser fiscal rules with headroom already eroded below £24 billion, long-end investors will demand paying for it. The rising-floor pattern says even the retreats stop lower yields from coming back; 'below 5.25%' at 12% is priced as the outlier it is.
