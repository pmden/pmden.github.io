The cap rises 4% in October. January has never gone the other way.

Ofgem set the October price cap at £1,723 a year for a typical direct debit household, a 4% rise driven almost entirely by gas.

4 min read

What happened

Ofgem set the energy price cap at £1,723 a year for a typical dual-fuel direct debit household from 1 October to 31 December 2026, a 4% rise on the current £1,663.

(Ofgem, 26 Aug 2026)

Ofgem raised the price cap by 4% to £1,723 a year for a typical dual-fuel household paying by direct debit, up £60 from the current £1,663 and effective from 1 October. Around 22 million households on default tariffs are covered; roughly 11 million on fixed deals are not (Ofgem, 26 Aug 2026).

The rise is almost entirely gas. Gas bills go up 8% while electricity is broadly flat, so households without gas see an increase of less than 1%. Ofgem attributes the move to wholesale gas prices, which rose 11% over three months on continued pressure from the Middle East conflict (Ofgem, 26 Aug 2026).

The government's removal of VAT from electricity is doing visible work. Without it, Ofgem says, the cap would have been about £45 higher. Prices remain 52% below the 2022 crisis peak in cash terms, but adjusted for inflation this cap is 7% higher than October 2025. The next cap, covering January to March, is the one that lands with the heating on.

The Pattern

Ofgem has set the cap quarterly since the start of 2023. The January level, covering the coldest and highest-consumption quarter, has moved in only one direction against the October level before it, in falling markets and rising ones alike.

2024
The January cap was set at £1,928, a 5% rise on the previous quarter and £94 more over a year, announced on 23 November 2023 and driven by wholesale costs (Ofgem, Nov 2023).
2025
The January cap rose 1.2% to £1,738, following an October 2024 level of £1,717 (Ofgem, Nov 2024).
2026
The January cap rose 0.2% to £1,758 from £1,755, about 28 pence a month, announced on 21 November 2025 (Ofgem, Nov 2025; Ofgem, Jan 2026).
2026
The April cap then fell 7%, or £117, to £1,641 after the government took £150 of policy costs off bills (Ofgem, Feb 2026). The winter quarter is the exception in the series, not the rule.
3 of 3
Each January cap under the quarterly regime has landed above the preceding October: up 5% into 2024, 1.2% into 2025 and 0.2% into 2026. The mechanism is seasonal rather than political. Wholesale gas for delivery in the first quarter is bought into winter demand, and the observation window Ofgem uses for the January cap sits in the autumn. The counterweight this year is a government that has already removed VAT from electricity and could take further policy costs off bills before the Budget on 28 October.

3 of 3 January caps since the quarterly system began have come in above the October cap before them, including in years when spring brought sharp falls.

The Read

The weighted case is another rise. The October increase is 8% gas against flat electricity, and the gas leg is the one that dominates a January bill. Ofgem's stated driver, an 11% rise in wholesale gas over three months on Middle East supply pressure, has not resolved, and the observation window for the January cap runs through the autumn when European storage refills compete with heating demand. Three of three January caps have risen, and the two smallest of those rises came in quarters when wholesale prices were falling, not climbing.

The honest counter is fiscal. This government has already shown it will spend to hold the headline down: removing VAT from electricity cut about £45 from the October figure, and £150 of policy costs came off bills in April. A Budget on 28 October gives the Chancellor one more opportunity before the November announcement, and a winter cap that starts with a £1,6 is a better political number than one that starts with £1,8. The prediction fails if the Treasury moves further levies off bills, or if a Hormuz settlement pulls wholesale gas back through the autumn window.
