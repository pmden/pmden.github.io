Assisted dying returns. The Prime Minister stands aside.

Andy Burnham has told ministers to keep out of the assisted dying debate and confirmed he will not vote himself. The reintroduced bill faces its Commons second reading on Friday 11 September.

4 min readEcho #116UK
1 · Today

The Prime Minister writes to ministers on the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill, setting aside collective responsibility and asking ministers to stay out of the public debate.

GOV.UK, 28 Aug 2026
2 · The event

A free vote, a fixed Friday, and a government that will not take sides

Downing Street published the Prime Minister's letter to ministers on the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill on 28 August 2026 (GOV.UK, 28 Aug 2026). Following the long-standing convention on matters of conscience, collective responsibility is set aside and ministers vote as individuals; they are also asked to stay out of the public argument and not to comment on what the bill would mean for their own departments. Andy Burnham has said he will not cast a vote at all, on the grounds that a prime minister taking a side would weigh on the debate.

The bill itself is a reintroduction. Lauren Edwards, drawn second in the private members' bill ballot for the 2026-27 session, presented it on 17 June 2026, with the text published on 16 July; its second reading is set for Friday 11 September 2026 (House of Commons Library, 2026). The 2024-26 version cleared the Commons on 20 June 2025 by 314 votes to 291, was introduced in the Lords three days later, and fell when the session ended without completing its stages there.

The mechanics of a sitting Friday matter more than the arithmetic of the chamber. A private member's bill dies of the clock unless a closure motion carries with at least 100 members voting in favour, so opponents do not need a majority to stop it, only enough time and enough absences. That is the structural feature which separates this vote from an ordinary government second reading, and it is why attendance on 11 September is the variable to watch.

3 · The pattern

What the Commons does with conscience bills at second reading

Inclusion rule, fixed before the vote: this pattern counts every House of Commons second-reading division since 1964 on a backbench-sponsored bill that would remove or narrow a criminal prohibition on a matter of personal conscience, where the government declared itself neutral and both front benches withdrew the whip. Government bills carrying free votes are out, because the decision mechanism differs. Bills that create a new offence rather than remove one are out, because the direction of travel is reversed. Lords bills are out, because there is no Commons second-reading division to count.

1964
Sydney Silverman's Murder (Abolition of Death Penalty) Bill carried its second reading on 21 December by 355 to 170, on a free vote with the government standing back. It became law the following year.
1966
David Steel's Medical Termination of Pregnancy Bill carried its second reading on 22 July by 223 to 29. It reached the statute book as the Abortion Act 1967.
2015
Rob Marris's Assisted Dying (No. 2) Bill was defeated 118 to 330 on 11 September, the only clear rejection in this set and on the same subject as the current bill.
2024
Kim Leadbeater's Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill carried its second reading on 29 November by 330 to 275, then third reading by 314 to 291 in June 2025 before dying in the Lords.
3 of 4
3 of the 4 qualifying second readings since 1964 carried. Four cases is a thin base and the uncertainty around it is wide, which is why the fraction is published rather than a percentage. The defeat in the set is the one closest in subject matter to the bill now before the House.

3 of the 4 qualifying free-vote second readings since 1964 carried; the single defeat, in 2015, was on assisted dying itself.

What this pattern excludes

Sexual Offences (No. 2) Bill 1966 (Leo Abse)
Fits the rule on every condition, but the second-reading division figures could not be traced to a primary Hansard record within the checking window, so it is left out rather than estimated.
Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Bill 2013
Carried a free vote at second reading, but it was a government bill drafted and timetabled by ministers, so the decision mechanism is not the private members' Friday.
Wild Mammals (Hunting with Dogs) Bill 1997 (Michael Foster)
Backbench and free vote, but it created a criminal offence rather than removing one, reversing the direction the rule specifies.
Assisted Dying Bill 2014 (Lord Falconer)
A Lords bill that never reached a Commons second-reading division, so there is nothing in this set to count.

5 · The read

The read

The weighted case is that it carries. The same measure won a Commons majority twice inside eighteen months, at 330 to 275 and then 314 to 291, and the membership of the House has not changed since. Government neutrality removes the usual reason for a bill of this kind to fail, which is a payroll vote mobilised against it, and the sponsor drew a high ballot place, which secures the Friday slot rather than leaving the bill queued behind others. The pattern record leans the same way at 3 of 4.

The counter-case is procedural rather than ideological. Third reading was carried by 23 votes, a margin narrow enough that ordinary Friday absenteeism could reverse it, and the closure threshold of 100 gives opponents a way to win without assembling a majority. The 2015 defeat is the reminder that this subject, uniquely in the set, has already been rejected once by a large margin. A prime ministerial abstention and a gagging instruction to ministers also remove roughly two dozen senior members from the visible campaign on both sides, which cuts against the assumption that last year's vote simply repeats.
